Hatching Eggs Under a Broody Hen: A Beginner's Guide to Spotting Broodiness, Setting Eggs, the 21 Days & Breaking a Broody

Sooner or later one of your hens will flatten herself into a nest box, puff up and growl when you reach for the eggs. That's a broody hen, and she's either a free incubator or a layer that has stopped laying. Here's how extension poultry specialists at Kentucky, Georgia, Arizona and Mississippi State, plus the Merck Veterinary Manual and the FAO, say to hatch eggs under her or, if you don't want chicks, to break the broodiness.

Is she really broody?

A hen sitting in a nest box isn't automatically broody; she may just be laying. The University of Kentucky's broodiness fact sheet says a truly broody hen sits all day and makes only short trips to the feeder and waterer. Signs to look for:

  • She stays put. Arizona Extension says a broody spends nearly all her time in the nest box and leaves "only once or twice daily" to eat and drink.
  • She stops laying. The Merck Veterinary Manual defines a broody as a hen that stops laying so she can sit on eggs, "even when no eggs are present."
  • She defends the nest. Expect puffed-up feathers, growling or squawking and hard pecks when you reach under her (Kentucky, Arizona).
  • She plucks her breast. Kentucky notes she pulls out breast feathers to line the nest, leaving a bare brood patch that warms the eggs.
  • Big, smelly droppings. Because she holds them while sitting, the droppings she leaves on her trips off the nest are unusually large and foul (Kentucky).

Broodiness is hormonal. Kentucky explains that the hormone surge before each egg sometimes stays high, and that "the same hormones that prepare a mammal to lactate" cause broodiness in hens. You can't switch it on or off, and you don't need a rooster for a hen to go broody. It's most common in spring, though Arizona notes it can happen in summer or fall too.

Breed matters. Kentucky lists Buff Orpingtons, Cochins, Australorps, Sussex, Brahmas and Silkies among the breeds most likely to go broody, while commercial laying strains were selected to reduce it. Poultry author Gail Damerow, interviewed by Murray McMurray Hatchery, adds that production Leghorns rarely brood, and that you can't be sure a hen will make a good mother "until she does." A hen that has hatched a good clutch before is the safest bet. (Our breed guide covers which breeds are reliable setters.)

First decision: hatch chicks or break the broody?

  • No rooster, no chicks. University of Minnesota Extension's Wayne Martin warns that without a rooster the eggs "won't ever hatch," and a hen left on them "might stay on them until she starves." If you have no fertile eggs and don't plan to buy any, skip to breaking a broody below.
  • About half the chicks will be roosters. Damerow points out that hen-hatched chicks are straight run, so have a plan for the cockerels before you set eggs.
  • Mind the season. The UN Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) suggests timing a hatch so the chicks are about two months old before the weather turns hard.

Setting eggs under her

  1. Pick the right hen. FAO recommends a large, healthy hen with a good brooding and mothering record, and checking her for lice and mites before she settles in for three weeks.
  2. Gather good eggs. FAO's storage guidance for hatching eggs is no more than 7 days, at 12–14°C (about 54–57°F) and 75–85% humidity. If a rooster is new to the flock, FAO says eggs become fertile about four days after he's introduced. Our incubation guide covers picking and storing hatching eggs in more detail.
  3. Don't overload the nest. FAO puts the maximum at 14 to 16 eggs per nest but notes hatchability often drops above about ten, depending on the hen's size. Damerow's rule of thumb is that most hens can cover 12 to 18 eggs of the size they lay; a bantam covers only 8 to 10 large-hen eggs. Eggs that roll to the edge of the nest cool off, so fewer, well-covered eggs usually beat a big pile.
  4. Set them all at once. Eggs slipped in days later will hatch days later, after the hen is busy with her first chicks. If you must add or swap eggs, FAO says to do it at dusk. Many keepers also pencil-mark the set eggs so any new eggs laid in the nest by other hens can be spotted and removed.
  5. Put feed and water close by. FAO says food and water near the nest keep the hen in better condition and cut the time her eggs spend cooling while she's away. In very dry climates, FAO suggests slightly damp soil under the nesting material to hold humidity up.
Other eggs work too. Kentucky notes hens will brood turkey, duck and guinea fowl eggs. Just plan for a longer sit: Merck lists 21 days for chickens, 23–24 for bobwhite quail, 27–28 for guinea fowl, 28 for ducks and 28–33 for geese. Damerow adds that large eggs from heavy breeds can take a day longer than 21 days, and bantam eggs may hatch a day or two early.

The 21 days: caring for a sitting hen

A setting hen barely looks after herself. Damerow says she eats only about one-fifth of her usual amount and can lose as much as 20 percent of her body weight; Arizona Extension also warns of heavy weight loss and of heat stroke in summer. Keep fresh water and feed within a few steps of the nest every day, and don't worry when she hops off for a few minutes to eat, drink and leave one of those huge droppings. That brief break is normal.

Candling. FAO suggests checking fertility after one week with a bright light in a dark room: a dark shape means the egg is fertile, and a clear egg is infertile. Merck gives 7 to 10 days and describes a fertile egg as showing blood vessels around a dark embryo spot, while a dead embryo shows a red ring. Taking out clear and dead eggs leaves the hen fewer eggs to keep warm. Work quickly and put the good eggs straight back.

Have a backup. Damerow notes a first-time broody may quit partway through. If she abandons the nest and the eggs are still warm, moving them to a running incubator can save the hatch.

Hatch day and after

Kentucky explains that the peeping of hatching chicks is what tells the hen her job is changing from sitting to mothering. Let her manage it: Merck advises against moving chicks until they're dry and fluffed. If no eggs hatch, the hen never gets that signal, and Kentucky warns she may keep sitting rather than giving up at day 21. Give the eggs a day or two of grace past the due date, then remove the duds so she can move on.

FAO calls an 80 percent hatch of the eggs set normal for natural incubation, with 75–80 percent satisfactory, so don't be discouraged if a couple of eggs don't make it. One more thing worth knowing: Merck notes that Marek's disease vaccination is normally given at the hatchery or in the egg, so hen-hatched chicks won't have had it. Ask your vet or extension office whether vaccinating makes sense for your flock. The chicks will still need chick starter and chick-height waterers; our brooding guide covers feed and water for young chicks.

Breaking a broody hen you don't want to hatch

A broody that isn't hatching anything is a lost layer. Georgia Extension says each broody spell can cost as many as 8 to 10 eggs per hen, and left alone a broody can stay that way for 3 to 4 weeks (Merck) or even 3 to 6 weeks (Arizona). Start with the gentle steps from Kentucky:

  1. Collect eggs as often as you can, so there's nothing to sit on.
  2. Lift her off the nest at night and put her on the roost with the flock.
  3. Close the nest boxes in the evening once the other hens have laid.

If she's still determined, the extension fix is a wire-bottomed cage (often called "broody jail"), such as a dog crate, with food and water but no bedding or nest. The airflow under her cools her underside and the lack of a nest takes away the urge. How long it takes:

Arizona's test for when she's cured: let her out, and if she goes back to normal flock life instead of heading straight for the nest box, she's done. Some hens are "serial" brooders that go broody again a month later (Kentucky), and Damerow says owners who value their broodies discourage more than one brooding a year to protect the hen's health. Keeping at least one nest box per four hens (Arizona, Mississippi State) and collecting eggs often also helps prevent the egg eating that a crowded, broody-occupied nest can lead to.

The short version

A true broody sits all day, stops laying, plucks her breast and defends the nest. If you want chicks, give a proven, parasite-free hen about 10 to 12 fresh fertile eggs of her own size all at once, keep feed and water right by the nest, candle at 7 to 10 days, and leave her alone at hatch on day 21. If you don't want chicks, collect eggs often, move her to the roost at night, and use a wire-bottomed cage with food and water for a few days until she stops heading for the nest.
