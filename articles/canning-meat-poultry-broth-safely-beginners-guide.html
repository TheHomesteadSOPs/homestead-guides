Canning Meat, Poultry & Broth Safely at Home: A Beginner's Guide

A homestead that raises its own broilers, rabbits, or a hog for the freezer eventually runs into the same question: how do you put meat up in jars instead of relying on freezer space alone? The answer is narrower than canning vegetables or fruit — meat only has one safe method, no shortcuts — but it's very doable once you know the exact numbers.

If you haven't canned anything before, start with our general home canning basics guide for jar prep, altitude adjustments, and the acidity logic behind the two canning methods. This article picks up from there and goes deep on the one method meat actually uses.

Why meat only has one safe method

Meat, poultry, and broth are all low-acid foods, and Clostridium botulinum spores survive a 212°F boiling-water bath. There is no tested boiling-water (water-bath) recipe for any of them — a pressure canner reaching 240–250°F is the only method that reliably destroys those spores, and that's true regardless of how the meat was raised, how fresh it is, or how acidic the surrounding liquid tastes.

Use only a pressure canner, and only the full pressure and time a tested recipe specifies. Regulate heat to hold steady pressure at or slightly above the target for the entire processing time — a dip partway through isn't made up for by running longer afterward on most tested guidance, so if pressure drops below the target, extension sources direct you to restart the process from the beginning.

Hot pack vs. raw pack

Both packing methods exist in tested guidance, but hot pack is the one extension sources actually recommend for most home canners — it gives a better-filled jar and better quality in storage, because raw meat's own juices usually aren't enough to fully cover it once packed.

Meat typeHot packRaw pack
Chicken, rabbit, or similar poultry/gamePartially cook (boil, steam, or bake) until about two-thirds done, then pack with hot brothPack raw pieces loosely into the jar; add no liquid
Strips, cubes, or chunksPrecook until rare, then cover with boiling broth, drippings, water, or tomato juicePack raw; add no liquid
Ground or chopped meatCook until lightly browned, then cover with boiling broth or water (hot pack only — no raw-pack version exists)—

Ground meat specifically is worth a second thought: it can be canned safely, but extension sources note freezing gives noticeably better quality — canning is really there for when freezer space runs out, not as the first-choice method for ground meat.

Getting the meat ready before it ever sees a jar

  • Keep meat chilled at 40°F or below until you're ready to can it, and plan to can within a day or two of slaughter/processing — if that's not realistic, freeze at 0°F or below and thaw in the refrigerator before canning rather than letting it sit.
  • Chill dressed poultry 6–12 hours before canning.
  • Trim excess fat, gristle, and bruised spots. Fat that migrates up to the jar rim during processing can prevent a proper seal and can also turn strong-flavored in storage.
  • Soak strongly flavored meat — wild game, rabbit, strong-tasting poultry — for 1 hour in a brine of 1 tablespoon salt per quart of water, then rinse before packing. (Skip the salt in the jar afterward if you brined this way, so you're not salting twice.)
  • Large bones come out, but smaller bones (poultry, rabbit) can stay in — meat with bones actually needs a shorter processing time than boneless, since bone is more porous and heat moves through it faster.

Making and canning broth or stock

Homestead broth is usually the by-product of processing poultry or an animal for meat, which makes it a natural second product to can alongside the meat itself:

  1. Simmer bones and bony trim in water until the meat falls away easily — roughly 3–4 hours for beef bones, 30–45 minutes for a chicken or turkey carcass.
  2. Strain, cool quickly, and skim off the fat that rises to the top.
  3. Reheat the strained broth to a full boil, then fill jars, leaving 1 inch of headspace.
  4. Process in the pressure canner at the time/pressure for stock (below) — never water-bath, even though broth looks and pours like a clear liquid.
Don't thicken broth before canning it — any starch or flour changes how heat moves through the jar in ways the tested process time doesn't account for. Thicken it in the pot after opening, not before it goes in.

Processing times and pressure

Processing time depends on the food and jar size; pressure depends on your altitude and which type of gauge your canner uses. Always use the full time below regardless of jar size you choose — a smaller jar is safe, but it doesn't get a shorter processing time.

Before closing the canner, vent steam for 10 minutes — don't close the petcock or add the weight until then. Start timing once the gauge reaches the target pressure (dial) or once the weight begins to jiggle steadily (weighted). When processing ends, let the canner cool and depressurize completely on its own, then wait another 10 minutes before opening the lid — never force-cool it or vent it early.

A few things worth knowing before your first batch

  • Use cayenne, not sage, in home-canned sausage. Sage reliably turns bitter after pressure processing — a flavor problem, not a safety one, but worth avoiding on a first try.
  • Venison benefits from added fat. Extension guidance suggests grinding in about one part pork fat to every three or four parts venison, since deer meat is lean enough to can dry and bland otherwise.
  • Have your dial gauge checked yearly. Many Cooperative Extension offices will test a dial-gauge canner's accuracy for free or at low cost — a gauge reading even a couple of pounds off changes whether your jars actually reached a safe temperature.
  • Sealed jars store well, but not forever. Properly sealed, home-canned meat keeps its quality for up to about two years in a cool, dark spot — label jars with the date so older ones get used first.
  • When in doubt, don't taste it. A lid that isn't concave, any bulging, spurting liquid on opening, or an off smell all mean the jar goes in the trash, unopened and untasted.
