Body Condition Scoring Goats and Sheep: A Beginner's Guide to Where to Feel, What Each Score Means & Targets Through the Year

A thick winter coat or a full fleece can hide a thin doe or an overweight ewe until it's too late to fix before kidding or lambing. Body condition scoring takes a minute per animal and only your hands. Here's where to feel, what each score from 1 to 5 means, when to check, and the target scores extension specialists recommend from breeding to weaning.

Why you have to feel, not look

Weight alone doesn't tell you whether a goat or ewe is in good shape, because frame size and pregnancy change the number on the scale. A body condition score (BCS) describes how much muscle and fat an animal is carrying, and it's one of the cheapest management tools you have. The catch is that you can't do it by eye. Michigan State University Extension says a score "cannot be assigned by simply visually evaluating an animal": you have to put your hands on it. Wool hides a lot (Utah State and South Dakota State say visual scoring of sheep in full fleece is unreliable), and Ontario's goat guidance notes a thick winter coat does the same on goats.

The 1-to-5 scale

  • Most guides use 1 to 5 in half-point steps, where 1 is emaciated and 5 is obese (MSU, eXtension Goats/Langston, Ontario, Utah State, SDSU). Montana State's sheep program uses 0 to 5 ("0 being a dead ewe"), and says to use 2.5 when you're torn between 2 and 3.
  • Goats: healthy goats generally score 2.5 to 4.0. Scores of 1.0, 1.5 or 2.0 point to a management or health problem, and 4.5 to 5 are rarely seen and can be harmful (eXtension Goats, MSU).
  • Sheep: about 90% of a flock usually scores between 2 and 4 (Utah State, Montana State).

Where to feel

  • The loin (lumbar area), on both species. This is the stretch of backbone behind the last rib and in front of the hip bone. Feel the spinous processes (the bumps running along the top of the spine), the transverse processes (the short bones sticking out sideways from the spine) and the eye muscle that fills the space between them.
  • The ribs. How easily your fingers slip into the spaces between the ribs, and how much fat covers them (MSU, Ontario). Montana State also checks the fat over the fore-ribs on ewes.
  • The breastbone (sternum), on goats. Goat guides add a third spot: the size of the fat pad you can pinch on the breastbone (eXtension Goats, MSU, Ontario).

How to score, step by step

  1. Have the animal standing, relaxed and on level ground (Montana State), held in a chute, jug, stanchion or milk stand, or by a helper (SDSU).
  2. Run your fingertips along the backbone behind the last rib. Is it a sharp ridge or smooth?
  3. Grasp the spine between your thumb and fingers to feel how much covers the spinous processes (eXtension Goats).
  4. Slip your fingers under the ends of the transverse processes. In thin animals you can easily curl your fingers under them; in fat ones you can't find the ends at all. SDSU notes they are easier to feel in thin sheep.
  5. Lay your whole hand over the loin to judge how full the eye muscle is.
  6. On goats, feel the ribs and pinch the breastbone fat pad.
  7. Write the score down with the date and the animal's ID, so you can compare next time (SDSU).

When to score

Score at the points in the year when you can still change things with feed. Utah State and SDSU both list before breeding, early to mid pregnancy, before lambing or kidding, and at weaning; Ontario adds that goats should be scored often and before any management decision. Mid-pregnancy matters most: SDSU says to check about 60 days before lambing, and Montana State points out that up to 80% of fetal growth happens in the last 4 to 6 weeks of pregnancy, when the dam's needs climb fast. In a big flock, scoring 10 to 20% of the animals gives an estimate (Utah State, SDSU); in a small homestead herd, just score everyone.

Why too thin or too fat both cost you

  • Too thin: Montana State says a ewe scoring 1 to 2 is likely to have lambing problems, light lambs and little milk. Utah State found ewes at 3 to 4 at lambing lost fewer lambs than ewes at 2.5 or below. UF/IFAS adds lower fertility, more disease and parasites, and less milk.
  • Too fat: Utah State says scores above 4 at breeding raise the chance a ewe won't have a lamb, Montana State says fat ewes (4 to 5) can't be economically flushed, and SDSU notes both extremes can cause birthing problems. UF/IFAS points out that extra fat is feed you paid for that doesn't produce anything.

Changing a score

  • It takes time and real weight. SDSU's rule of thumb: raising a sheep one full score takes a gain of about 10 to 12% of body weight, so a 150 lb ewe needs 15 to 18 lb. Start early, not in the last month.
  • Sort and feed by group. Utah State suggests pulling thin animals into their own group on a better diet and moving fat ones to a group on lower-quality feed, so the middle of the herd isn't overfed to rescue the tail end. For working out how much hay a group needs, see how much hay you need for winter.
  • Find out why a thin animal is thin. SDSU recommends recording, separating and running health checks on thin sheep, and says a low score can be a sign of gut worms. If an animal stays thin on good feed, call your vet.
  • In winter, don't forget water. Animals that can't drink enough eat less. See keeping livestock water from freezing.

The short version

Put your hands on every animal: feel the spine and the short side bones behind the last rib, the loin muscle between them, the ribs and, on goats, the breastbone fat pad. Score 1 to 5 in half points; most healthy animals sit between 2.5 and 4. Score before breeding, in mid-pregnancy, before lambing or kidding and at weaning, and write it down. Aim for about 3 to 3.5 at breeding and 3 to 4 at birth (higher for twins), and treat anything under 2 or over 4 as a problem to fix now. Plan for the birth itself with our goat kidding season guide.
