Brooding Baby Chicks: A Beginner's Guide to Brooder Temperature, Space, Bedding & the First Six Weeks

Day-old chicks can't keep themselves warm. Mississippi State University Extension notes that a newly hatched chick's body temperature runs about 3°F below an adult bird's, and it takes 2 to 4 weeks for chicks to develop their own temperature control. Until then, the brooder is their mother hen. Get the heat, space, bedding, feed and water right for the first few weeks and most of the problems beginners worry about never show up. Here's how extension poultry specialists say to do it.

Before the chicks arrive

  • Buy from a clean source. University of Maine Cooperative Extension recommends buying chicks from a hatchery that takes part in the National Poultry Improvement Plan (NPIP), and asking the hatchery to vaccinate for Marek's disease.
  • Clean the space. UMaine's checklist: brush down cobwebs and dust, sweep and wash the floor, scrub with a disinfectant, then air the building out. Keep rats, mice and other predators out.
  • Start the heat a day early. Mississippi State says to light the brooder 24 hours before the chicks arrive, so you can confirm it works and adjust the temperature. UNH Extension and UMaine give the same advice: set up and test the brooder at least a day ahead.
  • Add a brooder guard. For a bigger batch, Mississippi State uses an 18-inch-high solid guard (corrugated cardboard works) placed 3 to 4 feet out from the edge of the heat source, so chicks can't wander off and chill in a corner. Widen it every day and remove it after 7 to 14 days. UNH says a guard can usually come out after the first week. Solid sides also block drafts (UF/IFAS Extension).

Brooder temperature, week by week

Every source agrees on the starting point and the rule: 90 to 95°F at chick level for the first week, then about 5°F cooler each week. UNH Extension measures 2.5 inches above the litter directly under the hood; UMaine says 95°F at 2 to 3 inches off the floor. You lower the heat by raising the lamp (UNH).

Schedule from Mississippi State University Extension, which recommends the lower end in warm months and the higher end in winter, and raising the heat about 5°F for a few days if chicks are stressed. Mississippi State notes that by about 5 weeks chicks can hold their own body temperature if the room stays near 70°F. UNH says the heat can come off once the temperature under the brooder is in the 65 to 70°F range, or matches the temperature outside; UMaine adds that you can switch the brooder off on warm days.

Watch the chicks, not just the thermometer

A thermometer tells you the temperature in one spot. The chicks tell you whether it's right. Mississippi State recommends checking on them several times a day, especially in the evening.

What you seeWhat it means
Chicks huddled tightly under the heat, chirping sharplyToo cold: lower the lamp a little at a time (UF/IFAS; Mississippi State)
Chicks pressed against the far walls, panting or drowsyToo hot: raise the lamp (Mississippi State; UNH)
Chicks bunched on one side of the brooderA draft is hitting the other side (UNH; Mississippi State)
Chicks spread evenly, softly peeping, eating and exploringJust right (UNH; Mississippi State; UMaine)

Chicks need fresh air, but not a draft (UMaine).

How much space chicks need

Chicks grow fast, and a brooder that's roomy on day one is crowded by week three. University of Maine Extension's space guide:

4–8 weeks1 sq ft2 inches0.75 inch
8–12 weeks2 sq ft2.5 inches1 inch

So 12 chicks need about 6 square feet for the first month (a 2 by 3 foot box) and about 12 square feet from 4 to 8 weeks. UMaine stresses that the table is a starting point; watch the birds to make sure every chick can reach feed and water easily. Bedding: what to use and what to avoid

  • Good choices: UMaine recommends 2 to 4 inches of clean, dry litter, such as dry pine shavings. UNH lists wood shavings as the most common and effective option, along with ground corn cobs, peanut or rice hulls, and chopped straw.
  • Avoid slippery floors. UNH warns against shiny printed newspaper, cardboard or plastic as the floor surface, because chicks slip on it and can damage their legs.
  • Avoid hardwood shavings and hay, which UMaine says are not suitable for chick litter.
  • Keep it dry. Wet litter wastes heat (Mississippi State) and breeds disease. Remove wet spots around the waterer as they appear.

Heat lamp safety

Heat lamps start barn and garage fires every year. UNH Extension's rules: hang the lamp from a chain, never by its cord; keep it high enough that it can't ignite the bedding; protect the bulb from splashing water, which can shatter a hot bulb; and use a hard-glass bulb where you can. Mississippi State notes that setting a brooder too low raises the risk of igniting the litter and burning chicks, and recommends at least 5 to 6 inches of clearance above the chicks' backs. Follow the manufacturer's height guidance, check that the lamp's clamp and chain are secure every day, and keep the brooder well away from hay, feed sacks and anything else that burns.

Feed and water

  • Starter feed: UMaine recommends a complete chick starter for the first six weeks, then a grower feed. For laying-breed pullets, UMaine's starter protein is 18 to 20%; meat-type broilers start higher, at 22 to 23%. UF/IFAS Extension gives 20 to 22% for the first six weeks. Use fresh feed, stored no more than four weeks (UMaine).
  • Medicated or not? UMaine says the conventional way to control coccidiosis is a starter and grower containing a coccidiostat. But chicks vaccinated against coccidiosis (Coccivac) do not need a coccidiostat and should get non-medicated feed. Ask your hatchery which vaccines your chicks received before you buy feed.
  • Help them find it. For the first few days, sprinkle feed on egg flats, box lids or shallow trays under the heat (Mississippi State; UNH); UMaine uses cut-down boxes with sides about 1¼ inches high for the first 7 to 10 days. Don't fill feeders more than half full, which wastes feed (UMaine).
  • Water: UMaine's rule of thumb is one quart fountain waterer per 16 chicks the first week, and one per 20 chicks from 1 to 4 weeks. Mississippi State suggests at least two 1-gallon waterers per 100 chicks. UNH recommends dipping each chick's beak in the water when it arrives so it learns where water is. Clean waterers daily (UMaine).
  • Placement: arrange feeders and waterers like spokes of a wheel around the heat, not all directly under it, so chicks can always eat and drink at a comfortable temperature (Mississippi State; UNH).

Keep your family healthy too

Any backyard poultry, including healthy-looking chicks, can carry Salmonella. The CDC's advice: wash your hands with soap and water right after touching chicks or anything in the brooder; don't kiss or snuggle them, or eat and drink around them; keep the flock and its supplies (feeders, waterers, coop shoes) outside the house and clean them outside; supervise children, and keep children younger than 5 from handling chicks. That last point matters if your brooder is in a mudroom or spare bedroom: a garage, shed or barn is a better home for it.

Moving out of the brooder

Lower the heat on schedule and the chicks will be ready by the time they're feathered. Mississippi State says chicks can maintain their own body temperature at about 5 weeks with a room near 70°F, and UNH says to remove the heat once the brooder and outdoor temperatures match. In cold weather, that may take longer. Move them to a draft-free, predator-proof coop, and keep young birds separate from an older flock until they're close in size; our adding new chickens guide covers introductions.

The short version

Buy NPIP chicks, clean the brooder, and run the heat for 24 hours before they arrive. Start at 90 to 95°F at chick level and drop 5°F a week, but trust the chicks' behavior over the thermometer. Give them half a square foot each for the first month, dry pine shavings (not slick newspaper), a chained, high-hung lamp, fresh starter feed and clean water every day. Wash your hands after every visit.
