Rendering Beeswax at Home: A Beginner's Guide to Melting, Filtering & Molding Wax From Cappings and Old Comb

Every honey harvest leaves a second crop behind: the wax cappings you sliced off the frames, plus any old or broken comb. Utah State University Extension puts the yield at about 1 to 2 pounds of wax for every 100 pounds of honey harvested, so it's worth saving. Winter, with the bees clustered and the extractor put away, is a good time to turn that sticky bucket into clean golden blocks for candles, salves and woodworking.

Cappings vs. old comb

  • Cappings make the cleanest wax. University of Florida Extension notes they come from the newest comb and usually render the best-colored, light-yellow wax.
  • Old brood comb renders darker wax, often quite dark (UF/IFAS), and leaves much more debris (cocoons, pollen and propolis), so you get less wax per pound. Keep it in a separate batch from your cappings so it doesn't darken the good wax.

Before you melt: drain and rinse

  1. Get the honey out first. USU Extension says to extract as much honey as possible, then let the cappings drain in a strainer. (Our honey harvesting guide covers uncapping and extracting.)
  2. Pick out the big debris (dead bees, wood bits) by hand or with a brush.
  3. Rinse gently in cool water to wash off leftover honey. USU warns that too much water pressure washes wax away with it.
  4. Keep sticky wax away from the bees. UF/IFAS notes that honey left on wax can trigger robbing during a nectar dearth, so don't leave buckets of wet cappings in the yard.

Safety first: beeswax is flammable. Melt it in a water bath (double boiler), never in a pot sitting directly on a burner, keep the heat low, and never leave melting wax unattended. Stainless steel or enamel pots avoid the discoloring metals above; thrift-store pots kept only for wax are ideal. Keep a lid nearby to smother a flare-up, and never pour water on burning wax.

Four ways to melt it

  1. Double boiler (water bath). USU's main method: a large pot of water with a smaller pot or heat-resistant bowl set in it, heat on medium or lower, stir occasionally. The water keeps the wax well below the temperatures that damage it.
  2. Slow cooker. USU lists it as an alternative that takes longer; use one you keep just for wax.
  3. The cheesecloth-bag method. Also from USU: tie the wax up in cheesecloth or muslin and float the bundle in heated water. The wax melts out and rises; most of the dirt stays inside the bag. Repeat if needed. Let the pot cool and lift the wax disk off the water.
  4. Solar wax melter. University of Florida Extension describes a box with a glass lid, a black interior and a slanted (usually metal) tray inside, faced toward the sun. Wax melts off the tray and drips into a collecting pan. It must be bee-tight, or bees will rob it. UF/IFAS calls solar-rendered wax "extremely clean," and the sun bleaches it lighter. It only works in warm, sunny weather, so it's a spring and summer option in most places.

Filtering and settling

  • Strain while it's liquid. Pour through cheesecloth, muslin or a coffee filter over a bowl. USU: "The finer your strainer, the cleaner your wax will be." Start coarse and go finer on later passes.
  • Let gravity help. UF/IFAS says the longer wax stays liquid, the more foreign material settles out. Holding it in a warm, turned-off oven works.
  • Scrape the bottom. Fine dirt ends up on the underside of the cooled block. Scrape it off (UF/IFAS) and re-melt and strain again if the wax still looks cloudy. USU suggests 1 to 2 extra filtering passes when needed.
  • Expect dark leftovers from old comb. The dark, spongy residue left in the cloth is mostly cocoons and debris. Compost or discard it.

Cooling, molding and storing

  • Cool slowly, at room temperature. USU warns that the fridge or freezer will crack the wax; UF/IFAS also says to cool it "very slowly."
  • Use a mold with sloped sides. UF/IFAS prefers a bread pan with slanted sides, so the block pops out. Silicone molds and lined loaf pans work for small blocks.
  • Store it airtight, dry and out of the sun (USU). Wrapping blocks in plastic also keeps them clean, and Bee Culture notes stored wax absorbs odors from around it.

What to make with it

Clean, light cappings wax is the one to save for candles, lip balm and salves; darker wax from old comb is fine for waterproofing, wood finishes, lubricating drawer runners or coating new foundation. Our herbal oils, salves and candles guide covers safe beginner projects. Some beekeepers also melt a little clean wax back onto new frames to encourage the bees to draw comb.

The short version

Drain the honey, rinse the cappings in cool water, and melt the wax slowly in a water bath, keeping it below about 180°F. Use stainless steel or enamel, not aluminum, copper, iron or zinc. Strain through progressively finer cloth, let the liquid wax settle while it's warm, scrape the dirt off the bottom of the cooled block and repeat if needed. Cool it slowly at room temperature in a sloped mold, and store it wrapped, dry and out of the sun. Keep old-comb wax separate from cappings wax.
