Preparing for Goat Kidding Season: A Beginner's Guide to Late-Pregnancy Feeding, Kidding Kits, Labor Signs & Newborn Care

Most does kid without any help at all. The ones that don't usually give you a short window to act, and the difference between a good kidding season and a hard one is mostly decided weeks earlier: how the doe was fed, whether she was vaccinated, and whether the pen and kit were ready before she was. Here's the countdown university Extension goat specialists recommend, the signs that labor is close, the time limits for calling the vet, and the first hour of a newborn kid's life.

The countdown: what to do and when

A goat pregnancy runs about five months (N.C. Cooperative Extension), roughly 150 days, so mark each doe's due date from her breeding date as soon as you know it. The weeks before that date are where most of the work is:

WhenWhat to do
Last 4 to 6 weeksStep up feed quality. Nutrient needs almost double in this stretch (Kentucky State).
4 to 6 weeks beforeCD&T booster (Clostridium perfringens types C and D plus tetanus) so antibodies pass to the kids through colostrum. Ask your vet about a selenium/vitamin E shot if your area is selenium-deficient and it isn't in the feed or mineral (Purdue, Ohio State).
Before the last 3 weeksFinish any hoof trimming, parasite checks and deworming. Kentucky State advises not working does in the last 3 weeks unless you must, and checking every label for pregnancy warnings.
About 2 weeks beforePens cleaned, bedded and ready; kit packed; heat lamps checked; vet's number by the door.
Final daysWatch for the signs of labor below and start regular barn checks.

Kentucky State calls CD&T the one vaccine recommended for every goat herd, and suggests giving it about 4 weeks before kidding; Purdue and Ohio State both say 4 to 6 weeks.

Feeding the last month: avoid pregnancy toxemia

Most of a kid's growth happens in the last six weeks, just as the growing kids leave less room in the rumen for feed (Purdue, Ohio State). A doe carrying twins or triplets can end up burning more energy than she can eat. That shortfall causes pregnancy toxemia (also called ketosis), which Kentucky State calls the most serious problem of late pregnancy.

  • Feed better, not just more. Purdue recommends high-quality forage plus energy-dense concentrate. N.C. Cooperative Extension says does in the last 30 days of pregnancy and lactating does should be on about 16% protein, with supplement if pasture or hay is limited or poor.
  • Aim for moderate condition. Kentucky State's target is a body condition score of 2 to 3 on a 5-point scale. Fat does are more prone to toxemia and can have oversized kids and fat in the birth canal; thin does need more grain and better hay.
  • Know the warning signs. Purdue lists a doe that's dull, off feed or slow to the trough, sometimes with swollen pasterns, then weakness, staggering or "stargazing" and being unable to rise. It is life-threatening and needs treatment the day you see it. Kansas State lists propylene glycol as a supplement used for toxemia around kidding; call your vet for the right treatment and dose.

Set up the kidding pen

Kansas State recommends individual kidding pens (often called jugs) of at least 5 by 5 feet, cleaned and bedded before the first doe is due and cleaned again between each doe and her kids. Keep them dry, draft-free and away from doors. Straw, pine shavings or cheap hay all work as bedding (eXtension). Kentucky State suggests disinfecting or liming portable pens and letting them sun-dry for a couple of days.

Heat lamps start barn fires. If you use one, check it before the season, hang it securely where the doe can't reach it or chew the cord (Purdue), and keep it away from hay and bedding. Kentucky State and N.C. Extension describe a simple "kid box" made from a 55-gallon plastic barrel or wood: kids can get in under the lamp, the doe can't, and hay stays away from the bulb.

Pack the kidding kit

Kentucky State's advice is to keep everything in one tote or bucket so you aren't hunting for it at 2 a.m. Combining the Extension lists, a beginner's kit holds:

  • For the birth: disposable exam gloves and long OB sleeves, OB lubricant, clean towels (lots), a clean bucket for warm soapy water, a halter, a flashlight or headlamp, and a thermometer.
  • For the kids: navel dip (7% iodine per eXtension, or dilute chlorhexidine per Purdue) in a small cup, small scissors, a bottle and nipple, a 60 cc syringe and soft feeding tube, and frozen or powdered colostrum (Kansas State, Kentucky State).
  • For records: ear tags or a marker, a scale for birth weights, and a notebook or record sheet.
  • Phone numbers: your vet and an experienced goat keeper you can call. Kansas State stresses setting up a vet relationship before kidding season, not during an emergency.

Signs labor is close

No single sign is reliable on its own, and experienced does sometimes show very little (Purdue). Watch for several together:

  • About 2 weeks out: the ligaments on either side of the tail head soften and the area looks hollow (eXtension).
  • Last 3 to 4 days: the udder fills fast and becomes tight; teats look full in the last day or two. First-time does may not fill until after kidding (eXtension).
  • Last day: restlessness, pawing and "nesting" in the bedding, lying down and getting up again, looking or kicking at her sides, wandering off from the herd, soft talking bleats, going off feed, and a string of mucus from the vulva (Kentucky State, Kansas State).

Once these appear, check her often. Kansas State suggests every 3 to 4 hours; Kentucky State says every 4 to 6 hours in mild weather and every 2 to 3 hours in the cold.

The stages of labor and when to get help

StageWhat you seeTypical length
1. Early laborRestless, nesting, mild contractions as the cervix opensSeveral hours: about 4 (eXtension), 1 to 4 (Ohio State), up to 12 (Purdue)
2. DeliveryWater sac appears, hard pushing, kids bornThe first kid usually within 30 to 60 minutes of the water sac (eXtension, Kansas State, Kentucky State)
3. AfterbirthPlacenta passesUsually within 4 to 6 hours (Kansas State)

A normal kid arrives in a "diving" position: two front feet with the nose resting on them. Kids also come hind feet first, especially twins. Sources differ on how hands-off to be with a backwards kid, so this is a good thing to talk through with your vet ahead of time.

Get help (or call the vet) when:

  • She has pushed hard for about 30 minutes with no progress, or strained with no water sac at all (Purdue, Kansas State, eXtension). Ohio State suggests checking the kid's position after just 20 minutes of no progress.
  • You see a head with no feet, one leg, a tail first, or legs you can't match to one kid. Kansas State warns to check that legs and head belong to the same kid before pulling, and not to try to turn a breech kid around.
  • You've tried for about 15 minutes to correct a position and can't (Kansas State), or the kid seems too big or the cervix isn't open (Kentucky State).
  • The fluid is dark brown rather than clear or yellowish (a sign of stress, Kansas State), or she aborts.
  • The placenta hasn't come out within 12 hours (Purdue). Never pull on it.

If you do go in, wash up, wear a clean glove and use plenty of lubricant. Purdue warns that if a delivery drags on for 2 to 3 hours the cervix starts to close, so waiting too long makes things harder, not easier.

Protect yourself too. Kansas State notes that abortions and difficult births can carry diseases people can catch. Wear gloves, wash your hands and change gloves between does, and call your vet about any abortion or unusual discharge.

The first hour for newborn kids

  • Breathing first. Clear mucus from the nose and mouth and rub the kid briskly with a towel; a piece of straw tickled in a nostril can start a sneeze (eXtension, Purdue). Purdue advises against swinging or hanging kids upside down; sit the kid up on its chest instead.
  • Let mom take over. Put the kid by the doe's head so she can lick it dry (Kansas State).
  • Dip the navel. Trim a long cord to under 2 inches and dip or spray it (Kansas State) with 7% iodine (eXtension) or dilute chlorhexidine (Purdue).
  • Strip the teats. Milk a squirt from each teat to clear the wax plug and make sure milk flows (Kansas State).
  • Colostrum, fast. Kids should be up and nursing within about an hour (Kansas State says 30 to 60 minutes; Purdue, 1 to 2 hours). The sooner they nurse, the more antibodies they absorb, and absorption drops off sharply after the first 24 hours (eXtension).

If you have to bottle- or tube-feed, Kansas State's guideline is about 10% of body weight in the first hour and more over the first day, split into feedings every 3 to 4 hours:

Warm colostrum to 102 to 103°F in a water bath; never microwave it. Milk replacer is not a substitute. Kansas State's order of preference is fresh colostrum from another doe, then your own frozen colostrum, then colostrum from another herd, then cow colostrum, then commercial colostrum replacer. Freezing a little from each easy kidding builds your supply for next year.

Watch for chilled kids

A newborn's normal temperature is 102 to 103°F (Kansas State). Wet kids chill fast in cold, wind or rain, which is why a dry, draft-free pen matters so much (eXtension).

  • 99 to 101°F: mildly chilled. Feed warm colostrum, return it to the doe and keep watching.
  • Below 99°F: warm the kid first, with a warming box or heat lamp, until it's above 99°F. Do not tube-feed a cold kid; its gut can't handle it yet. Then give a few ounces of warm colostrum.

A kid that isn't nursing often stands hunched, doesn't stretch when it gets up, has a hollow belly and a cold mouth, and cries little (Kansas State). Kentucky State's warning is that kids can go from healthy to near death quickly, so act on these signs right away.

After kidding

Kansas State suggests keeping each doe and her kids in the jug for 24 to 48 hours so they bond, then moving them to a mixing pen. Weigh and tag kids before they leave the jug, and record the birth date, weights, sex, dam and any help given. Those notes are what tell you next year which does kid easily and which ones need a closer eye.

The short version

Count about five months from breeding. In the last 4 to 6 weeks, feed better-quality hay and some concentrate, keep does in moderate condition, and give the CD&T booster. Have a clean, dry 5×5 ft pen and a packed kit ready two weeks early. Once the water sac shows, expect a kid within about an hour, and get help after 30 minutes of hard pushing with no progress or any abnormal position. Newborns need a clear airway, a dipped navel, and colostrum within the first hour.
