Package Bees vs. Nucs: A Beginner's Guide to Buying, Ordering & Installing Your First Honey Bees

Your first bees won't arrive until spring, but the decision about how to buy them is a winter job. Alabama Cooperative Extension tells new beekeepers to place orders in November or December for spring delivery, and suppliers often sell out. The two common choices are a package and a nuc. Here's what each one actually is, what it costs, and how extension specialists install them.

What you actually get

  • A package is a screened wooden box holding about 3 pounds of worker bees plus a mated queen in her own small cage, usually with a can of sugar syrup for the trip (Texas A&M AgriLife; Alabama Extension). UF/IFAS Extension says 3 pounds works out to about 10,000 bees. There is no comb, no honey, no pollen and no brood; the queen hasn't started laying in her new home yet.
  • A nuc (short for nucleus colony) is a small working colony on frames. Alabama Extension describes it as five frames of drawn comb with a laying queen, two frames of food and two to three frames of brood. Texas A&M says 4 to 5 frames; UF/IFAS says five Langstroth frames including 2 to 4 of brood, and a bee count of roughly 6,000 to 12,000 that it calls "highly variable."

The practical difference is the head start. A nuc's queen is already laying and there's brood on the frames to replace workers as they die. A package colony starts from bare foundation, and UF/IFAS notes it takes at least a month before it produces new bees.

Alabama Extension also lists a third option, buying an established hive (about $300), and says a healthy nuc should fill two hive boxes by the end of its first year.

When to order, and when bees arrive

  • Order in winter. Alabama Extension: order in November or December for delivery the following spring. UF/IFAS: small-scale beekeepers usually pre-order from December to February, with bees available around April.
  • Packages tend to come first, nucs later. In Alabama, packages arrive from mid-March through April and nucs from mid-March to early May. UF/IFAS says packages are sometimes available sooner and nucs sometimes later. Further north, dates shift later; ask your supplier for their pickup window.
  • Aim for the nectar flow. Alabama Extension recommends installing a package before or early in the spring nectar flow, so the new colony has forage while it builds comb.
  • Have the hive ready first. Assemble the hive and set up the site before the bees arrive. Texas A&M suggests setting boxes out a few days ahead and mowing tall grass around the site. A home-built Langstroth hive works fine if it's built to bee space.

Buy from a reputable, local supplier

  • UF/IFAS Extension advises buying only from a well-established, reputable local supplier and discourages internet classified ads, warning of colonies with pests or disease, old queens, feral colonies and, in parts of the South, Africanized bees.
  • Ask to look at a nuc before you pay. Alabama Extension says to look for a productive laying queen, plenty of healthy brood and adult bees. It also notes nucs made the previous summer or fall are better than ones made in early spring.
  • Ask about mite treatment history. UF/IFAS says sellers treat nucs for varroa, but some mites may be hidden in capped brood; Alabama Extension recommends sampling a new nuc for varroa about a month after installing it (see how to test for varroa).
  • Check your state's rules. Alabama, for example, bars bees on comb from entering the state, so nucs there must be bought in-state, and apiaries must be registered every year. Your state apiary inspector or extension office can tell you what applies where you live.
  • Buying a package or nuc, rather than catching a swarm, also gives you some choice of genetics, which Texas A&M says matters most for beginners and anyone with close neighbors who wants gentle bees.

Installing a package, step by step

Texas A&M says to install a package within 1 to 2 days of arrival. Its steps and Alabama Extension's are close:

  1. Suit up, and remove four or five frames from the brood box to make room.
  2. Pry off the package lid, lift out the syrup can and pull out the queen cage. Put the lid back right away.
  3. Remove the cork from the queen cage to expose the candy plug (Texas A&M suggests gently poking a nail through the candy, without touching the queen). The workers eat through the candy and release her in a day or two.
  4. Hang the cage between two frames near the center. Texas A&M notes a vertical cage with the candy up can drip melted candy onto the queen, and candy down can get blocked by dead workers; horizontal, screen side down, avoids both.
  5. Lightly spray the bees with 1:1 sugar syrup, then either shake them into the hive (Texas A&M) or set the open package inside the box among the frames and let them walk out (Alabama Extension).
  6. Add a feeder of light 1:1 syrup and an entrance reducer, and close the hive.
  7. Check that the queen is out of her cage: Alabama Extension checks after three days, Texas A&M waits a week. If she's still caged, release her onto the frames by hand. Then remove the empty package box and put the missing frames back.
The syrup recipe: Alabama Extension's light syrup is 1 pound of white cane sugar dissolved in 1 pound (2 cups) of warm water, a 1:1 ratio by weight. A package colony has no stored food at all, so keep the feeder full while the bees draw comb; Texas A&M notes small feeders may need refilling daily.

Installing a nuc

  1. Bring the nuc home closed up and out of direct sun. Texas A&M suggests covering it with a sheet in a truck bed, or carrying it inside the vehicle if it's above 80°F.
  2. Set it on or beside the new hive and open its entrance. Texas A&M lets the bees forage for a day after an overnight rest; Alabama Extension leaves the nuc in place for 5 days and moves the frames on day 6.
  3. Move the frames into the center of the new 10-frame brood box in the same order and facing the same way they sat in the nuc. Spot the queen if you can, and note how many frames hold brood and food.
  4. Fill the empty spaces on either side with new frames, then add a feeder of light syrup and an entrance reducer.
  5. Wait about a week before inspecting. Texas A&M says a moved queen may take up to 2 weeks to start laying again, so don't panic over a short pause; check again a week or two later.

Which should a beginner buy?

If you can buy a healthy nuc from a local supplier you trust, it's the more forgiving start: brood, comb and a proven laying queen, with a better chance of making it through the first winter. A package costs less, ships anywhere and fits any hive style, and it lets you watch a colony build from nothing, but it needs more feeding and more patience, and extension sources agree it carries a higher risk of failing in year one. Many beginners start with two hives rather than one, so they can compare colonies and borrow a frame of brood if one struggles.

The short version

Order this winter (November to February) for spring pickup. A package is about 3 pounds of bees and a caged queen, with no comb; a nuc is 4 to 5 frames with brood, food and a laying queen. Nucs cost more but establish faster. Buy locally from a reputable supplier, have the hive built before the bees arrive, feed 1:1 syrup, check that a package queen is released after 3 to 7 days, and test a new nuc for varroa about a month in.
