Protecting Young Fruit Trees in Winter: A Beginner's Guide to Rabbit Guards, Vole Cages, Deer & Sunscald

A young apple or cherry tree can survive a hard winter and still be dead by spring because rabbits or voles chewed a ring of bark around its trunk, or because the sun split the bark on its southwest side. Both problems are cheap to prevent in an afternoon of late-fall work. Here's how, drawn from Iowa State, Illinois, Vermont, Maine, Wisconsin and Utah State Extension.

Why winter is the dangerous season for young trees

When snow covers the grass, rabbits, mice and voles turn to tree bark. They feed on the soft, living tissue between the bark and the wood, and Iowa State University Extension explains why that matters: if they chew all the way around the trunk (girdling), the tree dies, because that layer is what carries sugars from the leaves down to the roots. Iowa State's fruit-tree FAQ puts it bluntly: a girdled fruit tree is "essentially destroyed."

  • Apples and crabapples are favorites. University of Vermont Extension notes their bark is especially attractive to rabbits, mice and voles.
  • Damage can start above the guard. Rabbits standing on deep snow can chew higher up the trunk than you'd expect (UVM).
  • Voles work out of sight. University of Maine Extension says voles (meadow mice) girdle the lower trunk, mostly in winter, often under snow or mulch, and UVM adds that they can tunnel down and damage roots too. Iowa State says vole activity in a lawn shows up after snowmelt as narrow, winding runways in the grass.

Rabbit and vole guards: hardware cloth cylinders

The most reliable protection is a loose cylinder of hardware cloth around the lower trunk. Every extension source below agrees on the basic idea; they differ a little on the numbers.

DetailWhat extension guides say
Material1/4-inch hardware cloth stops voles and mice (Iowa State, UVM). Wisconsin's Dane County Extension also accepts 1/2-inch mesh, but says chicken wire is not recommended for voles because they slip through it.
HeightIowa State's how-to says 24 to 36 inches; its fruit-tree FAQ says 10 to 12 inches above the snow depth you expect. UVM protects from the soil line to at least 2 feet, or up to the first branches in deep-snow areas. Dane County prefers 36- or 48-inch mesh where snow is deep.
Distance from the trunk1 to 2 inches out from the trunk (Iowa State FAQ), "not tight against the trunk" (Maine). UVM says make it wide enough to allow five years of growth.
At ground levelBury the bottom 2 to 3 inches (Iowa State, Illinois); UVM sets it 4 to 6 inches into the ground. Dane County instead splays the bottom out flat and pins it with sod staples so voles can't tunnel under.
Closing itFasten the edges with rubber-coated wire or cable ties so it's easy to open in spring (Dane County).
How tall is tall enough? The sources don't agree on one number because snow depth varies so much. A practical rule that covers all of them: at least 24 inches, and at least a foot above the deepest snow you usually get. If you're in heavy-snow country, use 36- or 48-inch mesh. Wear gloves and eye protection when cutting it; Dane County warns that the cut edges are very sharp.

Plastic spiral guards: handy, but take them off in spring

White plastic spiral wraps are quick to put on and also shade the bark. UVM lists 24-inch spirals as an option, and Illinois says plastic or vinyl guards must be wider than the trunk. But both UVM and Maine warn they must come off in spring, because they can harbor insects and encourage trunk borers. Illinois says the same of any tubes or wraps: remove them for the growing season. Hardware cloth set well away from the trunk can stay on year-round, and UVM notes that sealed at the top it also helps keep apple borers out.

Clear the base of the tree

  • Pull mulch back. Iowa State says keep wood mulch at least 6 inches from the trunk, and if you use straw or leaves as winter mulch, put hardware cloth around the trunk first. Mulch against bark is ideal vole cover.
  • Keep a bare circle. UVM recommends a vegetation-free zone at least 2 feet out from the trunk to discourage voles. Illinois also says to remove nearby vegetation that gives them shelter.
  • Water before freeze-up. Trees planted in the last 2 or 3 years should get a deep watering before winter if fall has been dry (Iowa State); Illinois says keep watering deeply until the ground freezes. Utah State adds that drought-stressed trees are more prone to sunscald.

When to put guards on and take them off

  • On: Iowa State says install rabbit guards by early to mid-November. If you're reading this later, put them on now; it's never too late while the bark is still intact.
  • During winter: check them after big storms. Iowa State suggests clearing snow from around guards after heavy snowfall, and Illinois says check for both animal damage and damage to the guards themselves.
  • Off: in spring, typically April once the snow is gone (Iowa State), or "once mid-April comes" and the grass is greening (Dane County). Plastic spirals and wraps must come off; well-spaced hardware cloth can stay.

Deer

Deer browse buds and shoots rather than chewing bark at the base, but on a young or dwarf tree that can be just as damaging; Maine Extension says it can severely stunt them. Iowa State says deer feed up to about 6 feet, higher when standing on deep snow.

  • Single trees: a wire cage at least 6 feet tall (shorter if closed on top) per Iowa State; Illinois suggests a 5-foot wire tube for specimen trees.
  • Whole orchards: a fence at least 8 feet tall (Iowa State, Illinois, Maine). Iowa State notes a 50-inch fence is often enough for small enclosures under about 8 by 16 feet, since deer avoid jumping into tight spaces.
  • Repellents: start in September or October, spray from about 6 feet down, reapply about weekly or after rain or snow, and rotate products (Iowa State). Maine mentions scented soap bars hung in the branches as a short-term deterrent.
  • Buck rubs: in fall, bucks rub thin-barked young trees about 1.5 to 3.5 feet up. Iowa State says the risk drops once trunks reach 3 to 4 inches across; three stakes set no more than 18 inches apart around the trunk is a simple fix.

Sunscald (southwest injury)

Sunscald is winter damage you'll usually find in spring: a long, sunken or split patch of dead bark on the south or southwest side of the trunk. Utah State University Extension explains the cause: on sunny winter days the bark on that side warms up and the living cells under it come out of dormancy, then freeze again when the sun drops. Utah State cites research showing the south side of a trunk can be as much as 77°F warmer than the north side on a cold day.

  • Who's at risk: young, thin-barked trees, including apple, cherry, crabapple and peach. Utah State notes cherries, peaches and plums can stay susceptible even as they age.
  • White paint: paint the lower trunk, or at least the southwest side, with white latex paint, which reflects the sun. Utah State says use interior latex, since some exterior paints contain additives that can harm trees, and it can be diluted 1:1 with water.
  • White tree wrap: wrap the trunk and lower limbs in late fall and remove it the following spring after the freezing nights are over. Utah State says keep this up for at least two to three winters after planting, and not to leave wraps on into the growing season.
  • Shade the trunk: a light-colored board or opaque fence on the south and west sides also works (Utah State).

If you find damage in spring

  • Chewed on one side only: the tree can often recover, and the wound may close over with callus in time (Iowa State, UVM).
  • Chewed all the way around: the tree will die unless it is bridge grafted (grafting scions across the wound). UVM calls bridge grafting "a dying art," and Iowa State says it's difficult for home gardeners, so for most people replacing the tree is the realistic choice.
  • Don't paint the wound. Iowa State says wound dressings, pruning paints, latex paint and wraps won't repair severe damage, and Utah State says not to paint or seal sunscald wounds.

Spring is also when you'll do formative pruning; our guide to pruning fruit trees in winter covers what to cut and when.

The short version

Before the ground freezes, give each young fruit tree a deep drink, pull mulch at least 6 inches back from the trunk and clear a bare circle around it. Wrap the lower trunk in a loose cylinder of 1/4-inch hardware cloth, 1 to 2 inches from the bark, at least 24 inches tall and a foot above your usual snow, with the bottom buried or pinned down. Add a white spiral, white tree wrap or diluted interior white latex paint on the southwest side against sunscald, cage or fence against deer where they're a problem, and check everything after big storms. Take plastic guards and wraps off in April. If you're still choosing trees, start with our backyard apple orchard guide.
