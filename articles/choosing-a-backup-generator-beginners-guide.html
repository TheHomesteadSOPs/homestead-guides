Choosing a Backup Generator: A Beginner's Guide to Sizing Watts, Transfer Switches, Fuel & Carbon Monoxide Safety

When an ice storm takes the power out, a generator keeps the freezer cold, the well pump running and the furnace fan turning. Buying the right size comes down to simple arithmetic, and using it safely comes down to a few rules that aren't optional. Here's how Mississippi State University Extension, University of Illinois Extension, Wisconsin's consumer protection bureau, the Consumer Product Safety Commission and an electric utility's sizing worksheet say to choose and run a backup generator.

Portable or standby?

There are two broad kinds of home generator, and they connect to the house in very different ways. Wisconsin's consumer protection bureau (DATCP) explains that a permanent standby generator is wired in to "energize the building's wiring," which means it needs a transfer switch and should be installed only by "a qualified professional, such as a licensed electric contractor." A portable generator, by contrast, is "designed to be connected only to selected appliances or lamps" through extension cords, and DATCP says it should never be connected directly to a building's wiring.

Most homesteads start with a portable unit: it's cheaper, it can run a freezer in the barn as easily as one in the kitchen, and it can travel to a cabin or a fence-line job. The rest of this guide focuses on choosing and using one, with notes on where a transfer switch comes in.

Step 1: List what you actually need to run

Before looking at generators, decide what has to keep working in an outage. For most homesteads that short list is the fridge and freezer, the well pump, the furnace blower (a gas or oil furnace still needs electricity to run its fan), a few lights and phone chargers. Our power outage food safety guide explains how long a full freezer holds without power, which helps you decide whether the freezer needs to run all the time or just a few hours a day.

Then find each item's wattage on its label or manual. Mississippi State University Extension adds that if a label lists only amps, you can work out watts the other way round: amps = watts ÷ volts. Its example is a 1,100-watt hair dryer on 120 volts, which draws about 9.2 amps. Add up the amps of everything that will run at once and make sure the total stays under the generator's continuous rating.

Step 2: Running watts vs. starting watts

Anything with an electric motor (a fridge compressor, a well or sump pump, a furnace fan) needs a short burst of extra power to start. Mississippi State says motors typically draw about three times their running current at startup, and that this extra load has to be allowed for when you size the generator. That's why generators list two numbers: running (or rated) watts and starting (or surge) watts.

Notice how the resistance heaters (space heater, water heater) dwarf everything else. Leaving them off the list is the easiest way to keep a generator small.

Step 3: Add it up

The EWEB worksheet uses a simple three-step method:

  1. Pick the items you want to run at the same time and write down each one's running watts and additional starting watts.
  2. Add up all the running watts.
  3. Find the single item with the highest additional starting watts and add that number to your running total. That's your total starting watts.

Only the biggest starting surge is added because, as long as you switch motors on one at a time, only one of them is starting at any moment. Here's a worked example for a small homestead, using the table above:

LoadRunning wattsAdditional starting watts
Refrigerator/freezer7002,200
Well pump, 1/3 HP1,0002,000
Furnace fan blower7001,400
5 LED bulbs75–
Laptop + 2 phone chargers100–
Total2,575Largest: 2,200

Total starting watts = 2,575 + 2,200 = 4,775 watts. So this household needs a generator rated for at least about 2,600 running watts and about 4,800 starting watts. Two practical notes: many well pumps run on 240 volts, so check the pump's label (Mississippi State notes small generators supply 120 or 240 volts, and a hard-wired pump is a transfer-switch job, below). And Mississippi State warns that the size can go wrong in both directions: too small causes operating problems, while too large costs more to buy, burns more fuel and is heavier to move.

Switch things on one at a time. University of Illinois Extension's generator tips say to start the generator first, then plug in appliances one by one to avoid overloading it. Starting the fridge, pump and furnace all at once stacks their surges and can trip the generator even when your math says it's big enough.

Inverter or conventional?

Mississippi State explains the difference: in a conventional generator, the engine speed sets the electrical frequency, so the engine has to run at a fixed speed. An inverter generator regulates voltage and frequency electronically, independent of engine speed, so it can run quieter and throttle down at light loads. The trade-offs it lists:

  • Inverter units are "highly susceptible" to the starting surge from electric motors, so check the surge rating carefully if you plan to run a pump or a fridge.
  • Some inverters produce electrical noise that can interfere with electronics. Mississippi State suggests a plug-in voltage and frequency monitor for any generator, to check what it's actually putting out.
  • Noise (decibel) ratings aren't measured the same way across brands, so it recommends comparing them only within one brand.
  • Electric start is convenient but adds weight, and the 12-volt outlet on some units isn't carefully regulated and is mainly for charging batteries.

Connecting it to the house: transfer switches and backfeed

This is the most dangerous mistake people make with a generator. Never plug a generator into a wall outlet or wire it into your panel without a transfer switch. Wisconsin DATCP explains that electricity can "back-feed" out through your service into the power lines, where the stepped-up voltage can electrocute utility line workers "some distance from your home." University of Illinois Extension says the same: a generator connected to house wiring "must have a transfer switch," which keeps power from flowing back onto utility equipment and endangering line workers or anyone near downed lines.

  • Portable unit: run heavy-duty extension cords from the generator to the appliances themselves (Illinois Extension).
  • Powering house circuits: DATCP recommends a double-pole, double-throw transfer switch installed by a licensed electrician, and says to notify your electric company that you have a standby system.
  • Before starting a backup generator, DATCP says to always properly disconnect from your utility service.

Extension cords and wet weather

  • Use cords "properly sized to carry the electric load" (DATCP), and the shortest cord that will reach (Mississippi State). Check the cord's rating against what you'll plug into it.
  • Inspect cords for exposed wires and frayed insulation and replace damaged ones; never run cords under rugs or carpets (DATCP).
  • Use ground-fault circuit interrupter (GFCI) protection, including extension cords with built-in GFCI (DATCP).
  • Run it dry. Mississippi State says never use a generator in wet conditions. If you must run one in bad weather, Illinois Extension suggests an open, canopy-like cover over a dry surface where water can't puddle, and dry hands whenever you touch it. DATCP adds to keep it away from pools and sprinklers.
  • Set it on level ground; DATCP warns that running at an angle can cause lubrication failure, and that sandy or dusty sites cause problems too.

Carbon monoxide: the rule that matters most

The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission says generator exhaust carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless and "can kill in minutes," and that more than 80 people die each year from CO poisoning caused by portable generators. Its rules:

  • Never run a generator inside a home, garage, basement, crawlspace, shed or porch, "even if doors and windows are open." DATCP adds: not in any enclosure, even with fans running.
  • Run it outside, at least 20 feet from the house, with the exhaust pointed away from your home and other buildings, and away from doors, windows and vents.
  • If you're buying, choose a model with an automatic CO shut-off where available.
  • Install battery or battery-backup CO alarms outside sleeping areas and on every floor, and test them monthly.
  • Symptoms of CO poisoning include headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, vomiting, sleepiness and confusion. If you suspect it, get outside immediately, then call 911.

The 20-foot rule matters for barns and coops too: a generator running beside an animal shelter sends exhaust into it just as it would into a house. Our hunting cabin heating guide covers how CO builds up in small, tight buildings.

Fuel, refueling and keeping it ready

  • Refuel cold. "Never fill a hot generator with fuel" (Mississippi State); Illinois Extension and DATCP both say the generator should be off and cool first. Wipe up any spilled fuel before restarting.
  • Store gasoline in approved containers only, out of children's reach (DATCP), and keep a charged fire extinguisher near the generator.
  • Keep it fueled and fresh. DATCP says to keep the tank filled with fresh fuel and use a fuel conditioner so the gas doesn't break down. Don't add an auxiliary fuel tank: DATCP warns it only adds pressure to the fuel system.
  • Check the fuel type. Most small generators run on regular gasoline; some two-cycle models need oil mixed in, so check the manual (Mississippi State).
  • Exercise it. DATCP says a generator "should be started and loaded at least once a month." A monthly run with a lamp or a power tool plugged in is the time to find a stale carburetor or a dead battery, not the night of the ice storm.
  • Plan fuel use. DATCP notes the heavier the load, the more fuel the generator burns, which is one more reason to run only what you need.

The short version

List the few things that must run in an outage, add up their running watts, then add the single biggest starting surge; that pair of numbers is the generator you're shopping for. Start the generator first and switch loads on one at a time. Never plug it into a wall outlet: use extension cords to individual appliances, or have an electrician install a transfer switch. Run it outdoors, at least 20 feet from any building, never in a garage, and keep CO alarms in the house. Refuel only when it's cool, store fuel in approved cans with a conditioner, and run it under load once a month.
