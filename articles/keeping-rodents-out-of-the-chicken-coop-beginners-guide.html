Keeping Mice and Rats Out of the Chicken Coop: A Beginner's Guide to Rodent-Proofing, Feed Storage & Safe Trapping

Cold weather pushes mice and rats indoors, and a chicken coop full of spilled feed, warm bedding and eggs is about the best winter address a rodent can find. They eat and foul feed, spread disease, and big rats will kill chicks and eat eggs. The fix is mostly construction and housekeeping, not poison. Here's what Cooperative Extension specialists at Oklahoma State, Maryland, Nebraska and the wildlife-damage network recommend.

Why rodents in the coop are worth fighting

It's tempting to shrug off a few mice. Extension sources give good reasons not to:

  • Disease. University of Maryland Extension says rats and mice can carry up to 45 diseases, including salmonellosis and leptospirosis. Oklahoma State names Salmonella and fowl cholera as the main poultry concerns.
  • Feed loss. Oklahoma State cites an estimate of about 25 pounds of feed eaten per rat per year. Ontario's agriculture ministry (OMAFRA) adds that a rat can contaminate around 10 times the feed it actually eats, and produces about 25,000 droppings a year.
  • Eggs and chicks. Oklahoma State notes rats have killed baby chicks and broken and eaten eggs.
  • Fire risk. Rats chew the insulation off electrical wiring. In a coop with a heated waterer or a heat lamp, that matters.

They also multiply fast. Maryland Extension puts a house mouse at 5 to 10 litters a year averaging 6 young, so one female can produce up to 60 offspring in a year. OMAFRA's rule of thumb is about 25 rodents for every one you actually see.

Signs you already have mice or rats

  • Droppings. Mouse droppings look like black grains of rice. Rat droppings are bean-sized, about 3/4 inch long (Maryland Extension, Oklahoma State).
  • Gnawing on feeders, wooden walls, feed bags and door edges. Fresh gnaw marks are pale, with wood chips nearby (OMAFRA).
  • Burrows and runways. Fresh digging along the coop foundation, and dust-free paths along walls.
  • Grease marks on beams, pipes and rafters where rats repeatedly brush past.
  • Nests of shredded paper, leaves or bedding, a musky or strong ammonia smell, and a flock that's restless at night.

Maryland Extension suggests checking the coop at night with a flashlight. Oklahoma State notes that seeing rats in daylight usually means a large population.

The gap rule: 1/4 inch for mice, 1/2 inch for rats

Every source agrees on the numbers. Mice get through openings a little over 1/4 inch, rats through openings a little over 1/2 inch (University of Nebraska–Lincoln NebGuide G1530; the Internet Center for Wildlife Damage Management). OMAFRA's easy version: a mouse fits through a hole the size of your little finger, a rat through one the size of your thumb. And small cracks don't stay small, since rodents gnaw them bigger.

That's why the standard chicken wire on many coops does nothing against rodents. It has holes far bigger than 1/2 inch.

One trade-off to keep in mind: ICWDM notes that mesh finer than 1/2 x 1/2 inch "significantly reduces air flow." A coop needs good ventilation year-round (see chicken frostbite), so if you go to 1/4-inch mesh, make the vents a bit larger to compensate.

Rodent-proofing the coop, step by step

  1. Walk the outside with a flashlight. Look for gaps at the bottom of walls, around the door, where the roof meets the walls, and around any pipe or cord. Anything wider than a pencil is a mouse door.
  2. Cover vents and windows with 1/4-inch hardware cloth, screwed down with washers or battens rather than stapled, so it can't be pushed loose.
  3. Close the gap under the door. NebGuide G1530 says keep it under 1/4 inch. A strip of sheet metal along the bottom of a wooden door stops gnawing at the edge.
  4. Stop the diggers at ground level. Rats burrow. NebGuide and ICWDM recommend foundation walls or curtain walls that reach at least 36 inches below ground for permanent buildings, and a horizontal footing extension of about 1 foot outward to turn burrowers away. OMAFRA's lighter standard is 19 inches deep with an 8-inch outward apron. For a backyard coop or run, the practical version is a skirt of 1/4-inch hardware cloth buried or laid flat around the outside edge, bent outward in an L so a digging rat hits mesh. Galvanized mesh lasts much longer than plain mesh, which NebGuide says may only last 5 to 10 years in soil.
  5. Raise the floor or seal it. Rats love to nest under structures sitting right on the ground. A coop on a concrete pad, or raised well off the ground with the underside open and easy to see, gives them nowhere to hide.
  6. Re-check every few months. NebGuide is blunt that rodent-proofing "will not guarantee rodent-free buildings." Doors warp and mesh rusts, so build a quick inspection into your routine.

If you're building a new coop, it's far easier to design this in from the start: 1/4-inch mesh everywhere, a mesh apron around the run, and a floor rodents can't get under (see building a backyard chicken coop).

Take away the food, water and cover

A sealed coop next to an open feed bag won't stay rodent-free for long. Housekeeping matters as much as hardware:

  • Store feed in metal cans with tight lids. Maryland Extension recommends lidded aluminum cans; ICWDM says metal containers with tight-fitting lids for both feed and garbage. Never leave feed in the paper bag.
  • Clean up spilled feed every day, inside the coop and under the feeder. Treat-scattering is fine, but only as much as the hens clean up quickly.
  • Pull feeders at night or use a treadle feeder, if spillage is a problem. Rats feed mostly at night.
  • Use nipple waterers instead of open pans where you can (Maryland Extension).
  • Clear a 3-foot strip around the coop. NebGuide and ICWDM both recommend a weed-free zone about 3 feet wide around buildings; OMAFRA suggests replacing the vegetation with crushed rock, which rodents can't tunnel through. Keep grass short beyond that, since rodents avoid open ground.
  • Move the junk. Lumber piles, old bedding heaps and stacked feed bags right next to the coop are rat housing. ICWDM recommends storing stacked items about 18 inches off the floor and away from walls.
  • Collect eggs at least daily, so eggs aren't sitting overnight as rat food.

If you use the deep litter method, keep the litter dry and turned. Undisturbed, packed bedding in a quiet corner is a natural nesting spot.

Sizes from Maryland Extension; range, behavior and spacing from Oklahoma State and OMAFRA (the two give slightly different numbers, so the ranges above span both). The practical lesson: with mice, put a lot of traps close to where you see signs. With rats, be patient.

Trapping safely around chickens

Traps are the first choice around poultry because they don't put poison anywhere a hen can reach it. The extension advice:

  • Set traps along walls, behind objects and in dark corners where you've seen droppings or gnawing. OMAFRA says set them at right angles to the wall with the trigger end nearest the wall.
  • Keep traps away from the birds. Maryland Extension warns not to put snap traps or glue boards anywhere pets, children or free-range poultry can reach them. Put them inside a covered box with a small entrance, under a pallet, or in a feed room the hens can't get into.
  • Pre-bait for rats. Because rats avoid new things, OMAFRA suggests leaving baited traps unset for 4 to 5 days, then setting them once the rats are feeding from them.
  • Use enough traps. One trap is rarely enough. Check traps daily and wear gloves when you handle them.

Cats help a little but don't solve a real infestation; OMAFRA says they may limit small populations but can't control heavy ones, and that ultrasonic devices "may not be effective."

Be very careful with rat poison around a flock. Rodenticides are poisonous to chickens, pets, wildlife and people, and some carry a risk of secondary poisoning to animals that eat a poisoned rodent (Oklahoma State). If you use bait at all, Maryland Extension says to put it only inside tamper-resistant bait stations, never loose, out of reach of children, pets, livestock and poultry, and to wear disposable gloves. Oklahoma State adds: use only products labeled for use in and around poultry houses and follow the label exactly. Rules for who can use which products vary by state and province, so check locally. For most backyard flocks, sealing the coop and trapping is enough, and is the safer route.

The short version

Mice need a 1/4-inch gap and rats a 1/2-inch gap, so chicken wire doesn't stop them. Cover vents and windows with 1/4-inch, 24-gauge hardware cloth, close the door gap, and run a buried or L-shaped mesh skirt around the coop and run. Keep feed in lidded metal cans, sweep up spills daily, collect eggs often and keep a clear 3-foot strip around the coop. Trap along walls, out of the hens' reach, and pre-bait for rats. Leave poison as a last resort, and only in locked bait stations.
