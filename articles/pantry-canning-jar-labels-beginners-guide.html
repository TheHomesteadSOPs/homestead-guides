How to Label Your Pantry & Canning Jars: Dates, Shelf Life & Rotation Explained

Most of the dates printed on food aren't safety dates at all — and most home canners never write a date on the jar in the first place. Both habits are easy to fix once you know what the labels are actually for.

If you haven't canned before, start with our home canning guide — this article picks up after the jars are sealed and shelved.

"Expiration date" doesn't mean what most people think

Texas A&M AgriLife Extension's food-storage guidance breaks down the four kinds of dates actually printed on packaged food, and they mean genuinely different things:

  • Expiration date: the last day the food should be eaten — discard after this date (eggs are the one common exception, which often stay safe past theirs).
  • Best if used by: a peak-quality date, not a safety date. The food may still be fine afterward if it's been stored properly and shows no signs of spoilage.
  • Sell by: a date for the store's own inventory management, not the shopper.
  • Pack date: when the food was packaged — useful for judging real age even when no other date is printed.

Utah State University Extension adds an important caveat: there's no federal standard requiring any of these labels, which is exactly why "is this safe?" and "is this just past its best?" get confused so often.

Home-canned jars play by different rules than store cans

Penn State Extension is direct about this: a home-canned jar processed with a tested, up-to-date recipe stays safe indefinitely as long as the seal is intact — but quality (firmness, color, flavor) declines well before that. Their practical guidance: plan to can only what you'll realistically eat within about a year, and treat anything older as a quality call, not a safety one, provided the seal has held.

Storage conditions actually matter here: keep jars between 40–70°F, out of direct light (a closet, a covered box, or a dark pantry corner). Avoid anywhere that gets above 90°F — near a furnace, water heater, or in direct sun — since heat is what accelerates both quality loss and seal failure.

What to actually put on the label

At minimum: the canning date and what's in the jar. Both extensions' guidance agrees on dating anything that doesn't have an obvious, printed date already (this applies to freezer containers and leftovers too, not just canning jars) — a jar of green beans looks like every other jar of green beans a year later, and "I'll remember" rarely survives past the second shelf.

FIFO: first in, first out

Both Penn State and USU Extension point to the same simple system for keeping a pantry or freezer from quietly accumulating forgotten jars: new goes to the back, older moves to the front, and the oldest or shortest-dated items get used first. Penn State specifically recommends an annual inventory pass — go through the shelves once a year, move anything old to the front, and pull anything that's spoiled or discolored enough that you genuinely won't eat it.

Real storage times, not guesses

Texas A&M AgriLife's canned-goods storage chart gives concrete numbers worth having on the pantry wall rather than memorizing:

Canned vegetables1 year—
Canned fruit12 months1 week (in a covered glass/plastic container, not the can)
Canned fish or shellfish12 months1 day
Canned juice—6 days, tightly covered
Shelf-stable canned ham2 yearsRefrigerate immediately
Canned baby food1 year2 days

For dry pantry goods generally, both extensions recommend 50–70°F, dry, dark storage, off the floor and away from the stove, dishwasher, or water heater — humidity above 60% can cause caking and staleness even in sealed packaging.

When to actually toss it

Discard any can or jar that's swollen, badly dented (especially along a seam), rusted, or leaking — no date math needed at that point. For home-canned jars specifically, a broken seal, visible mold, cloudy liquid, or an off smell on opening means the jar goes in the trash, not the taste test, regardless of how recently it was canned.
