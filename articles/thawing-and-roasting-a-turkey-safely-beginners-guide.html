Thawing and Roasting a Turkey Safely: A Beginner's Guide to Thaw Times, Roasting Charts & the 165°F Rule

The most common holiday turkey mistake happens days before the oven goes on: starting to thaw too late. A big frozen bird can need almost a week in the refrigerator. Whether you bought it or raised it yourself, here are the USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) and extension timetables for thawing, roasting, checking doneness and handling leftovers.

How big a turkey, and when to buy it

  • Allow 1 pound per person, whether fresh or frozen (FSIS; University of Tennessee Extension). Buy a bit bigger if you want leftovers.
  • Fresh: buy it only 1 to 2 days before cooking, and don't buy a fresh pre-stuffed turkey (FSIS).
  • Frozen: keep it frozen until it's time to thaw. FSIS says a frozen turkey stays safe indefinitely in the freezer but is best cooked within a year.

Three safe ways to thaw (and one to skip)

University of Georgia Extension says never to thaw a turkey on the counter: bacteria multiply rapidly at room temperature, even while the center is still frozen. Use one of these instead.

1. In the refrigerator (easiest, needs the most time)

Allow about 24 hours for every 4 to 5 pounds in a refrigerator at 40°F or colder, with the bird in its wrapper on a tray or pan to catch drips (FSIS; UGA Extension). Once thawed, it keeps 1 to 2 more days in the fridge, and FSIS says a refrigerator-thawed turkey can be refrozen if plans change.

Turkey weightRefrigerator thawing time
4–12 lb1–3 days
12–16 lb3–4 days
16–20 lb4–5 days
20–24 lb5–6 days

Count backward from the meal: a 20-pound turkey needs up to 5 days, so for a Thursday dinner it should go into the fridge by the Saturday before. Since a thawed bird keeps 1 to 2 days, starting a day early is safer than starting late.

2. In cold water (faster, more hands-on)

Submerge the turkey in its airtight wrapper or a leak-proof bag in cold tap water, allowing about 30 minutes per pound, and change the water every 30 minutes so it stays cold (FSIS; UGA Extension; University of Tennessee Extension). Cook it immediately once thawed; FSIS says not to refreeze a cold-water-thawed turkey before cooking.

Turkey weightCold-water thawing time
4–12 lb2–6 hours
12–16 lb6–8 hours
16–20 lb8–10 hours
20–24 lb10–12 hours

3. In the microwave (small birds only)

Check that the turkey fits and follow the oven maker's directions for defrosting by weight; UGA Extension says to generally allow about 6 minutes per pound, rotating and flipping it several times. Remove all the wrapping first, and cook it immediately afterward, because some areas may have started to cook (FSIS; UGA).

Out of time? Cook it frozen

FSIS says a turkey can go into the oven frozen. It's safe, but roasting takes at least 50 percent longer than for a thawed bird. Pull the giblet package out with tongs partway through cooking, once it loosens.

Before it goes in the oven

  • Remove the giblets from the body and neck cavities of a thawed turkey and cook them separately (UGA Extension; FSIS).
  • Wash your hands, the sink and anything else that touched the raw turkey or its juices with hot, soapy water (FSIS).
  • Use a shallow roasting pan with a rack, breast side up, and an oven set no lower than 325°F (FSIS; UGA; University of Tennessee).

Is it done? Use a thermometer

  • 165°F is the U.S. safe minimum for the whole turkey (FSIS; UGA). Check three places: the innermost part of the thigh, the innermost part of the wing, and the thickest part of the breast, without touching bone.
  • Don't trust the pop-up timer alone. UGA Extension and FSIS both say to confirm with a food thermometer.
  • Pink isn't proof of anything. UGA notes that turkey can still look pink once every part has reached 165°F and is safe; color doesn't tell you either way.
  • Let it rest 20 minutes before carving or removing stuffing (FSIS; UGA; University of Tennessee).
In Canada the number is different. Health Canada's chart lists 82°C (180°F) for whole poultry, including turkey, and 74°C (165°F) for poultry pieces, stuffing and leftovers. U.S. agencies use 165°F for the whole bird. Use the guidance where you live, and see our safe internal temperatures guide for other meats.

Stuffing: safest outside the bird

FSIS and UGA Extension both recommend cooking stuffing in a separate casserole dish for safety and even doneness. If you do stuff the turkey, FSIS says to keep wet and dry ingredients apart and chilled until just before filling, stuff it loosely right before it goes in the oven, and cook it immediately. The center of the stuffing must reach 165°F, and it all comes out of the bird after cooking.

Leftovers

  • Refrigerate within 2 hours of cooking (within 1 hour if it's above 90°F), carved off the bone into shallow containers, with stuffing and gravy stored separately (UGA Extension; FSIS).
  • Use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Frozen leftovers are best within 2 to 6 months (FSIS; UGA; University of Tennessee).
  • Reheat to 165°F (FSIS; UGA). UGA notes it's safe to refreeze leftover turkey even if the bird was bought frozen.
  • Got a carcass? Simmer it for stock and keep it in the freezer, or follow tested directions for pressure canning broth.

The short version

Plan 1 pound of turkey per person. Thaw in the fridge at about 24 hours per 4 to 5 pounds (up to 6 days for a 24-pound bird), or in cold water at about 30 minutes per pound with fresh water every half hour, and never on the counter. Roast at 325°F using the chart as a guide, cook stuffing separately, and take it out when the thigh, wing and breast all read 165°F (82°C for a whole bird under Health Canada's guidance). Rest 20 minutes, and get leftovers into the fridge within 2 hours.
