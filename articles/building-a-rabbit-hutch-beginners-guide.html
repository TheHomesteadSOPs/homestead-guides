Building a Rabbit Hutch: A Beginner's Guide to Size, Wire Mesh & Predator-Proofing

Most rabbit hutch plans online are sized by eye, not by the rabbit. Washington State University Extension's rabbit-housing guide gives real floor-space numbers by breed size, a wire-mesh rule that surprises a lot of first-time builders, and the siting and ventilation details that decide whether a hutch is comfortable or a health risk in July and January alike.

Already decided you're raising rabbits for meat and just need the housing to go with it? Our raising backyard meat rabbits guide covers breed choice, feeding and breeding. This article is just the hutch.

How much space a rabbit actually needs

WSU Extension's housing guide sizes floor space by the rabbit's adult breed size, not a single one-size-fits-all cage:

Breed sizeFloor spaceTypical dimensions
Small breedsAbout 6¼ sq ft2½ × 2½ ft
Medium breedsAbout 7½ sq ft2½ × 3 ft
Large breeds10 sq ft2½ × 4 ft

Depth usually runs about 30 inches, though a well-designed cage can go to 36 inches without making it hard to reach the back corner. Height of 18 to 24 inches is enough for every breed except giants. Size the door large enough to handle a rabbit comfortably or to slide a nest box in and out — a door that's an afterthought is the detail beginners most often regret once the hutch is built.

The wire mesh rule most beginners get backwards

It's tempting to build the whole hutch out of hardware cloth, since it's cheap and sold everywhere. WSU's guide specifically advises against it for flooring: hardware cloth is a woven surface, manure clings to it, and its galvanized edges can irritate a rabbit's feet. For the floor specifically:

  • Small and medium breeds: ½" × 1" welded wire floor mesh, which stays nearly self-cleaning as droppings fall through.
  • Large breeds: ¾" × 1" welded wire floors, which stay cleaner under more weight.
  • Gauge: 14-gauge wire for floors. The guide's own diagrams call for 16-gauge mesh on hutch tops and 14-gauge mesh on the sides.

Welded wire, not woven hardware cloth, is the detail worth spending a little more on — it's the difference between a hutch that's genuinely low-maintenance and one that needs scrubbing every week.

Siting, shade, wind & ventilation

WSU's guide treats airflow as a real design problem, not an afterthought:

  • Site selection: good drainage, natural shade where it's available, and protection from prevailing winds.
  • Roof overhang: enough to shield the hutch from driving rain, with the back and windward sides built solid rather than open wire.
  • Ventilation: "a rabbitry should have plenty of fresh air, but no draft," since drafts are linked to colds and pneumonia in rabbits. The fix is continuous vents under the eaves and along the lower side walls, below the cage floor, with adjustable dampers — open in summer, partly closed in winter.
  • Moisture control: small adjustable ridge ventilators release excess moisture, and an insulated roof cuts both summer heat buildup and winter condensation.

Predator-proofing basics

WSU's guide is blunt about the stakes: a frightened rabbit can injure itself or kill its own young, so a calm, genuinely secure hutch matters as much for welfare as for preventing loss. Its own recommendation is to fence the rabbit area so stray animals can't reach the hutches in the first place, on top of the solid back/side walls already built in for weather. Beyond that fencing, three build details are standard practice worth building in from the start, not added later: raise the hutch on legs well off the ground rather than setting it directly on soil, fit doors with a latch a raccoon can't flip open with a paw, and use the same welded wire — never thin chicken wire — on any open side, not just the floor.

The short version

Size the hutch to the breed (6¼–10 sq ft of floor space depending on size), floor it in ½"×1" or ¾"×1" welded wire rather than hardware cloth, site it with shade, wind protection and a real roof overhang, and build in continuous low vents with no direct draft. Fence the area, raise the hutch off the ground, and use a latch nothing can flip open. Get those right and the hutch takes care of itself for years.
