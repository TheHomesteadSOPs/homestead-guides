Feeding Honey Bees: A Beginner's Guide to Sugar Syrup Ratios, Feeders, Fondant & Candy Boards

A colony that goes into winter light on honey can starve in February with the cluster inches away from food. Feeding sugar is the fix, but the recipe changes with the season: thin syrup in spring, thick syrup in fall, and solid sugar once it's too cold for bees to take liquid. Here's how Alabama, Arkansas and Utah State extension specialists say to do each one.

When a hive actually needs feeding

  • A new colony, right away. Alabama Cooperative Extension (UNP-2206) says to give a newly installed package or nuc light syrup immediately, and again during a summer nectar dearth. A package arrives with no comb and no stored food (see package bees vs. nucs).
  • Early spring. Established colonies can get light syrup early in spring to boost brood rearing, or after the honey harvest if you took a lot.
  • Fall, if the hive is light. Alabama Extension says to check stores in late October and feed heavy syrup, up to about four gallons per colony, so the hive reaches roughly 60 pounds of honey, which it describes as about one medium super's worth.
  • Late winter, if a heft check says so. Alabama Extension reassesses in February and adds solid sugar on top if the colony is short. Arkansas's beekeeping course (UADA) recommends tilting the hive back on its bottom board now and then over winter; if it feels light, "emergency feeding is probably in order."

Equally important is when not to feed. Both Alabama and Arkansas say to take all feeders off while honey supers are on, so sugar syrup doesn't end up in the honey you harvest. Alabama also suggests marking any frames that hold syrup stores so they aren't extracted. And if a feeder sits untouched for a week, Alabama Extension says the bees have a better food source; take it off until the season changes. As UADA puts it, "If the bees don't need it, they won't take it."

Light vs. heavy syrup

Light syrup (1:1)Heavy syrup (2:1)
Recipe by weight1 lb sugar to 1 lb (2 cups) water2 lb sugar to 1 lb (2 cups) water
WhenSpring and summer; new colonies; dearthFall, before cold weather
What it doesStimulates comb building and brood rearingStored more efficiently as winter food

Ratios and uses from Alabama Extension UNP-2206 and the UADA Beekeeping Short Course. Both ratios are by weight. If you don't have a scale, UADA notes a cup of white sugar weighs about 8.8 ounces and a cup of water 8 ounces, so measuring by volume is "generally close enough."

How to mix sugar syrup

  1. Use plain white table sugar. Cane or beet sugar are both fine (Alabama Extension). Alabama also lists high-fructose corn syrup as acceptable.
  2. Heat the water, then stir in the sugar. Alabama Extension says to mix the sugar into warm water and never boil the mixture. UADA's method brings the water to a gentle boil, turns it down to a simmer, then adds the sugar and stirs until it dissolves. Both agree the goal is fully dissolved sugar without cooking it.
  3. Let it cool. UADA: "Never feed hot syrup to bees." Add anything else only once the syrup is at room temperature.
  4. Optional: a splash of acid. UADA says about 1 teaspoon of white vinegar, apple cider vinegar or lemon juice per gallon helps reduce mold. Wash feeders between batches, and mix only what the bees will take in a few days; Alabama notes large volumes are more likely to ferment.
Never feed these: brown sugar, molasses, raw or unrefined sugar, organic sugar, confectioners' (powdered) sugar and artificial sweeteners (Alabama Extension; UADA). And never feed honey from an unknown source: UADA warns it can carry American foulbrood spores into your hive.

Alabama Extension's rule of thumb: feed liquid when it's above 50°F and switch to solid sugar below 50°F. UADA recommends feeders inside the hive whenever daytime highs stay below 55°F. In both cases, keep feeders enclosed so bees from other colonies can't reach them.

Solid sugar for winter: three options

Once it's too cold for bees to break cluster and take syrup, solid sugar goes directly on top of the frames, above the cluster, where the bees can reach it without leaving warmth. You'll usually need a feeding shim or an empty box around it to make room. Alabama Extension says to check and replenish solid feed every two weeks or so and remove it once nectar starts blooming. Our winterizing guide covers heft checks and moisture; here's how to make the sugar itself.

1. Mountain camp (dry sugar)

The quickest emergency method. Alabama Extension lays one sheet of newspaper over the top bars and pours up to 4 pounds of granulated sugar on it, misting it with just enough water to moisten. UADA describes the same idea: dry sugar on the inner cover, or on newspaper on the top bars with an empty super around it.

2. No-cook candy board

UADA's recipe: 5 pounds of sugar, 5 ounces of cold water and 1 tablespoon of white or apple cider vinegar, mixed to a "wet sand" texture, packed into a frame-sized mold and left to dry for 1 to 3 days. It goes on top of the hive sugar side down. UADA notes the bees won't build burr comb in that space over winter, and you can mix pollen or protein into the candy or set a protein patty into the mold.

3. Cooked candy board or fondant

This is candy making, and it uses the same soft-ball sugar stage as fudge.

  • UADA candy board: boil 1 pint of water, add 5 pounds of sugar and heat to 240°F, stirring constantly so it doesn't caramelize. Cool to 180–200°F, then pour into a mold about 16¼ × 19⅞ inches and ¾ inch deep.
  • USU fondant patties (makes three 1½-pound patties): dissolve 4 pounds of sugar in 16 ounces of simmering water, then boil without stirring to 235–240°F. Utah State notes that at Wasatch Front elevations that drops to 224–230°F, because water boils at a lower temperature at altitude. Cool to about 210°F, beat it at 200–210°F, then shape patties thin enough that the lid still closes. USU says patties that reach the edges of the box are unlikely to be eaten, so keep them centered over the cluster.
Don't use bakery fondant. Alabama Extension warns that baker's fondant is not the same as beekeeping fondant and shouldn't be fed to bees. Cooked sugar at 240°F also sticks to skin and burns badly (UADA), so keep kids and pets out of the kitchen and pour carefully.

Pollen patties: protein, with a catch

Sugar is carbohydrate only. Brood also needs protein, which bees normally get from pollen. Utah State's pollen patty is ½ cup boiling water, ½ cup sugar and 1 cup of pollen or pollen substitute, mixed to a "wet peanut butter" consistency and set under the top board, centered and touching the frames. USU uses patties through winter into late spring to support brood. UADA adds two cautions: feeding protein early can trigger early buildup and early swarming, and small hive beetles are attracted to protein, so keep an eye on uneaten patties once the weather warms.

Feeding stimulants and medications

Products like Honey B Healthy blend essential oils (lemongrass and spearmint) into syrup to encourage bees to feed; USU even gives a homemade version. UADA points out these stimulants are not labeled as medications. Actual medications, such as fumagillin for Nosema, should be given exactly per the label and finished at least 4 weeks before the first honey super goes on (UADA).

The short version

Feed new colonies 1:1 syrup right away, feed light hives 2:1 syrup in fall until they're near 60 pounds of honey, and switch to solid sugar (mountain camp, a candy board or fondant) once it's below about 50°F. Use plain white sugar only, never brown sugar, powdered sugar or unknown honey, and keep feeders inside the hive to prevent robbing. Pull every feeder when the honey supers go on, and heft-check through winter so you can catch a light hive before February.
