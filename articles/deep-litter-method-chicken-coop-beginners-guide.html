The Deep Litter Method for Chickens: A Beginner's Guide to Bedding Depth, Moisture & Ammonia

Scraping out a chicken coop every week gets old fast, especially in January. The deep litter method lets you leave the bedding in, keep adding to it, and clean out only a few times a year. The trade-off: it only works if you manage moisture carefully. Get it right and the coop smells like a forest floor and you end the season with a head start on garden compost. Get it wrong and you have a wet, ammonia-heavy floor that's worse for your hens than weekly cleaning ever was.

New to chickens? Start with our backyard flock guide for the basics. This article goes deep on one job every coop owner faces weekly: what to do with the bedding.

What the deep litter method actually is

Most coop-cleaning advice says to scoop out soiled bedding every week and put fresh material down. The deep litter method does the opposite. You leave the old bedding in place, stir it, and keep adding fresh bedding on top. The droppings get worked into a thick, slowly composting layer instead of sitting on the surface. University of Delaware Cooperative Extension sums it up: deep litter "can work if managed well and only needs cleaning out a few times per year." The key phrase is if managed well. Neglected deep litter is just a dirty coop with more bedding in it.

Done right, you get three payoffs:

  • Less work: a few full clean-outs a year instead of one every week.
  • Garden compost: what you finally shovel out is already partly broken down, which makes it a head start for the compost pile.
  • A healthier floor, maybe: University of Tennessee Extension notes that built-up litter can support beneficial bacteria that help keep pathogens in check. That research comes from commercial poultry houses, so treat it as a possible bonus for a backyard coop, not a guarantee.

How deep to build it, and with what

UF/IFAS Extension (Baker County) gives a simple start-to-finish plan for composting litter inside the coop: put down 4 inches of litter, then add bedding over time until it's 12 inches deep, stirring and turning it regularly so it doesn't clump. Don't start with a foot of bedding on day one. Build up to it as the droppings build up.

Good bedding materials named by UF/IFAS and the University of Delaware include:

  • Pine shavings: the most common choice. Absorbent, easy to stir, and widely available in compressed bales.
  • Chopped straw: cheap and easy to find. Chopped works better than long straw, which mats down and is harder to turn.
  • Dry leaves, pine needles or pine bark, wood chips: useful free or low-cost additions, especially mixed in with shavings.
  • Other options UF/IFAS lists: pine pellets or sawdust, paper by-products, sand, and crop by-products.

Deep litter works best on a solid floor: dirt, wood or concrete. A wire floor can't hold it. It also needs room. The University of Delaware's floor-space guideline is at least 2 to 3 square feet per chicken inside the coop, plus 8 to 10 square feet per bird in the run. A crowded coop makes more droppings than the litter can absorb. Not sure your coop is big enough? Try our free chicken coop size calculator.

The squeeze test: moisture is everything

Deep litter lives or dies on moisture. Too wet, and it turns into a smelly, matted cake. Too dry, and it gets dusty. University of Tennessee Extension's poultry litter publication puts the ideal range at 20 to 25 percent moisture, and you can check it by hand:

  • Grab a handful from a few inches down and squeeze it.
  • Too wet: it forms a tight ball that holds its shape. Anything above about 25 percent moisture counts as wet litter.
  • Too dry: it won't clump at all and just falls apart.
  • About right: it holds together loosely, then crumbles apart.

Wet litter forms a crust (a "cake") on the surface that seals the moisture in and keeps it from drying out. That crust is linked to footpad sores and other welfare problems. UT's advice for commercial houses applies just as well to a backyard coop: once caking starts it's very hard to fix, so prevent it. When a waterer leaks or spills, remove the wet bedding right away and replace it with clean, dry material. Don't just cover it up.

The most common wet spots are under the waterer and under the roost, where droppings pile up overnight. Check those two places first every time you're in the coop. Raising the waterer onto a block, or moving it outside to the run, fixes a lot of deep-litter problems on its own.

Your nose is the ammonia meter

Ammonia comes off wet, manure-heavy litter, and it's the clearest sign the system is failing. A University of Georgia poultry science tip sheet gives the numbers that matter: people can usually smell ammonia at about 20 to 25 ppm, and it has been suggested that coop ammonia should not exceed 25 ppm. Long-term exposure even around 20 ppm can harm birds' health and performance, and higher levels irritate their eyes and airways. The practical takeaway: if you can smell ammonia when you open the coop door, you've already reached the recommended limit. Healthy deep litter should smell earthy, like a forest floor, not like a litter box.

When you notice the smell:

  • Turn the litter with a pitchfork or garden fork to dry it out and break up any crust.
  • Add a fresh layer of dry bedding on top.
  • Remove any wet or caked patches entirely.
  • Check ventilation. The University of Delaware recommends a coop that is "well-ventilated but draft-free." Moisture from the litter and from the birds' breath has to leave through vents high on the walls, away from where the birds roost.

That last point matters most in winter, when the urge to close everything up is strongest. As our guide to keeping chickens laying through winter explains, humid coop air raises frostbite risk. A sealed coop with deep litter is a moisture trap.

A simple routine that keeps it working

  • Every visit: look and sniff. Check under the waterer and the roost for wet spots.
  • Weekly, or whenever it looks packed: stir and turn the litter. Toss a handful of scratch grains on top and the hens will do much of the turning for you.
  • As needed: add a thin layer of fresh bedding over the top to cover fresh droppings and absorb moisture.
  • Always: remove wet patches right away rather than burying them.
  • A few times a year: do a full clean-out. A common rhythm is to build the litter up through fall and winter, then clean out in spring once the ground thaws and the compost pile and garden are ready for it.

Keep the nest boxes separate from all of this. They should stay clean and freshly bedded, with roughly one box per 3 to 4 hens per the University of Delaware, so eggs come out clean.

Turning spent litter into garden compost

Deep litter is a compost head start, not finished compost, and chicken manure needs careful handling. University of Georgia Extension notes that chicken manure and litter can carry E. coli, Salmonella and other pathogens. Their guidance for the vegetable garden:

  • Compost it properly first. Raw poultry litter should heat to about 140°F within five days and hold that temperature for 7 to 21 days. Turn the pile regularly. UF/IFAS suggests composting outside the coop in a contained area, ideally on an impervious surface.
  • Never put raw manure on growing plants. UGA is explicit: never use raw manure as a side-dressing.
  • Respect the waiting periods if any manure goes on beds uncomposted: at least 90 days before harvest for crops that don't touch the soil (trellised tomatoes, peppers) and at least 120 days for crops that do (lettuce, carrots, strawberries).
  • Don't overdo it. UGA suggests about 50 pounds of poultry manure per 1,000 square feet of garden. UF/IFAS suggests finished compost on a lawn at about 1/4 inch thick.

For building and managing the pile itself, see our backyard composting guide.

The short version

Start with 4 inches of pine shavings or chopped straw on a solid floor, and build up to about 12 inches as the season goes on. Stir it, top it up, and pull out wet patches the moment you find them. Keep it moist but not wet: it should crumble after you squeeze it, not hold a ball. If you can smell ammonia, it has already gone too far, so turn it, add bedding, and check your ventilation. Clean out a few times a year, compost the litter hot before it goes near the vegetable garden, and you'll get easier chores and better soil.
