Power Outage Food Safety: A Beginner's Guide to What to Keep, What to Toss & How Long the Freezer Lasts

When the power goes out in a winter storm, a homestead freezer can hold hundreds of dollars of meat, vegetables and berries you put up all season. The rules for saving it are simple, and they come down to two numbers: 40°F and 2 hours. Here is what U.S. food-safety agencies and university extension services say to do before, during and after an outage.

How long food stays cold without power

The single most useful thing you can do once the lights go out is keep the doors shut. Every opening lets cold air out.

Appliance (door kept closed)About how long it stays safe
RefrigeratorAbout 4 hours
Full freezerAbout 48 hours
Half-full freezerAbout 24 hours
Fully stocked 18 cu ft freezer with 50 lb of dry iceAbout 2 days

Times are from FoodSafety.gov, University of Connecticut Extension and University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension. A full freezer lasts twice as long as a half-full one, which is one more reason a well-stocked homestead freezer is worth keeping full. If you know the outage will be long, UW–Madison Extension suggests getting dry ice or block ice.

Before the storm: five things to do now

University of Connecticut Extension (in guidance credited to Clemson Extension and USDA) recommends these steps ahead of time:

  • Put an appliance thermometer in both the fridge and the freezer. Safe temperatures are 40°F or lower in the refrigerator and 0°F or lower in the freezer. After an outage, the thermometer reading is what tells you whether food is still safe, so it's the most important tool on this list.
  • Freeze water in one-quart plastic bags or small containers. Don't fill them too full, because water expands as it freezes. The extra ice helps keep the freezer, fridge or a cooler cold.
  • Freeze refrigerated items you won't need right away, such as leftovers, milk, and fresh meat and poultry.
  • Know where you can get dry ice or block ice before you need it.
  • Have coolers on hand to keep refrigerator food cold if the power will be out for more than 4 hours, and group foods together in the freezer so they keep each other cold longer.
Running a generator to save the freezer? The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission says to run a portable generator outside only, at least 20 feet from the house, with the exhaust pointed away from doors and windows. Never run one inside a home, garage, basement, crawlspace or shed, or on a porch or in a carport; opening doors or windows does not give enough ventilation. Put battery-powered (or battery-backup) CO alarms on every level of the home. Our family emergency binder guide covers storm prep beyond the kitchen.

Can you just put food out in the snow?

It's tempting in a winter outage, but University of Connecticut Extension and NC State Extension both advise against setting food outside in ice or snow. Sun can warm it to an unsafe temperature even on a cold day, and wild or neighborhood animals may find it first.

University of Maine Extension offers a more careful alternative: when outdoor temperatures stay at 40°F or below, food can go outside in a clean, insulated cooler, which is more sanitary than putting it directly into the snow. If you try this, put a thermometer in the cooler and keep checking it, because daytime sun can change things quickly.

After the outage: the 40°F, 2-hour rule

Once the power is back, check the thermometers first. If you don't have one, check each package; UW–Madison Extension notes you can't rely on how food looks or smells.

  • Discard perishable food that has been above 40°F for 2 hours or more, such as meat, poultry, seafood, eggs and leftovers (UConn; UW–Madison Extension).
  • Frozen food can be refrozen if it still contains ice crystals or is at 40°F or below, though quality may suffer (FoodSafety.gov; UW–Madison).
  • Throw out anything with an unusual odor, color or texture, or that feels warm to the touch (UConn).
  • Never taste food to decide if it's safe, and when in doubt, throw it out (FoodSafety.gov; UConn).

UW–Madison Extension adds that cooking doesn't rescue food that got too warm: meat, poultry, fish, eggs and milk that weren't kept cold enough may cause illness even when they are thoroughly cooked.

Refrigerator food: keep or toss

FoodSafety.gov's chart for refrigerated food held above 40°F for more than 2 hours, condensed:

Raw or cooked meat, poultry, fish and seafood; lunchmeat, hot dogs, bacon, sausageDiscard
Casseroles, soups, stews, gravy, stuffing, broth, pizza, cooked pasta, rice and potatoesDiscard
Milk, cream, sour cream, buttermilk, yogurt, soy milkDiscard
Soft cheeses (cream, cottage, ricotta, mozzarella, Brie, queso fresco), shredded and low-fat cheesesDiscard
Hard cheeses (cheddar, Swiss, Parmesan, provolone), processed cheeseKeep
Butter and margarineKeep
Fresh eggs, hard-cooked eggs, egg dishes, custards, puddings, quicheDiscard
Cut fresh fruit; cut or cooked vegetables; pre-cut packaged greensDiscard
Uncut fresh fruit and vegetables; fresh mushrooms and herbsKeep
Opened mayonnaise, tartar sauce, horseradish, creamy dressings, spaghetti sauceDiscard
Jelly, mustard, ketchup, relish, pickles, olives, peanut butter, soy and barbecue sauces, vinegar-based dressingsKeep
Bread, rolls, muffins, tortillas, fruit piesKeep

One exception worth knowing: FoodSafety.gov lists opened mayonnaise, tartar sauce and horseradish as a discard only if they were above 50°F for more than 8 hours. Commercial garlic in oil is always a discard.

Refrozen food is safe but may lose quality, so use it first. If you put up a lot of produce, our blanching and freezing guide and canning vs. freezing vs. drying guide explain why spreading your harvest across shelf-stable methods means an outage can't take all of it.

The short version

Keep appliance thermometers in the fridge (40°F or lower) and freezer (0°F or lower), freeze bags of water, and keep coolers ready. When the power goes out, keep the doors closed: the fridge holds about 4 hours, a full freezer about 48 hours and a half-full one about 24. Skip storing food loose in the snow. Afterward, discard perishables that sat above 40°F for 2 hours or more, refreeze anything that still has ice crystals or is at 40°F or below, never taste to check, and when in doubt, throw it out.
