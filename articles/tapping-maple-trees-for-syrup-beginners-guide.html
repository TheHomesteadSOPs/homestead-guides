Tapping Maple Trees for Syrup: A Beginner's Guide to Tree Size, Tapholes, Sap Storage & Boiling to 219°F

A few big maples in the yard are enough for a season of homemade syrup. Sap runs for a few weeks in late winter, and the time to get ready is now: pick and mark your trees while the leaves are still on the ground and you can tell a sugar maple from an ash, and order spouts before the rush. Here's what extension foresters in New Hampshire, Minnesota, New York and Utah say about which trees to tap, how many taps a trunk can take, keeping sap from spoiling and knowing exactly when the syrup is done.

Which trees to tap

All maples make sweet sap. Sugar maple is the one every source prefers, because its sap is sweetest and it leafs out later, which gives a longer season. Sap averages about 2 percent sugar, according to UNH, UMN and Cornell extension. The University of Minnesota lists four tappable species there: sugar, red, silver and boxelder. Cornell adds black maple, and Utah State University has published a guide to making syrup from boxelder and Norway maples, so a yard without sugar maples can still make syrup, just from more sap per gallon.

  • Sugar maple: the best choice. Rutgers Master Gardeners note its buds are brown and pointed.
  • Red and silver maple: fine for a backyard batch, but they bud out earlier, which cuts the season short. Rutgers suggests tapping red maples first for that reason. Red maple buds are rounded and red.
  • Boxelder and Norway maple: usable, with lower-sugar sap (USU).

Identify and flag your trees in fall. In February, a bare maple, ash and oak can be hard to tell apart.

When to tap

Sap flows when freezing nights are followed by thawing days. UNH and Rutgers both describe the ideal as nights in the 20s and days in the 40s. A season usually brings only 8 to 10 good runs (UNH), so being ready on time matters.

Region (source)Typical tapping time
Finger Lakes, New York (Cornell)About February 10 to March 10
Central and southern Minnesota (UMN)Taps in by mid-February
Northern Minnesota (UMN)Taps in by the second week of March
Logan, Utah (USU)Starting mid to late February

Tapping weeks too early lets the holes dry out and seal before the big runs, Cornell warns. Tapping late means missing early runs. Watch your local forecast for the first stretch of freeze-thaw weather.

How many taps a tree can take

Measure trunk diameter at about 4 to 4.5 feet off the ground. (Diameter is circumference divided by 3.14; a 38-inch circumference is about a 12-inch trunk.) The sources don't agree exactly:

UMN also uses a 10-inch minimum and caps trees over 20 inches at two taps. For trees you want to keep healthy for decades, the conservative column is the safer pick.

Drilling the taphole

  • Spout size: UNH prefers small 5/16-inch spouts, which leave a smaller wound. The traditional 7/16-inch spout is still common with buckets. Use a drill bit the same size as the spout.
  • Depth: no deeper than about 1½ inches for a 5/16-inch spout and 2½ inches for a 7/16-inch spout (UNH). UMN uses 2 inches.
  • Angle: slightly upward so sap drains out of the hole (UNH, UMN, Cornell).
  • Height: 2 to 4 feet off the ground in sound wood (UMN), at a comfortable working height that won't be buried by snow (USU).
  • Wood check: the shavings should be light and clean. UNH says tap only into white, clean wood; brown or dark shavings mean you hit old stain, so move.
  • Spacing: keep new holes at least 6 inches to the side of and 2 feet above or below old tapholes (UNH). Never re-use an old hole.
  • Seating the spout: tap it in gently until it is snug. Pounding too hard can split the bark and wood.

Hang a covered, food-grade bucket or sap bag. A lid keeps out rain, snow and debris, which USU notes also means less straining later.

How much syrup to expect

UNH estimates about 1 gallon of sap per tap per run and 8 to 10 gallons per tap per season; UMN says 10 to 12 gallons. At about 40 gallons of sap per gallon of syrup (USU, OSU), one tap typically makes roughly a quart of syrup in a season, and five or six taps make somewhere around one to one and a half gallons.

The Rule of 86. UNH explains how to work out your own ratio: divide 86 by your sap's sugar percentage (measured with a sap hydrometer or refractometer). At 2 percent sugar you need 43 gallons of sap per gallon of syrup; at 2.5 percent, about 34; at 3 percent, about 29.

Collecting and storing sap

Sap is mostly water with a little sugar, and it spoils like milk. UNH says to keep it below 40°F and warns that above 50°F it spoils fast. Oregon State says to store it at 38°F or colder and use it within 7 days; UMN says that with cold temperatures and good storage, 1 to 2 days costs little quality.

  • Collect daily during good runs.
  • Store it in clean, food-grade containers in a snowbank, a cold garage or the fridge, out of the sun.
  • Boil as soon as you can. Warm, old sap makes darker, off-flavored syrup (UMN).
  • Throw out sap that turns cloudy or yellow (UNH, USU).

Boiling sap into syrup

  1. Boil outside. Forty gallons of water have to go somewhere. UMN advises boiling outdoors or in a very well-ventilated space so the large amount of steam can escape; boiling down a full batch on a kitchen stove leaves the house dripping with condensation. Use a wide, shallow pan with high sides over a propane burner or wood fire. Check local fire rules before burning outside (UNH).
  2. Keep it shallow. UNH says to keep the sap less than 2 inches deep and boil hard, adding sap as it boils down. A shallow pan boils faster than a deep pot.
  3. Control foam. Skim it (UMN), or add a drop or two of vegetable oil or a commercial defoamer (UNH).
  4. Move indoors to finish. When the liquid turns golden and approaches 217 to 218°F, UNH and USU suggest filtering it and finishing in a smaller pot on a burner you can control. Near the end, syrup can scorch or boil over in seconds.
  5. Finish at 7°F above boiling water. Syrup is done at about 7°F above the temperature water boils at on that day and at your elevation, which is about 219°F at sea level (UMN, Cornell, OSU; UNH uses 7.5°F). USU notes water's boiling point changes daily with barometric pressure, so check your thermometer in plain boiling water each day you finish syrup.
  6. Check the density if you can. Finished syrup is about 66 to 67 percent sugar. UNH's standard is 67° Brix; under that it can ferment or mold, and over that it forms crystals in the jar. A syrup hydrometer or refractometer is the precise way, but UNH notes temperature alone works fine for most backyard batches.

Filtering and bottling

Hot syrup carries "sugar sand" (niter), gritty mineral deposits that make it cloudy. Filter it while hot through a wool, Orlon or felt syrup filter, ideally with a paper pre-filter (UNH, UMN). Cornell notes a coffee filter works for small batches, though it's slow.

Bottle hot in clean, sterilized jars. The recommended temperatures vary a little: about 180°F (Cornell), 185 to 190°F (UNH), and above 185°F with 190°F preferred (UMN). Aim for 185 to 190°F. Cap right away and lay the jars on their side so the hot syrup sterilizes the lid (UMN, UNH). Properly packed syrup keeps for years unopened (USU). Refrigerate it after opening; USU adds that syrup can also be frozen for long-term storage.

When the season ends

The season ends when nights stay above freezing or the trees begin to bud. Once buds swell, sap chemistry changes and makes "buddy" syrup with an off flavor (USU, Rutgers). When a batch tastes off, pull the spouts. Oregon State says to tap each tree only once per season and remove spouts in spring so the bark can heal. UNH also advises against putting sanitizer in tapholes.

The short version

Flag sugar maples (or red, silver or boxelder) at least 10 to 12 inches across this fall. Tap in February or early March when nights hit the 20s and days the 40s, with one tap per tree under 18 inches and no more than two or three above that. Drill a slightly upward 5/16-inch hole about 1½ inches deep into clean white wood. Keep sap below 40°F and boil within a few days, outdoors, in a shallow pan. Finish at 7°F above boiling water (about 219°F at sea level), filter hot, and bottle at 185 to 190°F. Expect about 40 gallons of sap per gallon of syrup.
