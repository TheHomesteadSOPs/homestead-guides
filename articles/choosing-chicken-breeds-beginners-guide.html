Choosing Chicken Breeds: A Beginner's Guide to Egg Layers, Dual-Purpose Birds, Sex-Links & Ordering Chicks

Hatchery catalogs list dozens of breeds, and the photos all look good. The choice gets much simpler once you decide what the flock is for: the most eggs, eggs plus a calm, hardy bird that can raise its own chicks, meat, or a little of everything. Here's how extension poultry specialists at Kentucky, Maine, Ohio State, Mississippi State and NC State sort the breeds, and what to know before you place an order.

Start with what you want from the flock

Mississippi State's advice is to pick the trait that matters most to you first, then find a hatchery that carries it. Extension guides generally sort chickens into three working groups, plus birds kept mostly for looks:

  • Egg breeds are small-bodied birds bred to lay. Ohio State names Ancona, Leghorn and Minorca among the best layers.
  • Dual-purpose breeds such as Plymouth Rock, Rhode Island Red, New Hampshire and Wyandotte lay fewer eggs but are heavier and meatier, which Mississippi State describes as "much better meatiness" than egg breeds.
  • Meat birds, mostly the Cornish Cross hybrid, grow fast but are not efficient layers (University of Kentucky).
  • Exhibition and bantam breeds are kept for shows, small spaces and personality. UMaine notes bantams are 25–30 percent of standard size, and that three bantam eggs equal about two large eggs.

Egg layers: hybrids vs. heritage breeds

For the most eggs, hatcheries sell production hybrids (crosses bred by commercial breeding companies). UMaine lists brown-egg crosses such as ISA Brown, Golden Comet, Red Star and Cinnamon Queen, and white-egg crosses such as ISA White and Hyline W-36. Kentucky notes the White Leghorn can lay "260-plus eggs in 12 months."

The catch is temperament. Kentucky calls commercial egg breeds "flighty and high strung," not good choices for small backyard flocks, and Ohio State warns White Leghorns "can be loud, nervous and occasionally aggressive towards people." For a home flock, Kentucky suggests the sex-link crosses instead, and NC State says sex-linked hens "are excellent egg layers and are often chosen for home garden flocks."

What a sex-link is. It's a cross where chicks hatch in different colors by sex, so the hatchery can sort them reliably. Kentucky describes the black sex-link (a Barred Plymouth Rock hen crossed with a Rhode Island Red or New Hampshire rooster): pullets feather out solid black and cockerels barred, and the cockerels hatch with a white dot on the head. Red sex-links (Golden Comet, Gold Star, Cinnamon Queen) hatch with white males and buff or red females. One trade-off from Ohio State: hybrids "cannot reproduce" true to type, so you can't hatch more of the same from your own flock.

Heritage and purebred hens lay less, but they last. NC State says commercial-type hens aim for an egg almost every day in their first year but are "designed for short term," while heritage breeds such as Barred Rock and Rhode Island Red are "hardy, proven breeds." Both Kentucky and UMaine say dual-purpose hens are "more dependable setters" than commercial crosses, which matters if you'd like a broody hen to hatch chicks someday.

A quick egg-color trick: Kentucky notes that white ear lobes generally mean white eggs and red ear lobes mean brown eggs, with the blue- and green-egg breeds as the exceptions.

For beginners and children, Ohio State points to the docile breeds: Plymouth Rock, Wyandotte and Orpington. For cold winters, the breeds the sources call cold-hardy are Plymouth Rock, Wyandotte and Welsummer; Kentucky also notes the California White sex-link was bred to handle small-flock and colder conditions. Meat birds

The Cornish Cross is the standard broiler. Kentucky says fryers reach 4 to 5 pounds in six weeks, and UMaine defines a broiler as 4½ to 5 pounds at seven to eight weeks. UMaine also lists slower-growing range lines such as Freedom Ranger and Red Broiler, which reach 5 to 5½ pounds at nine to eleven weeks and do better outdoors; Kentucky notes the Cornish Cross does poorly on pasture. If you raise dual-purpose straight-run chicks, UMaine says the extra cockerels make about 3 pounds at 10 to 12 weeks, which is smaller but still usable. How many hens do you need?

  • Per person: UMaine suggests planning on 1.5 layers per person with hatchery crosses, or 2 per person with heritage breeds. For a family of four, that's 6 hybrid hens or 8 heritage hens.
  • Per hen: NC State says a hen lays around two eggs every three days in her first laying year, up to about 15 dozen, dropping to about 12 dozen in year two.
  • For a pet-and-eggs flock: Ohio State suggests three to five birds.
  • When eggs start: NC State says pullets begin laying at 16 to 24 weeks of age.
  • How long they live: Ohio State gives an average chicken lifespan of about seven to eight years, well past the years of good laying, so plan for retired hens too.

Before you order, make sure the coop can hold them: NC State plans 2.5 to 3.5 square feet indoors and 4 to 5 square feet outdoors per bird, with one nest box per four or five hens. Our free chicken coop size calculator does the math.

Ordering chicks: straight run, sexed and where to buy

  1. Check your local rules first. Ohio State and NC State both warn that town ordinances often prohibit roosters, and some limit flock size.
  2. Know what "straight run" means. Ohio State: straight-run chicks are a mixture of pullets and cockerels; to get only pullets, buy chicks that are sexed. NC State advises against straight run unless you have a plan for the roosters, and says flocks of fewer than ten hens shouldn't have a rooster.
  3. Order one or two extra. NC State suggests buying one or two more sexed chicks than your final number to cover losses. If you buy started pullets, buy the number you want or one more.
  4. Buy healthy stock. UMaine recommends U.S. Pullorum-Typhoid Clean stock and a hatchery that vaccinates for Marek's disease, which has "no cure." Ohio State says to use NPIP-participating hatcheries. UMaine calls auctions and swap days "not the best places" for beginners to buy.
  5. Buy them all at once. Ohio State says it's best to buy birds of a similar age at the same time; adding birds later means quarantine and introductions (see our guide to adding new chickens).

Day-old chicks need a heated brooder for their first weeks; our brooding guide has the temperature schedule. If you're not sure which hatcheries serve your area, Mississippi State suggests asking your county extension office.

The short version

Decide what the flock is for first. For the most eggs in a backyard, sex-link hybrids are the extension favorite; for calm, hardy birds that also go broody and give some meat, pick a dual-purpose heritage breed such as Plymouth Rock, Wyandotte, Orpington or Rhode Island Red; for meat, Cornish Cross grows fastest while range lines handle pasture better. Plan about 1.5 hybrid hens or 2 heritage hens per person, check local rooster rules, order sexed pullets (plus one or two extra) from an NPIP hatchery that vaccinates for Marek's, and buy them all at the same age.
