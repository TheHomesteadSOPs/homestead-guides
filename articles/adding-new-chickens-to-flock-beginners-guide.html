Adding New Chickens to Your Flock: A Beginner's Guide to Quarantine, Introductions & the Pecking Order

Sooner or later every flock grows: a few new pullets in spring, a rescue hen from a neighbor, a bargain at the poultry swap. New birds are also the most common way disease gets into a healthy backyard flock, and dropping strangers straight into the coop is how pecking-order squabbles turn into bloody injuries. The fix is two separate steps, done in order: quarantine first, for disease, then a slow introduction, for behavior.

Brand new to hens? Start with our backyard flock guide. This article is about one specific job: getting new birds into a flock you already have without bringing in disease or starting a war.

Step one: buy smart, because healthy-looking isn't the same as healthy

Alberta Agriculture's small-flock guidance is blunt about the main risk: healthy-looking birds can still carry disease, and mature birds are a higher risk for bringing disease home than chicks. Where your birds come from matters:

  • Lower risk: a hatchery, chicks you hatch yourself, or a breeder you know and trust.
  • Higher risk: swaps, sales and auctions, where birds from many flocks share the same air and handlers.
  • Ask questions. A reputable seller knows the health status of their flock and has a plan if disease shows up. If a seller can't or won't answer, keep looking.

Before you bring a bird home, look it over. Agriculture Victoria's checklist for a healthy bird: good feather condition, bright clear eyes, clean nostrils and vent, an intact beak, a firm comb, normal weight, a full crop and an alert manner. Check under the feathers near the vent for lice and mites too.

Step two: quarantine for at least 30 days

This is the step most backyard keepers skip, and the one extension poultry programs agree on most. The University of Delaware's backyard flock fact sheet says to keep new birds isolated for at least 30 days before they meet your flock, and Alberta Agriculture gives the same 30-day minimum. (A University of Kentucky biosecurity article sets a floor of two weeks. Thirty days is the safer target, because some diseases take longer to show.)

Quarantine ruleWhat it means in a backyard
At least 30 daysMark the arrival date on the calendar. The new birds don't meet the flock until day 31 at the earliest.
Separate building, or at least 30 feet awayAlberta's distance. Agriculture Victoria says at least 10 meters (about 33 feet). A garage, shed or spare pen at the far end of the yard works.
No direct or indirect contactNo shared fence line, feeders, waterers or buckets. Use separate feed and water dishes.
Care for the new birds lastDo your main flock's chores first, then the quarantine pen. Never go from the new birds back to the old ones without changing.
Dedicated boots and clothingA pair of cheap boots and a barn coat that stay at the quarantine pen, plus hand washing before and after.
All in, all outIf you add another bird partway through, the 30-day clock restarts for everyone in quarantine.
The care-order rule is the easiest one to break by accident. The University of Kentucky puts it simply: move from the existing flock to the new birds, never the reverse, unless you change clothing and wash up first. If you only remember one thing, remember that.

What to watch for during quarantine

Look at the new birds every day. Alberta Agriculture lists the warning signs:

  • Low energy, moving less, or a poor appetite
  • Swelling around the head, neck or eyes
  • Coughing, sneezing or gasping
  • Tremors or poor coordination
  • Diarrhea, or a sudden death

If you see any of these, don't add the bird when the 30 days are up. Call a veterinarian or your state animal health office, and restart the quarantine count once the bird recovers. Agriculture Victoria adds two useful extras: have a lab check droppings for internal parasites such as coccidia and roundworms, and don't give antibiotics routinely; use them only with a vet's advice.

Step three: see but don't touch

Quarantine protects your flock from disease. It does nothing for the pecking order. Chickens sort out who ranks where by fighting, and a University of Kentucky poultry specialist notes that chickens will usually attack a new bird of the same species introduced into their pen, often targeting it as they work out where it fits. A slow introduction lets most of that sorting happen through a fence.

Alberta Agriculture's step is to pen the new birds beside the home flock for one week after quarantine, before full introduction. In practice:

  • Set up a pen inside or next to the run, divided by wire mesh, with its own food, water and shelter.
  • Leave them there about a week. Both groups see, hear and get used to each other without being able to peck.
  • Watch the fence line. Some posturing is normal. When the hens mostly ignore each other through the wire, you're ready for the next step.

Step four: the real introduction

Opening the divider is where most backyard keepers get nervous. These common-practice steps help, along with the extension guidance on why fights escalate:

  • Add birds in a group, not one at a time. A single newcomer takes all the attention. Two or three new birds share it.
  • Match sizes. Don't mix young chicks or small pullets in with full-grown hens. Wait until the young birds are close to adult size.
  • Give them room and extra stations. The University of Kentucky lists overcrowding as a trigger for aggressive pecking. Add an extra feeder and waterer for the first weeks so low-ranking birds can eat without being chased off. Make sure your coop is big enough for the new total with our coop size calculator.
  • Give them something else to peck. The same UK article suggests enrichment, such as hanging four to eight lengths of white polypropylene twine, or tossing scratch grains and greens, to redirect pecking away from flock mates.
  • Add hiding spots. A pallet leaned against the fence or a second perch lets a chased bird get out of sight.

Expect chasing, a few pecks and some pushing for the first days. That's the pecking order being settled, and stepping in too often just restarts it. What is not normal is blood or a bird being cornered and attacked nonstop.

Chickens are attracted to blood, and one injured bird can set off a pecking outbreak. University of Kentucky guidance: remove injured birds quickly and care for them apart from the flock until they heal. Put them back behind the mesh divider first, not straight into the group.

Keep a record

Alberta Agriculture recommends writing down each bird's origin, arrival date, how long it was quarantined, and the date it joined the flock. It takes two minutes, and if a disease ever does show up, that record tells you and your vet where it most likely came from.

The short version

Buy from a source you trust, and treat every new bird as a possible carrier no matter how healthy it looks. Quarantine for at least 30 days, at least 30 feet away, with separate gear, and always care for the new birds last. Then pen them beside the flock for about a week, introduce them in a small group once they're near adult size, add extra feeders and distractions, and pull out any bird that gets hurt. Write it all down.
