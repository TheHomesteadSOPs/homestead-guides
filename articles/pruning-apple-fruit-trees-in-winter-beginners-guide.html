Pruning Apple & Fruit Trees in Winter: A Beginner's Guide to Dormant Cuts, Timing & Tree Shape

Bare winter branches are the best view you'll get of a fruit tree's structure all year: every crossing limb, every crowded crotch, every water sprout is right there. That's why late winter is pruning season for apples, pears and most other fruit trees. Pruning scares a lot of beginners, but it comes down to two kinds of cut, one rule about where to make them, and the patience not to remove too much at once.

Still planning your first trees? Our backyard apple orchard guide covers rootstocks, chill hours and pollination. This article picks up after planting, with the yearly pruning job that keeps a tree productive.

When to prune: late winter, before buds break

Every extension source we checked agrees on the window: late winter or early spring, while the tree is still dormant. University of Minnesota Extension says to prune every year, "preferably after the coldest weather has passed and before growth begins." Oregon State University Extension explains why: once the worst cold is over, fresh cuts are less likely to suffer frost damage. The exact months depend on where you live:

  • New Mexico State University puts it at roughly February to early March for northern and central New Mexico.
  • UConn's home-garden program calls March and April the best months in Connecticut.

Use those as anchors: prune after your coldest stretch of winter, before the buds swell and open. Fall (right now) is the wrong time for major cuts. Use it to plan instead. OSU suggests one trick worth stealing: during the growing season, tie flagging tape around branches that aren't performing, so you know exactly which ones to cut once the leaves are gone and every branch looks the same.

The exceptions are dead, broken or diseased limbs. OSU recommends removing those as soon as you see them, at any time of year. Water sprouts (the fast, straight-up shoots) can also come off in summer.

Two cuts, and why one is better

Almost every pruning decision comes down to two kinds of cut. NMSU and UMN both explain the difference:

  • Thinning cut: removes a whole branch where it starts, at the trunk or at a larger limb. It opens the canopy to light and air without triggering much new growth. UMN calls this the cut usually recommended.
  • Heading cut: shortens a branch partway along its length. It stimulates a burst of new shoots just below the cut. It's useful for training young trees and controlling height, but overdone on a mature tree it produces a thicket of upright shoots that shade out the fruit.

As a beginner, default to thinning cuts. NMSU's advice for mature apples is to make a few large thinning cuts and avoid heavy heading.

How to make a clean cut

Where you cut matters as much as what you cut. Look at where a branch joins the trunk and you'll see a slightly swollen ring of bark: the branch collar. That's the tissue that seals the wound.

  • Cut just outside the collar, never flush with the trunk. University of Kentucky Extension recommends leaving about 1/4 to 1/2 inch of collar intact.
  • Don't leave a stub sticking out, either. UConn specifically warns against it.
  • Use the three-cut method for any limb heavy enough to tear. From NMSU: (1) undercut the limb a few inches out from the collar; (2) cut down from the top, a bit farther out, to drop most of the limb's weight; (3) make the final cut just outside the collar to remove the stub. The undercut stops the falling limb from peeling a strip of bark down the trunk.
  • Skip the wound paint. NMSU calls wound dressings debated and unnecessary when you prune at the right time, with clean tools and good technique.

For tools, sharp bypass pruners handle small shoots, loppers handle mid-sized branches, and a pruning saw handles anything thicker. University of Kentucky also recommends sanitizing blades between cuts with rubbing alcohol or a 10% bleach solution, especially when you're removing diseased wood, so you don't carry disease from one branch or tree to the next.

What to remove first

Work through the tree in this order. Pulled together from UMN, OSU and UK Extension:

  • Dead, broken and diseased wood, plus any shriveled "mummy" fruit still hanging on (UK).
  • Suckers coming up from the roots or low on the trunk.
  • Water sprouts: vigorous vertical shoots that rarely fruit well.
  • Crossing or competing limbs. Where two rub or crowd each other, remove one completely at its base.
  • Branches growing downward, or into the center of the tree and shading it.
  • Any side limb thicker than the trunk it grows from. UMN flags these because they compete with the leader.

Then step back and look before making more cuts. On a healthy tree, that list may be most of the year's work.

Shape: central leader vs. open center

Different fruit trees are trained to different shapes. NMSU's breakdown:

  • Apples and pears: central leader. One main upright trunk (the leader) with tiers of side branches, giving a cone or Christmas-tree shape that lets light reach the lower limbs. Plums are pruned like apples. Sweet cherries can go either way.
  • Peaches and nectarines: open center, or vase. No central leader, just 3 to 4 main scaffold limbs angled outward around a sunny, open middle. Peaches fruit on last year's wood, so NMSU says to prune them every year.

For a central-leader apple, UMN suggests spacing the main side branches (scaffolds) about 12 inches apart up the trunk, with no two starting at the same height. Train them toward about a 60-degree angle from the leader. OSU explains why that angle matters: narrow, V-shaped crotches trap bark inside the joint and can split under a heavy crop, while branches pulled too flat tend to throw up water sprouts. On a young tree you can widen narrow angles with a notched-stick spreader, or with a wooden clothespin clipped onto shoots under about 6 inches long. Remove spreaders after the growing season, per UMN.

Young trees vs. old trees

Young trees: prune less than you think. UMN warns that excessive pruning delays or reduces fruiting, and OSU notes that a young apple may give little fruit for its first four or five years anyway. For now, the job is building a strong framework of well-spaced scaffolds, not cutting for size. Once the tree reaches the height you want, OSU's method is to cut the leader back to a side branch and hold that height every year after.

Know your variety first. OSU points out that most modern apples bear fruit on spurs (short, stubby twigs along the branches), but many older varieties are tip-bearing and fruit at the ends of last year's shoots. Head back every shoot on a tip-bearer and you've cut off next year's crop.

Mature trees: OSU notes that new fruiting branches produce best for about three to five years, then taper off. Each winter, thin out a few of the oldest, least productive branches so young wood keeps replacing them.

Rescuing a neglected, overgrown tree

Inherited a tall, tangled old apple tree? The biggest mistake is fixing it all in one winter. Every source agrees on going slow:

  • Spread the work over two to three years. UConn warns that heavy one-time cuts cause excessive regrowth and can sunburn newly exposed limbs. NMSU warns that removing several large branches in one season can weaken or even kill the tree.
  • Remove up to about one-third of the larger branches across those years. That's University of Kentucky's figure for rejuvenating an older tree.
  • Year one: take out the dead and broken wood and a few big limbs that open up the center. UMN suggests deciding up front which branch will be the leader and which scaffolds to keep.
  • Bring the height down gradually. UConn suggests working toward 18 feet or less, keeping six to eight main scaffolds and thinning about a third of the bushy secondary growth.
  • Expect water sprouts near big cuts afterward, and remove most of them. UConn notes that sprouts on outward-bending branches can sometimes be kept and become fruiting wood.
Pears need extra care. NMSU recommends limiting pear pruning to thinning cuts once the framework is set, because heading cuts push out a flush of soft new shoots that are highly susceptible to fire blight.

The short version

Prune once a year in late winter, after your coldest weather and before the buds open. Use thinning cuts more than heading cuts, cut just outside the branch collar, use three cuts on heavy limbs, and wipe your blades between trees. Remove the dead, diseased, crossing, downward and water-sprout growth first, then shape apples and pears to a central leader and peaches to an open vase. Go easy on young trees and spread the rescue of an old one over a few winters. Light, yearly pruning beats one dramatic cut every time.
