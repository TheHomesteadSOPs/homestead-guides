Handling Venison After the Hunt: A Beginner's Guide to Field Dressing, Cooling, Aging & Freezing Deer Meat

The quality of venison on your table is mostly decided in the first few hours after the shot. Michigan State University Extension says the most common mistakes hunters make are contaminating the carcass with gut contents or dirt, and the second is letting it stay warm too long. Get those two things right and the rest is ordinary careful meat handling: aging (or not), trimming the fat, wrapping well and cooking to the right temperature. Here's the field-to-freezer process extension food-safety specialists recommend.

Before the hunt: pack the right kit

North Dakota State University Extension's field-dressing list is short: a sharp hunting knife, a small hatchet, about 12 feet of light rope or cord, and a cloth to wipe out the carcass and your hands. Wisconsin's Department of Agriculture (DATCP) adds disposable gloves, and recommends wearing them whenever you handle deer to reduce exposure to disease. Pack paper towels, a few clean plastic bags for ice, and a cooler if the weather may be warm. Fasten your tag before you move the deer (NDSU), and check your state's rules on tagging, registration and moving carcasses.

Chronic wasting disease (CWD): NDSU's precautions are: don't shoot, handle or eat any animal that looks sick; wear rubber gloves for field dressing and processing; minimize handling of brain and spinal tissue; bone out the carcass or at least don't eat brain, spinal cord, eyes, spleen or lymph nodes; and don't eat meat from any animal that tests positive. Many states offer CWD testing; check with your wildlife agency, especially if you hunt in a known CWD area.

Step 1: Field dress right away, and cleanly

Wisconsin DATCP says to field-dress the carcass immediately after death. NDSU agrees: the sooner it's dressed, the better the meat.

  • Protect the guts from your knife. NDSU's method: with the deer on its back, open the midline from breastbone to rectum, using the knife handle and your other hand to push the paunch and intestines away from the blade so you don't cut them. Cut around the anus and loosen it so it comes out with the intestines.
  • Clean up contamination. Remove visible dirt, feces, hair and bloodshot areas, and wipe the cavity with paper towels. If you rinse it, dry it quickly (DATCP). Clean your knife between cuts (DATCP).
  • Practice basic hygiene. University of Arkansas Extension advises removing jewelry, washing hands, wearing gloves, and not eating, drinking or using tobacco while handling game.
  • About the scent glands. The tarsal glands on the hind legs can be removed or left alone. NDSU cites Utah State research that they don't cause off flavors, but wash or change gloves if you touch them so you don't transfer the musk to the meat.
  • Check the organs. DATCP says not to eat the meat if the organs smell offensive or you see greenish discharge, black blood or blood clots in the muscle.

Step 2: Cool it fast, below 40°F

Above 40°F, harmful bacteria grow quickly (DATCP). The goal is to get the carcass to 40°F or below as soon as possible (MSU). How fast is fast enough? Arkansas Extension cites FDA guidance to cool meat to 40°F within four hours; Wisconsin's minimum target is to chill the carcass surface to 40°F within 24 hours. Aim for the faster figure whenever you can.

  • Open it up. Prop the rib cage open to let heat out (DATCP). NDSU suggests hanging the deer by the head to speed cooling.
  • Warm days: if the air is above 40°F, pack the cavity with bags of ice or snow and tie it shut (DATCP; MSU). Arkansas Extension suggests quartering the deer to fit in coolers. In very warm weather DATCP says to process it as soon as it's harvested, because venison will spoil if it hangs unrefrigerated.
  • Hide on or off? In cool weather, leaving the hide on prevents drying and discoloration. In hot weather you'll probably need to skin it to cool it, and protect it from flies with cheesecloth (NDSU).
  • Transport it cool. DATCP says don't tie a deer over the hood or roof, or put it in a car trunk, where heat promotes bacterial growth. Keep it out of the sun with air around it. NDSU advises against tarps or canvas bags, which hold in heat.

Step 3: Aging (optional)

Aging lets natural enzymes tenderize the meat, but it only works with proper temperature control.

GuidelineWhat the sources say
Aging temperature32 to 36°F, never above 40°F (Wisconsin DATCP); 34 to 37°F (NDSU)
How long10 to 14 days (NDSU); no longer than 2 weeks (DATCP)
HideLeave the skin on during cold aging (DATCP); skinned game is a poor candidate (NDSU)
Which deerOnly a clean, properly handled carcass that was not gut-shot (DATCP)
FreezingAvoid freezing the carcass before rigor sets in, which toughens it (DATCP)

If you don't have a cooler or a reliably cold space that stays in that range, skip aging. NDSU warns that aging without proper cooler space leads to spoilage and drying, and notes that meat you'll grind or chop doesn't need aging at all. A garage that swings from 25°F at night to 50°F in the afternoon is not an aging room.

Step 4: Butcher, trim and wrap

  • Trim the fat. MSU says deer fat goes rancid quickly and often has an undesirable "gamey" flavor, so trim it and clean each cut for its end use.
  • Bone it out. Boning saves freezer space and removes the spinal cord and lymph nodes (NDSU). DATCP recommends limiting sawing through bone, especially the spine, and cutting away damaged meat around the wound channel. Arkansas Extension advises waiting until rigor mortis has set in before removing all the meat from the bones.
  • Work clean. MSU recommends washing surfaces with hot soapy water, then sanitizing with 1 teaspoon of regular bleach per quart of warm water.
  • Wrap airtight. Use freezer paper, heavy-duty foil, vacuum bags or freezer bags, press out all the air, and label packages with a permanent marker (DATCP). Put a sheet of freezer paper between steaks or patties (MSU).

Fresh raw venison keeps 3 to 5 days in a 40°F refrigerator, stored on a platter on the bottom shelf (MSU).

Step 5: Freeze, store and thaw

Freeze quickly at 0°F or below (MSU), and space packages out so cold air can circulate until they're frozen (DATCP). Storage times are about quality, not safety, and the sources vary:

CutBest-quality freezer time
Ground venisonup to 3 months (MSU)
Roasts and steaks6 to 9 months (MSU)
Well-wrapped venison, general9 to 12 months (Wisconsin DATCP); up to a year (Arkansas Extension)

MSU's practical rule: only put up as much venison as your household will eat in a year. To thaw, use the refrigerator, the microwave, or cold water changed every 30 minutes, never the counter (MSU). Allow about a day per 5 pounds in the fridge. You can also cook it from frozen; it takes about 1.5 times as long (MSU). Don't refreeze thawed raw venison (DATCP).

Step 6: Cook it to 160°F

MSU Extension notes research showing color and texture are not safe ways to judge venison doneness. Use a thermometer. MSU, NDSU and Arkansas Extension all give 160°F as the safe internal temperature for venison and other game meats. Because venison is so lean, MSU recommends moist, low-heat methods like braising and stewing for most cuts, and Arkansas Extension suggests adding a little butter, cream or oil for juiciness. Venison jerky and canned venison

Jerky: drying alone doesn't reliably kill bacteria. University of Arkansas Extension's method is to bring the meat to 160°F either before drying (boil strips in the marinade for 5 minutes) or after drying (10 minutes in a 275°F oven for strips ¼ inch thick or less), then dry at 140°F. Slice no thicker than ¼ inch and trim all fat. Ground-meat jerky must reach 160°F too. Because wild game can carry trichinella, Arkansas Extension says game used for jerky should first be frozen at 0°F or below for at least 30 days in portions 6 inches thick or less. Don't make jerky from meat that touched gut contents. Properly dried jerky keeps about two weeks at room temperature in a sealed container; refrigerate or freeze it for longer.

Canning: venison is low-acid, so it must be pressure canned, never water-bath canned (MSU). For strips, cubes or chunks, the National Center for Home Food Preservation gives 75 minutes for pints and 90 minutes for quarts, hot or raw pack, with 1-inch headspace, at 11 lb pressure on a dial gauge or 10 lb on a weighted gauge (0 to 1,000 ft; adjust for altitude). NCHFP suggests soaking strong-flavored game for an hour in brine (1 tablespoon salt per quart of water) and removing large bones and excess fat first. The short version

Wear gloves, field-dress immediately without nicking the guts, and get the carcass below 40°F as fast as you can, with ice in warm weather and never in a hot trunk. Age only if you can hold 32 to 37°F for up to two weeks. Trim all the fat, bone it out, wrap it airtight and freeze at 0°F. Thaw in the fridge, cook to 160°F, heat jerky to 160°F, and pressure can only.
