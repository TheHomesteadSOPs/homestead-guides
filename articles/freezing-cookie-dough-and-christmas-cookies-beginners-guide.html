Freezing Cookie Dough & Christmas Cookies: A Beginner's Guide to Baking Ahead, Storing & Raw-Dough Safety

December baking gets a lot calmer when most of it happens in November. Michigan State and North Dakota State extension specialists both say cookie dough and baked cookies hold their quality for about six months in a 0°F freezer, so a few batches made now can be on the tray in time for Christmas. Here's how long cookies really keep, how to wrap them so they don't taste like the freezer, and the raw-dough rule that matters most when kids are helping.

How long cookies keep

WhereHow longSource
Counter, covered1–2 days; refrigerate or freeze anything you won't eat by thenNC Cooperative Extension
Counter, well packedAbout a week for most homemade cookiesOld Farmer's Almanac
FridgeRequired for cream, custard, cheese or egg-based fillingsNC Cooperative Extension
Freezer (0°F), best flavorAbout 3 monthsOld Farmer's Almanac
Freezer (0°F), baked cookies or doughNo more than 6 monthsMSU Extension; NDSU Extension

The sources disagree a little on the counter, so take the cautious view for anything soft or filled. Cookies keep at room temperature because they're low in moisture and high in sugar, NC Cooperative Extension explains, but homemade ones have no preservatives and go stale or spoil sooner than store-bought. In the freezer, the time limits are about quality, not safety: NDSU notes that food kept frozen at 0°F stays safe indefinitely, it just slowly loses flavor and texture. MSU adds that freezer burn (grayish-brown leathery spots) isn't a food-safety risk either, only a taste one.

Freezing cookie dough

MSU Extension describes two ways to do it:

  1. Scoop and freeze (drop cookies). Portion the dough onto cookie sheets as if you were about to bake, freeze until solid, then move the frozen balls into freezer bags or airtight containers and press out as much air as you can. You can bake a dozen at a time later.
  2. Freeze the whole batch. Wrap the dough in waxed paper or plastic wrap, then put it in a freezer bag or airtight container. Thaw it later and shape it into cookies. The same idea works for a log of slice-and-bake dough or a flat disk of cut-out dough.

Label every bag with the cookie type and the date, as both MSU and NDSU recommend. NDSU's tip is to use a permanent marker "to avoid frozen food surprise." Unbaked balls straight from the freezer usually need a minute or two longer in the oven than fresh dough, so bake one test tray and watch it.

Freezing baked cookies

  • Cool them completely first (MSU). Warm cookies steam inside the bag and turn soggy or frost over.
  • Wrap out the air. MSU suggests wrapping cookies individually in plastic wrap and then bagging them, which keeps them longest. The quicker option is to layer them between sheets of waxed paper in a container and cover the top layer with waxed paper, plastic wrap or foil.
  • Freeze frosted cookies uncovered first. MSU says to freeze iced cookies on a tray until the frosting is firm, then pack them, so the icing doesn't smear or stick.
  • Keep crisp and soft cookies apart. The Old Farmer's Almanac warns that packing them together ruins both textures: the crisp ones soften and the soft ones dry out. Put waxed paper between soft, frosted or delicate cookies and bars.
  • Pick real freezer containers. NDSU advises against reusing margarine, yogurt or cottage cheese tubs, narrow-neck glass jars that can crack, and microwave-dinner trays. Freezer bags, freezer-safe containers and metal tins with an inner bag all work.
  • Don't overload the freezer. NDSU's rule of thumb is to add no more than 2 to 3 pounds of food per cubic foot of freezer space in 24 hours, so things freeze fast. Bake-day surplus can go in over two or three days.

Thawing and re-crisping

Cookies are small and dry, so they thaw quickly. Leave them wrapped or in their closed container until they reach room temperature; any condensation then forms on the wrapping rather than on the cookies. For crisp cookies that went a little soft, the Old Farmer's Almanac suggests a few minutes unwrapped in a 300°F oven (unfrosted cookies only). Frosted and filled cookies are best thawed in the fridge or a cool room and served the same day or the next.

Frozen dough that was frozen as a single block needs to thaw before shaping; doing it in the refrigerator keeps it cold and easy to roll. Our power outage food safety guide covers what to do with a full freezer if the power goes out mid-December.

The raw-dough rule (especially with kids)

Don't eat raw dough, and don't let kids eat or play with it. The CDC explains that most flour is raw, meaning it hasn't been treated to kill germs such as E. coli and Salmonella, and that raw or lightly cooked eggs can carry Salmonella. That applies to play dough and craft dough made with raw flour too. Bake dough and batter before eating it.
  • Wash up after handling dough. The CDC and NC Cooperative Extension both say to wash hands, bowls, utensils and counters after working with raw flour, eggs or dough, and to keep them away from ready-to-eat foods (like the finished cookies on the cooling rack).
  • No finger-licking while decorating. NC Cooperative Extension notes that bacteria from hands have been linked to Norovirus and Staph outbreaks from baked goods.
  • Royal icing and meringue: use pasteurized egg whites, NC Cooperative Extension advises.
  • No-bake recipes that use flour: buy heat-treated flour, or, per NC Cooperative Extension, spread the flour on a single baking sheet and bake it at 350°F for 5 minutes first.
  • Recalls: the CDC says to throw away any flour or baking mix that has been recalled.

A simple make-ahead plan

  1. Early to mid November: pick your cookie list and make the sturdy doughs that freeze best: drop cookies, slice-and-bake logs, gingerbread and sugar-cookie dough. Scoop or wrap, label, freeze.
  2. Late November: bake and freeze plain cut-outs and unfrosted cookies, packed by type, crisp and soft kept apart.
  3. First half of December: bake the frozen dough balls a tray at a time as you need them, and decorate the frozen cut-outs after thawing.
  4. The last few days: fill tins and gift plates, and make anything with a cream or cheese filling, which needs the fridge anyway. Exact recipe amounts scale easily with the kitchen conversion guide.

The short version

Cookie dough and baked cookies keep about six months at 0°F (best flavor in the first three). Scoop and freeze drop-cookie dough on a tray before bagging it, or wrap whole batches. Cool baked cookies completely, wrap out the air, freeze frosted cookies uncovered until firm, keep crisp and soft cookies in separate containers, and label everything. Thaw cookies in their wrapping, re-crisp plain ones briefly at 300°F, and never eat raw dough.
