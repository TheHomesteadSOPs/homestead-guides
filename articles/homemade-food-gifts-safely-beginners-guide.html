Giving Homemade Food Gifts Safely: A Beginner's Guide to Jams, Jar Mixes, Infused Oils, Baked Goods & Mailing

A tin of cookies or a jar of jam is a classic homestead gift, but a few popular ideas, like cake baked in a jar or garlic in oil, carry a real botulism risk. Here's what extension food safety specialists at Oregon State, Iowa State, Missouri, NC State, Illinois, UGA, UMaine and Clemson, plus USDA FSIS, say about which homemade food gifts are safe, how to label them and how to mail them.

The easy, low-risk gifts

Oregon State's food safety specialist Joy Waite-Cusic notes that baked goods, candied or roasted nuts and candy are low-moisture foods and considered safe to give, and Illinois Extension lists peanut brittle, fudge and cookies as generally safe. A few rules keep them that way:

  • Breads and cookies can stay covered at room temperature (NC State Extension).
  • Refrigerate anything with a cream, custard, cheese, meat or vegetable filling, or a cream frosting (NC State), and tell the person you give it to.
  • Bake ahead and freeze. Missouri Extension recommends a freezer at 0°F or colder, freezer-safe packaging and a name-and-date label; baked goods can thaw at room temperature. Jams, jellies and other home-canned gifts

Oregon State says fruit products with a pH below 4.6, such as jams, jellies, pie fillings and applesauce, are usually safe when made from reliable, tested directions. To keep on a shelf, Illinois Extension says jams and jellies must be processed in a boiling-water canner for the correct time, in jars made for home canning with two-piece lids; if the processing time is under 10 minutes, sanitize the jars first by boiling them for 10 minutes. Our jam and pickle ratios guide explains why the sugar and acid in a tested recipe matter.

  • Skip the paraffin. Oregon State says sealing jam with wax is no longer recommended because of mold risk.
  • Vegetables, meats and soups are low-acid and must be pressure canned with the correct weight, time and altitude adjustment (Oregon State). Pickles need the right acid: bottled lemon juice, 5% vinegar or citric acid.
  • Check every seal. Don't give or eat from a jar that didn't seal within 24 hours or came unsealed in storage (Oregon State).
Don't give these as shelf-stable gifts. Pumpkin and sweet potato butters should never be canned; NC State Extension says to keep them refrigerated and eat them within a week. Chocolate, fudge and other dessert sauces have no tested canning recipes; Illinois Extension notes USDA recommends refrigerator or freezer storage only. Herbs or vegetables in oil are on Clemson's not-recommended list, and Oregon State says there are no safe methods for pesto or flavored oils.

Why "cake in a jar" is a no

Quick breads and cakes baked inside canning jars show up every December, but extension specialists at Iowa State, Missouri, Oregon State and Illinois all advise against them. Iowa State's AnswerLine explains that many of these recipes have a pH above 4.6 and the jars aren't free of oxygen, so recipes promising a shelf life of a year or more are "NOT SHELF SAFE." Missouri Extension notes that Penn State researchers found canned bread or cake can support the bacteria that cause botulism. The glass is a problem too: canning jars are made for wet heat, not dry oven heat, and can break.

Iowa State's alternatives: bake the bread or cake normally and freeze it ("Most cakes and breads freeze well"), or give a mix in a jar.

Mixes in a jar

  • Add a use-by date. Iowa State says one month is generally appropriate; Missouri Extension says one to two months after you make the mix.
  • Watch the add-ins. Brown sugar, nuts and dried fruit can harden or clump, so Missouri suggests packing toppings separately.
  • Attach the recipe and the full ingredient list (Illinois, Iowa State). Anyone with a food allergy needs to see everything that's in the jar.

Infused oils, honey and liquor

Garlic, herbs and vegetables can carry botulism spores, and oil keeps out oxygen, which is the main risk UMaine's food safety specialist warns about with homemade specialty oils. If you still want to give an infused oil, UGA's Carla Schwan gives two tested routes:

  • Simple, refrigerated: heat the oil to 180°F before adding fresh or dried garlic or herbs, keep it in the refrigerator, and discard it after four days (or freeze it). UGA adds that using dried herbs doesn't make it safe at room temperature.
  • Acidified (University of Idaho method): soak chopped garlic or herbs in a citric acid solution (1 tablespoon citric acid in 2 cups of water) for 24 hours before infusing. UGA says not to swap in vinegar or lemon juice, and the method's ratios and times need to be followed exactly.

For gifts, the four-day refrigerated version only works if you hand it over in person with a clear "keep refrigerated, use within 4 days" label. NC State Extension adds that there are no tested recipes for infused honey (treat it like oil), infused liquor recipes are untested, and all homemade infused foods should be refrigerated or frozen.

Jerky, smoked meat and dried foods

Oregon State says vacuum-sealed dried fruits, vegetables and nuts are fine at room temperature, and jerky is too once it has been properly dried. Smoked meats and fish are different: keep them refrigerated, and freeze them if they'll be stored longer than 2 weeks. Our food dehydrating guide covers the basics of drying at home.

Label every gift

Missouri Extension and Clemson both suggest a label or tag with:

  • What it is and the date you made it (Missouri says use home-canned foods within a year).
  • The ingredients, for anyone with a food allergy.
  • Storage instructions, such as "Store in a cool, dry place. Refrigerate after opening" and "Discard if the lid seal is broken or popped up."
  • Cooking directions and serving ideas, if needed.

Mailing food gifts

Clemson's Home & Garden Information Center lists foods that travel well without refrigeration: cookies, brownies, fruitcake, hard salami, hard cheese and country ham. For anything perishable, Clemson and USDA FSIS give the same steps:

  1. Freeze the food solid first; frozen food stays at a safe temperature longest.
  2. Pack it in a sturdy box of heavy foam or corrugated cardboard with frozen gel packs or dry ice, and fill empty space with crumpled paper or foam peanuts, since air space speeds thawing.
  3. Handle dry ice with gloves, never let it touch the food, and write "Contains Dry Ice" on the box.
  4. Mark the box "Perishable – Keep Refrigerated."
  5. Ship overnight, early in the week, so it doesn't sit in a warehouse over the weekend. Tell the recipient it's coming, and don't send perishables to an office unless it arrives on a workday and there's fridge space.

On arrival, food should still be frozen, partly frozen with ice crystals, or below 40°F on a food thermometer (USDA FSIS). If it's warmer, Clemson says don't eat it or even taste it. Perishable food shouldn't sit between 40°F and 140°F for more than 2 hours.

The short version

Cookies, candy, fudge, brittle, roasted nuts and breads are the safest homemade gifts, and they mail well. Jams and jellies are fine from a tested recipe processed in a boiling-water canner, but never can pumpkin butter, dessert sauces or cakes and breads in jars; give a dated mix in a jar instead. Treat garlic and herb oils as a refrigerated, four-day food. Label every gift with the date, ingredients and storage instructions, and ship perishables frozen, packed with cold packs, overnight and early in the week.
