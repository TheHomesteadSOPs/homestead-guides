Planning Next Year's Garden in Winter: A Beginner's Guide to Seed Inventory, Seed Viability & How Much to Plant

The garden is put to bed and the catalogs are arriving. Before you order anything, spend an evening with the seed box: sort what you have, check how old it is, test anything doubtful, and work out how much of each crop your household actually eats. This guide walks through each step using university Cooperative Extension guidance.

Step 1: Take a seed inventory

Gather every packet and jar in one place, including seed you saved yourself. For each one, write down the crop, the variety, the year it was packed or collected, and roughly how much is left. University of Minnesota Extension recommends labeling saved seed with its name, variety and collection date for exactly this reason: the date is what tells you whether it's still worth planting.

Sort the list three ways: plenty and fresh (use it), old or low (test or replace), and gaps (crops you want to grow but have no seed for). The last two piles become your order list.

Step 2: How long seeds last, by crop

Seed doesn't expire on a set date. It slowly loses the ability to sprout, and how fast depends on the crop and on how it was stored. South Dakota State University Extension notes most seed lasts well past the packet's "sell by" date, and some will keep 10 years or more if stored carefully. Step 3: Test old seed before you count on it

Iowa State University Extension recommends a germination test for any seed that is more than a year old or doesn't look fresh, ideally about a month before planting so there's time to reorder. It takes a paper towel and a week:

  1. Count out at least 20 seeds picked at random. Iowa State says 50 is better and 100 is best, but for a small home packet 20 gives a useful answer.
  2. Spread them on several layers of damp paper towel, roll it up so the seeds stay apart, and slip the roll into a plastic bag. Label it with the crop.
  3. Keep it warm, about 70 to 80°F.
  4. Check after 2 or 3 days, then daily for about a week. A seed counts as germinated once a root or seed leaf pokes through the coat.
  5. Divide the number that sprouted by the number tested. 16 of 20 is 80 percent.
Germination rateWhat it means (Iowa State Extension)
90% or moreVery good for most crops
70–80%Perfectly acceptable, especially for small or plentiful seed; sow a little thicker
50% or lowerToo low to be worth using for nearly all crops; replace it

Lower-germinating seed is still usable if you sow more of it, and SDSU notes some gardeners mix old seed with new. The arithmetic is simple: at 70 percent, sowing 14 seeds gives you about 10 seedlings (10 ÷ 0.7 ≈ 14).

Step 4: Decide how much to plant

Step 5: Store what's left so it lasts

Seed you keep for next year lasts longest cool, dry and dark. SDSU Extension calls humidity the most important factor and says relative humidity below 60 percent is best, while temperatures above 90°F greatly shorten seed life, so a summer shed or garage is a poor spot.

  • Use a tightly sealed glass jar, with packets of different crops inside one container (University of Minnesota Extension).
  • Add a desiccant: a small packet of silica gel, or 1 to 2 tablespoons of powdered milk from a freshly opened box wrapped in tissue or cheesecloth, which absorbs moisture for about six months (UMN).
  • The refrigerator works well. UMN gives 32 to 41°F as ideal, and SDSU says refrigeration can extend storage life by several years. Let a cold jar warm to room temperature before opening it, so moisture doesn't condense on the seed (SDSU).
Make a simple timeline next. Once you know what you're growing, count back from your last spring frost date to schedule seed starting. Our frost dates and succession planting guide shows how, and a soil test tells you what the beds need before planting.

The short version

List every seed you have with its year. Expect onion, parsnip, corn and pepper seed to fade after 1 to 2 years and cucumber, melon and radish seed to last about 5. Germination-test anything over a year old about a month before planting: 20 seeds in a damp paper towel at 70 to 80°F for a week. Above 70 percent is fine if you sow thicker; 50 percent or less, replace it. Size plantings with a per-person table (say 3 to 5 tomato plants and 10 to 15 feet of bush beans each for fresh eating), order to fill the gaps, and store leftovers in a sealed jar with a desiccant somewhere cool and dry.
