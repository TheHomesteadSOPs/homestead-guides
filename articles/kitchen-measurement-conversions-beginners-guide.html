Kitchen Measurement Conversions: A Beginner's Guide to Cups, Spoons, Grams & Oven Temperatures

Holiday baking season is when an old family recipe card says "a scant cup," a European cookbook says 250 grams, and your oven only reads Fahrenheit. Converting between them is mostly simple arithmetic, but a few traps catch nearly everyone: flour that weighs a third more when you scoop it, a "cup" that isn't the same size in every country, and spoon math that goes sideways when you halve a recipe. Here is the reference, plus the measuring habits that make it work.

Swapping an ingredient rather than converting it? Our fresh vs. dried herb guide covers herb ratios. This article is about the measurements themselves.

The spoon and cup ladder

Kansas State University Research and Extension's Cooking Basics: Measuring Tools sheet lays out the U.S. equivalents every recipe is built on. Memorize the first three lines and you can work out most of the rest:

1 cup16 tablespoons = 8 fluid ounces
3/4 cup12 tablespoons
2/3 cup10 tablespoons + 2 teaspoons

1/3 cup5 tablespoons + 1 teaspoon

1 pint2 cups

1 gallon4 quarts
1 stick of butter (1/4 pound)1/2 cup = 8 tablespoons

K-State's abbreviations are worth knowing too, because handwritten recipes mix them freely: Tbsp. or T. is a tablespoon, tsp. or t. is a teaspoon, and C or c. is a cup. A capital T versus a lowercase t is a threefold difference, so read old recipe cards carefully.

For weight, NIST rounds 1 ounce to 28 g, 1 pound to 454 g, and 1 kilogram to 2.2 pounds.

Not every cup is the same cup. A U.S. cup is 8 fluid ounces, which works out to about 237 mL (NIST rounds it to 240). Recipes from Canada, Australia and many other countries often use a 250 mL metric cup instead. The difference is only about 5%, and for soups and stews it doesn't matter. In baking it can, so if a recipe gives grams or milliliters, use those numbers rather than converting back to cups.

Why a cup of flour isn't a fixed weight

A cup measures volume, and flour compresses. Illinois Extension warns that scooping flour straight from the bag packs it down and can give you up to 150% of the amount the recipe intended. That's the classic cause of dry, dense cookies and bread.

Even published weight charts don't agree. King Arthur Baking lists 1 cup of all-purpose flour at 120 g, while America's Test Kitchen lists it at 142 g (5 ounces). Both are reputable test kitchens, and the gap shows how much a "cup" of flour can vary. Use the weight that matches the source your recipe came from, and if a recipe gives grams, weigh. Both King Arthur and America's Test Kitchen say a digital scale is more accurate than any cup.

A few everyday weights per 1 cup, from King Arthur's ingredient weight chart:

Notice that a cup of flour and a cup of sugar differ by more than 75 grams. You can't convert cups to grams with one number for everything. Each ingredient needs its own.

How to measure when you don't have a scale

Volume measuring works fine if you do it the same way the recipe writer did. K-State and Illinois Extension describe the standard methods:

  • Use the right cup. Dry ingredients go in nesting cups (1/4, 1/3, 1/2 and 1 cup) that you fill to the rim. Liquids go in a clear cup with a spout and printed lines.
  • Flour: fluff, spoon, level. Stir the flour in its container, lightly spoon it into the cup until it heaps over, then sweep the excess off with the flat edge of a knife. Don't pack it, shake it or tap the cup.
  • Level over the canister, not the mixing bowl. Illinois Extension's tip: the extra flour that falls off shouldn't end up in your dough.
  • Granulated sugar: spoon it in and level it.
  • Brown sugar: pack it firmly with the back of a spoon, then level. K-State's test: it should hold the cup's shape when you turn it out. Illinois adds a caution: pack it only when the recipe says "packed."
  • Liquids: set the cup on a flat counter, pour to the line and read it at eye level, not from above.
  • Solid fats like lard, shortening and peanut butter: pack them firmly into a dry cup or spoon and level with a knife. For stick butter, cut along the tablespoon marks on the wrapper. Measure melted butter or oil like a liquid.
  • Thick, sticky foods like yogurt or peanut butter go in a dry cup, not a liquid one, so you can level them (Illinois Extension).

Oven temperatures: Fahrenheit to Celsius

The formula is °C = (°F − 32) × 5 ÷ 9. Oven dials and recipes round to the nearest 5 or 10 degrees, so here are the usual settings, calculated and rounded:

Fan (convection) ovens cook faster than the temperature suggests. Many recipes written for them already account for that, so check which kind of oven a metric recipe assumes before you adjust anything.

Halving and doubling without getting lost

The spoon ladder above is what makes scaling easy. Convert awkward fractions to tablespoons first, then divide:

  • Half of 1/3 cup = half of 5 Tbsp + 1 tsp = 2 Tbsp + 2 tsp.
  • Half of 3/4 cup = half of 12 Tbsp = 6 Tbsp.
  • Half of 1/4 cup = 2 Tbsp.
  • Half of 1 Tbsp = 1 1/2 tsp.
  • Eggs: Utah State University Extension says 1 large egg is about 3 tablespoons, so half an egg is a beaten egg measured out to about 1 1/2 tablespoons.

For a doubled recipe, multiply everything except time, which usually doesn't double. A deeper pan takes longer, but not twice as long, so start checking at the original time.

A few handy pantry equivalents

Utah State University Extension's list of ingredient substitutions also has some conversions that come up constantly in a homestead kitchen:

  • 1 pound of brown sugar is about 2 1/4 cups, firmly packed.
  • 5 large eggs make about 1 cup.
  • Sour milk or buttermilk stand-in: put 1 tablespoon of vinegar or lemon juice in a cup, add milk to the 1-cup line, and let it stand 5 minutes.
  • Honey for sugar: use 3/4 cup honey per cup of sugar and reduce the other liquid by 1/4 cup. In baked goods, add 1/2 teaspoon baking soda per cup of honey.
Canning is the exception to all of this. Converting freely is fine for cookies, but a tested canning recipe's acid, salt and headspace amounts are part of its safety, not its flavor. Measure them exactly as written with standard measuring spoons and cups, and don't round or substitute. Our home canning guide explains why.

The short version

Three teaspoons make a tablespoon, sixteen tablespoons make a cup, and a U.S. cup is about 240 mL (a metric cup is 250). A cup of flour weighs about 120 to 142 g depending on how it's filled, so spoon and level it, never scoop, and weigh whenever a recipe gives grams. Pack brown sugar only when told to, read liquids at eye level, and halve awkward fractions by converting them to tablespoons first. Convert freely for everyday cooking, but never for the acid and salt in a canning recipe.
