Storing Emergency Drinking Water: A Beginner's Guide to How Much, Which Containers, Sanitizing & When to Rotate

A winter storm, a burst main or a well pump with no power can leave a homestead without safe water for days. A stored supply is cheap and simple, as long as you use the right containers, keep it clean and rotate it. Here's what the CDC and extension specialists at New Mexico State, Ohio State, Oklahoma State, Florida and Utah State say about storing emergency drinking water at home.

How much water to store

The CDC's baseline is at least 1 gallon per person per day for at least 3 days, and it suggests aiming for a 2-week supply if you can. That gallon covers drinking plus a little cooking and washing up; Utah State notes a quart a day will keep someone alive, but a gallon is far more comfortable, and adds half a gallon to a gallon a day more for hygiene and dishes.

Do the math once. Florida's example: 4 people × 1.5 gallons × 3 days = 18 gallons. At the CDC's 2-week goal, the same family at 1 gallon each needs 56 gallons. Our emergency binder guide has a worksheet approach for writing your own number down. Livestock need far more water than people, so plan for them separately (our winter livestock water guide covers daily needs). And in a real emergency, Oklahoma State is blunt: don't ration drinking water. Drink what you need today and look for more tomorrow.

Which containers are safe

The CDC calls unopened, commercially bottled water the safest and most reliable emergency supply. For water you bottle yourself, use food-grade containers: the CDC says FDA-approved food-grade containers are best, and otherwise a container with a tight lid, unbreakable material and ideally a narrow neck.

  • Good choices: purpose-made water containers (Utah State mentions 5-, 10- and 55-gallon sizes), and well-washed 2-liter soda bottles, which Oklahoma State says "work well." UF/IFAS also lists juice bottles; look for "food-grade" or "food-safe" on the label.
  • Glass is food-safe but breaks, and NMSU and Ohio State both prefer plastic, especially if water might freeze. Utah State says glass shouldn't be your only source.
  • Metal: NMSU advises against metal for long storage. Utah State says stainless steel is fine, but other metals only if they're coated and made for food or water.
Don't use these. Milk jugs: Oklahoma State says they're hard to clean and turn brittle; UF warns bacteria grow quickly in them, and Utah State says the milk proteins are hard to remove. Bleach jugs or anything that held chemicals: the CDC says never reuse a container that held toxic chemicals such as bleach or pesticides, and Ohio State explains that tiny amounts can stay in the plastic's pores. Waterbeds and pool liners: Utah State notes these plastics aren't approved for food or drinking water.

Cleaning and sanitizing containers

Even food-grade containers should be sanitized before filling. The CDC's method:

  1. Wash the container and lid with soap and water, then rinse.
  2. Mix 1 teaspoon of unscented household bleach per quart (4 cups) of water. Use plain bleach labeled 5–9% sodium hypochlorite, never scented, color-safe or "with cleaner" types.
  3. Pour the solution in, cap it and shake so it touches every inside surface.
  4. Wait, then pour it out. The CDC and UF say at least 30 seconds; NMSU says 30 minutes. Waiting longer does no harm.
  5. Let it air dry (CDC) or rinse with clean drinking water (NMSU, UF) before filling.

Filling, labeling and freezing

  • City tap water: NMSU, UF/IFAS, Oklahoma State and a 2021 Utah State Extension release all say water from a public supply doesn't need extra disinfecting before storage, unless there's a boil-water notice. (An older 2010 Utah State bulletin and Ohio State's fact sheet do suggest adding bleach; Utah State's figure is 8 drops, or 1/8 teaspoon, of 4–6% bleach per gallon.)
  • Well or spring water: treat it before storing. NMSU, UF and Oklahoma State all say untested private-well water should be disinfected first.
  • Label it. Mark each container "Drinking Water" with the date you filled it (CDC, UF).
  • Freezer space counts. UF and Utah State say you can freeze water in plastic containers if you leave 2–3 inches of headspace for expansion. Frozen jugs also help keep a freezer cold during an outage (see our power outage food safety guide).

Where to keep it

The CDC and NMSU both say cool (50–70°F) and out of direct sunlight, which slowly breaks down plastic. Keep stored water well away from gasoline, kerosene and pesticides: NMSU warns that their vapors can pass through plastic. Utah State Extension adds that containers sitting directly on concrete or dirt can pick up odors that affect the taste, so a shelf or a pallet is better than a garage floor. A basement or interior closet usually beats a hot attic or a sunny garage.

An easy habit is to rotate your water when clocks change in spring and fall. Rotate your bleach too: Utah State's 2010 bulletin says it can lose up to half its strength after 6 months, and the 2021 release says not to rely on bleach older than about 4 months for purifying water.

Water you already have in the house

If you're caught short, Oklahoma State and Utah State point to water already inside your home:

  • Water heater. Turn off the gas or electricity first, open a hot-water faucet, then drain from the valve at the bottom of the tank (a garden hose helps). Oklahoma State warns never to turn the heater back on while the tank is empty. Utah State suggests adding 8 drops of bleach per gallon and waiting 30 minutes.
  • Household pipes. Shut the main water valve, open the highest faucet in the house to let air in, and drain water from the lowest one (Oklahoma State).
  • Ice cubes in the freezer (Oklahoma State, Utah State).
  • Toilet tank, as a last resort only. Oklahoma State says use the tank, never the bowl, and disinfect it first. Skip it entirely if you use in-tank cleaners; Utah State Extension says tanks with added chemicals shouldn't be used for drinking.
  • Not for drinking: waterbeds and swimming pools (Utah State).

Treating water you're not sure about

If water is cloudy, let it settle or strain it through a coffee filter, paper towel or clean cloth first (NMSU, Oklahoma State). Then use one of these:

  • Boil it. NMSU says a rolling boil for 2 minutes, or 3 minutes above 6,500 feet; other extension guides give 3 to 5 minutes. Boiling is the best choice for parasites such as Giardia and Cryptosporidium, which Ohio State and NMSU note chlorine doesn't reliably kill. Pour boiled water between two clean containers a few times to improve the flat taste.
  • Use unscented bleach when you can't boil. Stir, wait 30 minutes, and check for a slight chlorine smell; if there's none, NMSU and Oklahoma State say to repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes.

These are emergency measures: NMSU notes that drinking water treated this way regularly isn't recommended, and Oklahoma State and Utah State point out that neither boiling nor bleach removes chemicals, nitrates or fuel contamination. If floodwater may have reached a private well, contact your local health department about testing before you drink from it.

The short version

Store at least 1 gallon per person per day for 3 days, and work toward 2 weeks, plus extra for pets. Use commercially bottled water or clean food-grade containers such as 2-liter soda bottles, never milk jugs or anything that held chemicals. Sanitize containers with 1 teaspoon of unscented bleach per quart of water, fill with city tap water, label with the date, and keep them cool, dark, off the floor and away from fuel. Replace home-filled water about every 6 months, and keep fresh bleach on hand to treat water if you have to.
