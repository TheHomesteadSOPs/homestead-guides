Building a Gingerbread House: A Beginner's Guide to Sturdy Dough, Safe Royal Icing, Assembly & Decorating

A gingerbread house that slumps, cracks or loses its roof is almost always a problem of dough, drying time or icing, not decorating skill. Build it like a little building: strong panels, a well-set frame, and a mortar that's safe for little hands to lick. Here's how, drawn from NC State, Utah State, Arkansas, Iowa State and Purdue Extension.

Start with a pattern, a base and a building recipe

  • Pick the design first. NC State Extension suggests choosing a style or theme before you start; patterns can be found online or drawn on paper or card stock.
  • Use a house recipe, not a cookie recipe. NC State says to look for "a recipe that is specifically for making a house." Construction gingerbread is firmer and holds its shape; it's made for building rather than eating.
  • Make a sturdy base. Stiff cardboard works well (NC State). Arkansas Extension covers thick cardboard with freezer paper, or uses a platter or cake board; Purdue's 4-H manual wraps a 3-layer cardboard base in foil taped on the underside.

  • Roll right on parchment, lay the pattern on top and cut with a pizza cutter (NC State). Utah State says not to use wax paper or cooking spray.
  • Cut doors and windows before baking (Arkansas). For candy "stained glass," Purdue's manual bakes the wall about halfway, fills the openings with crushed hard candy, then finishes baking; a full bake scorches the candy.
  • Trim while warm. Pieces spread a little in the oven. Arkansas says to trim uneven pieces before they cool and come off the sheet; Purdue suggests a sharp or serrated knife used like a saw. Lay the paper pattern back on top as your guide.
  • Let the panels dry hard. Utah State and Arkansas both leave the pieces out overnight; Purdue's manual gives them 48 hours on cooling racks before assembly. Dry panels are much stronger than fresh ones.

Royal icing: the mortar, made safely

Royal icing is egg white beaten with powdered sugar until it holds stiff peaks. It sets hard, which is why it works as glue. The catch is the egg. Iowa State's AnswerLine notes that raw egg whites can carry Salmonella, so icing made with them "may be a health risk," and Arkansas Extension doesn't recommend eating anything made with raw eggs. Since children will taste the icing, use one of these instead (Iowa State):

  • Meringue powder. Iowa State's version: 4 cups powdered sugar, 3 tablespoons meringue powder, and ⅓ cup plus 2 to 3 tablespoons warm water, beaten 8 to 10 minutes. Purdue's 4-H recipe is similar (3 tablespoons meringue powder, 1 lb sifted powdered sugar, 6 tablespoons water) and adds a tablespoon of light corn syrup to help it stick.
  • Pasteurized egg whites, dried or liquid, or pasteurized in-shell eggs.
  • Lightly cooked whites: heat the whites with sugar until they reach 160°F, checked with a thermometer, because there's no visual clue (Iowa State). Utah State's microwave method stops at 160°F and no higher than 175°F.
Keep the icing covered every minute you're not using it. Royal icing crusts fast. Utah State, Iowa State and Purdue all say to keep the bowl covered with a damp cloth or a tight lid. Keep grease away from it too (Purdue): a greasy bowl or spoon stops it whipping and setting. Leftover meringue-powder icing keeps about 2 weeks in the fridge in an airtight container (Iowa State, Purdue); Purdue gives egg-white icing only 1 week.

Putting the house together

  1. Mark the footprint. Arkansas suggests laying the wall pieces outside-down with their inside corners together, then piping a line of icing on the base to show where the house will sit.
  2. Set the first wall. Press one side wall into the icing line on the base.
  3. Add an end wall. Ice its side edges and press it against the first wall at a right angle. Repeat with the other two walls. Utah State's advice: "Use plenty of glue," and seal every crack.
  4. Prop it. Set cans against the walls to hold them square while the icing sets (Arkansas).
  5. Wait before the roof. Arkansas says to let the walls harden together for at least two hours before the roof goes on. Take the cans away first.
  6. Add the roof. Pipe a thick line of icing along the wall tops and press the roof panels on so they overhang for eaves. Let it set an hour, or better, overnight (Arkansas), before any heavy decorating.

Decorating

  • Let the structural icing dry before adding candy (NC State), and mix a fresh batch of icing for snow and candy (Arkansas).
  • Sort candy into a large muffin pan so it's easy to reach (NC State). Hard candies, sprinkles and gumdrops are classics.
  • Choose candy that holds up. Purdue's 4-H manual, written for houses on display in warm, humid fair weather, steers builders away from chocolate (it melts) and soft candies, toward candy canes, peppermints, gumdrops, ring candies, cereal squares and pretzels.
  • Humidity softens icing. Iowa State says not to refrigerate iced cookies because humidity affects the icing; the same goes for a finished house. Keep it in a cool, dry room out of direct sun.

Eat it, or keep it?

Decide before you build. If the house is for display only, Arkansas Extension says hot glue can help it stick together faster, but once there's glue on it, nobody should eat it. If you plan to break it apart and snack on it, skip the glue and use one of the egg-safe icings above. Construction gingerbread is made hard to stand up, not to taste its best, so many families bake a batch of soft gingerbread cookies alongside for eating.

The short version

Use a construction gingerbread recipe and a stiff, covered base. Chill the dough, roll it to the thickness your recipe gives (¼ in in Utah State's, about ⅛ in in Purdue's), cut openings before baking, trim pieces while warm, and let them dry overnight or longer. Make royal icing with meringue powder, pasteurized whites or whites heated to 160°F, and keep it covered with a damp cloth. Build the walls on an icing line, prop them with cans, wait at least two hours before the roof, and let the roof set before decorating. Use hot glue only on a house nobody will eat.
