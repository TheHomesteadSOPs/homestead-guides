Pruning Blueberry and Raspberry Bushes in Winter: A Beginner's Guide to Timing, Cane Selection & Renewal

Both berries get pruned in the same dormant-season window, but not the same way: a blueberry bush is a slow renovation project measured in years, while a raspberry row can be cut almost to the ground every single year. Here's what Oregon State, UConn, University of Minnesota and Iowa State Extension say about doing each one correctly.

Why bother pruning at all

It's tempting to leave berry bushes alone and let them fruit on their own. Oregon State University Extension's berry crops team puts the cost of skipping pruning plainly: "annual, moderate pruning leads to stronger plants, higher yields and larger berries." An unpruned or lightly pruned blueberry bush gets dense with weak, twiggy growth, producing small fruit and little of the strong new wood next year's crop depends on. Prune only occasionally and heavily instead, and you get the opposite problem: a flush of same-age canes that all become unproductive together, leaving nothing young to fall back on when you finally cut the old wood out.

When to prune each

  • Blueberries: prune during full dormancy. Oregon State puts the window at January through early March; UConn Extension says simply "late winter through early spring."
  • Raspberries and blackberries: University of Minnesota Extension says to cut spent canes "in the late fall or early spring when they are dormant." Iowa State University Extension gives a tighter spring window — March or early April — and a fall option after the first hard frost.

Pruning blueberries: the 18-cane rule

Start every year by removing the obvious problems, per Oregon State: low shoots that will touch the ground once loaded with fruit, short soft shoots that grew late in the season from the plant's base, and any cane damaged by winter injury, disease or insects. Cut to open up the center of the bush so sunlight reaches the whole canopy.

For the renewal cuts, UConn Extension gives a concrete target to aim a mature bush at: around 18 canes total, worked out as 3 canes kept per year of growth over 6 years (3 × 6 = 18). The yearly routine: remove the three oldest canes, and thin new growth back to three vigorous new canes to replace them. No cane should be older than six years — production drops off hard after that. If you'd rather keep it simple, Oregon State's version of the same idea is to remove the two oldest, least-productive canes every winter if you prune annually.

Two kinds of cuts. UConn distinguishes a thinning cut (an entire stem removed down to the base — the most important cut for blueberries) from a heading cut (only part of a stem removed). Heading cuts are for tidying up crossing lateral branches after the thinning cuts are done, not for the main renewal work.

If a bush is overbearing — loaded with many small berries — Oregon State's fix is to clip back some of the small shoots carrying the heaviest loads of flower buds; fewer, larger berries beat a bush buried in small ones. Flower buds sit near the tips of last year's growth and are large and plump; leaf buds are small and scale-like, a quick way to tell which bud you're cutting near.

Renewal pruning a neglected bush: if a bush has gone years without a prune, UConn says to remove at least a third, and up to all, of its canes in one go, confirm soil pH is in the blueberry-friendly 4.5–5.0 range, and fertilize, then return to the normal yearly routine once new shoots regrow.

Pruning raspberries: it depends which type you grow

Raspberries fruit on one of two cane types, and the whole pruning job turns on which one you're growing:

  • Floricane-fruiting (summer-bearing): canes grow one year as leafy, non-fruiting primocanes, overwinter, then fruit the following summer as second-year floricanes before dying.
  • Primocane-fruiting (fall-bearing/everbearing): canes fruit in their first fall, on the same growth they put up that spring.

Fall-bearing (primocane) raspberries: the simple route

Iowa State's "one-crop system" is the lowest-maintenance option: every spring, cut all canes back to ground level. University of Minnesota Extension says this can be done in late fall or early spring with a mower, sharp loppers or a hedge trimmer, and confirms the logic — "since the primocanes produce a full crop of fruit, there is no need to keep floricanes." Iowa State adds that this one-crop method actually yields a larger total fall crop than the alternative below, maturing 1–2 weeks earlier.

The alternative is a "two-crop system": leave some canes over winter so they fruit again as floricanes the next summer, giving you a small summer crop on top of the fall one. Minnesota's extension is candid that this isn't common practice and you're unlikely to see an overall yield benefit — the simple cut-everything-down approach usually wins unless you specifically want two harvest windows.

Summer-bearing (floricane) raspberries: a two-stage job

WhenWhat to do
Fall, after harvest (or early spring)Cut all spent floricanes (look for old, dried fruit clusters) down at ground level. Leave the first-year primocanes standing — they're next year's fruiting canes.
Spring (March–early April)Remove all weak, diseased and winter-damaged canes at ground level. Keep the most vigorous remaining canes — Iowa State's test is about ¼ inch in diameter measured 30 inches up the cane — spaced roughly 6 inches apart.

Finish the spring pass with a topping cut: if winter dieback was heavy, cut back to live tissue; if dieback was light, Iowa State says to remove just the top quarter of each cane, which keeps canes from going top-heavy and flopping over once loaded with fruit. Keep the whole row confined to a 1–2 foot wide hedgerow rather than letting it sprawl — both Minnesota and Iowa State extension give this same width.

Black and purple raspberries prune differently again

If you're growing black or purple raspberries rather than red, Iowa State's numbers are different from both red raspberry methods above: in spring, remove the small, weak canes and keep only 4 or 5 of the largest, most vigorous canes per clump. Then head back the lateral (side) branches to 12 inches for black raspberries or 18 inches for purple. In fall, remove the second-year canes that fruited, the same as red summer-bearing types.

Cleanup matters too

Iowa State's one consistent instruction across every raspberry type: remove the pruned canes from the garden and destroy them rather than composting them nearby, since old cane material can carry over diseases like anthracnose and spur blight into next year's planting.

The short version

Prune both berries while fully dormant: blueberries January–March, raspberries in late fall or early spring. On blueberries, remove damaged/weak/low wood every year and work toward roughly 18 canes on a mature bush with none older than 6 years, removing the 2–3 oldest and keeping 2–3 vigorous new ones annually. On raspberries, know which type you have: cut fall-bearing types entirely to the ground each year for the biggest harvest, or give summer-bearing types a two-stage prune — spent floricanes out at ground level after harvest, then thin and top the remaining canes in spring. Either way, haul the cuttings away rather than leaving them to rot in the row.
