Building Raised Garden Beds: A Beginner's Guide to Size, Lumber & Filling with Soil

Raised beds fix a lot of beginner garden problems at once: heavy clay, poor drainage, soil you'd rather not dig, and a back that complains about bending to ground level. They're also one of the best off-season projects on a homestead. Build and fill them this fall and the soil has all winter to settle before spring planting. Here's how big to make them, what to build them from, and how much soil you'll actually need.

Already have beds and just need to close them down for the season? See our guide to putting the garden to bed. This article is about building new ones.

Pick the spot first

University of Florida IFAS Extension's Gardening in Raised Beds (ENH1211) sets out three requirements for the site:

  • At least 6 to 8 hours of direct sun. Most vegetables need full sun to produce well.
  • Close to a water source. Raised beds dry out faster than in-ground beds (more on that below), so you'll be watering often.
  • Level ground, or ground you can easily level.

You don't need to dig out the grass. UF suggests smothering turf and weeds under cardboard or several layers of newspaper before setting the frame on top. If you do use an herbicide, make sure its label says it's safe for areas where you'll grow food.

How wide, how long, how tall

Width is the measurement that matters most. The whole point of a raised bed is that you never step in it and compact the soil, so you need to reach the middle from the edge. Iowa State University Extension's numbers:

  • Reachable from both sides: 3 to 4 feet wide.
  • Against a fence or wall (one side only): 1 1/2 to 2 feet wide.

UF IFAS puts it the same way: most people can comfortably reach about 2 feet toward the center, so 4 feet is the maximum width, and a 4 x 8 foot bed is the most common size.

Length is up to you. Iowa State says it depends on space and convenience. University of Arizona Extension's Jeff Schalau suggests splitting long runs into shorter beds, with about 24 feet as a practical maximum, because walking around a very long bed gets old fast.

Height depends on what you need:

  • 6 inches is UF's minimum. Iowa State calls a 6-inch-or-less bed a "temporary" bed that can be built right inside an existing garden without side supports.
  • About 12 inches is a common, affordable choice. The University of Arkansas's budget build uses stacked boards to reach 12 inches.
  • 18 to 24 inches is common in Arizona, per Schalau, and UF suggests up to 24 inches for gardeners with mobility challenges, since it brings the soil closer to hand height.

Leave room for paths. UF recommends at least 18 inches between beds, or 30 inches if you'll push a wheelbarrow. Iowa State and Arizona both suggest about 3 feet for wheelbarrows and carts.

What to build it from

This is where extension sources disagree, so here are the options and the trade-offs:

  • Cedar or redwood: naturally rot-resistant, and recommended by Iowa State, Arizona and the University of Arkansas. UF notes it costs more than treated lumber.
  • Modern pressure-treated lumber: UF IFAS recommends ACQ (alkaline copper quaternary) treated lumber for its long life and lower cost. Schalau explains that the EPA phased arsenic-based treatments out of consumer lumber in 2003, and newer treatments use copper compounds. Not everyone is comfortable with it in a food garden, though. Arkansas's budget-bed guide advises avoiding pressure-treated wood, and Schalau suggests using copper-treated lumber only where another material, such as galvanized sheet metal, sits between the wood and the soil.
  • Untreated pine or fir: cheap, but UF warns it can rot within about a year.
  • Stone, brick, concrete block, galvanized metal or recycled-plastic lumber: all on UF's and Arizona's lists of workable materials. Block and stone last essentially forever.
Never use railroad ties or old treated lumber. UF specifically says to avoid railroad ties and any pressure-treated lumber bought before January 2004, because of possible creosote and arsenic contamination. That includes salvaged boards of unknown age. If you're unsure, Iowa State suggests lining the inside walls with a woven geotextile fabric, which lets water drain while keeping soil and roots off the wood.

Building the frame

A raised bed is a simple box, but wet soil is heavy and pushes outward hard. The University of Arkansas Cooperative Extension's "Simple Raised Garden Bed on a Budget" build (8 feet long, 3 feet wide, 12 inches tall, in cedar) shows how to keep the sides straight:

  • Use 2-inch-thick boards. The Arkansas build used 1-inch boards to save money but recommends 2-inch lumber, which resists bowing from heavy, wet soil and lasts longer.
  • Put a post in each corner. A 2x4 (ripped down the middle in the Arkansas build) screwed into the inside of each corner holds the boards together. Metal corner brackets also work.
  • Brace long sides across the middle. On an 8-foot bed, a cross brace at the midpoint keeps the long sides from bulging. A shorter square bed, such as 4 x 4, puts less pressure on its sides.
  • Sink the posts a few inches into the ground for extra rigidity, then set the frame flush on the soil.
  • Choose rust-resistant screws. UF recommends stainless steel screws with pressure-treated lumber, with coated screws as the budget option.

The Arkansas presenter's materials (cedar lumber plus hardware) came in under $100, with another $20 to $30 for soil. Building took an afternoon, and placing and filling took another hour or two. Prices change, but that gives you a sense of scale.

Got gophers or moles? Schalau's advice for Arizona gardeners is to staple hardware cloth across the bottom of the frame before you fill it. It's far easier to do now than after the bed is full.

How much soil you need

UF IFAS gives the formula: length × width × depth, all in feet, divided by 27 = cubic yards. Bulk soil is sold by the cubic yard; bagged mix is usually labeled in cubic feet (just skip the divide-by-27 step).

Order a little extra. UF says to fill the bed right to the top, because it will settle by about 2 inches, and to top it off after every growing season as the organic matter breaks down.

What to fill it with

UF IFAS recommends a lightweight mix that's rich in organic matter and holds water while still draining and letting air reach the roots. You can buy it in bulk or by the bag, or blend your own from ingredients like compost, composted pine bark, composted manure, peat moss, perlite, vermiculite and topsoil. Two cautions from the sources:

  • Good topsoil is hard to find, Schalau notes, and bagged products are expensive and often very high in organic matter with little real mineral soil.
  • Check the pH. Depending on what goes into your mix, UF says you may need lime to bring the pH into range. A soil test settles it.

Before planting, UF suggests working a balanced fertilizer (such as 10-10-10 or a slow-release vegetable fertilizer) into the top 6 inches, at the rate the bag gives for your bed's square footage. Watering: the one downside

The good drainage that makes raised beds so easy to grow in also means they dry out fast. UF calls irrigation "critical" and recommends:

  • Low-volume watering such as soaker hoses or drip tubing, which saves water and keeps leaves dry.
  • Watering early in the morning, ideally on a timer.
  • Frequent, short watering for seedlings; longer, less frequent soaks for established plants so roots grow deep.

Lay soaker hose or drip line before you plant. It's much easier to run tubing through an empty bed than around a full one.

The short version

Put beds where they get 6 to 8 hours of sun and are close to water. Build them no wider than 4 feet (2 feet against a fence), any practical length, and 6 to 24 inches tall, with 18 to 36 inches of path between them. Use cedar, redwood, block or modern treated lumber (or line it), never railroad ties or pre-2004 treated wood. Use 2-inch boards, corner posts and a middle brace on long beds. Work out soil with length × width × depth ÷ 27, fill to the brim, and expect it to settle. Then plan on drip or soaker watering, because raised beds dry out quickly.
