Rendering Lard at Home: A Beginner's Guide to Leaf Lard, Cracklings, Straining & Storage

If you raised a pig or bought half a hog this fall, the butcher probably asked whether you wanted the fat. Say yes. Rendering it into lard takes a heavy pot, a low flame and an afternoon, and a University of Minnesota Extension guide notes that carefully rendered leaf fat yields about 90 to 91 percent lard. Here is which fat to use, the temperature to hold, how to tell when it's done, and how to keep it from going rancid.

Which pork fat to render

FatWhere it comes fromBest use
Leaf (kidney) fatThe soft, clean sheet of fat inside the body cavity, around the kidneysThe firmest, mildest lard; UMN suggests rendering it separately for baking and frying
Back fat / fatbackThe thick layer under the skin along the backGood all-purpose cooking lard; trim off the rind and any meat first
TrimmingsFat cut from roasts, chops and bellies on processing dayRender with back fat; remove every scrap of lean
Intestinal (caul and gut) fatFat around the intestinesStrong flavor; UMN says keep it out of cooking lard and use it for soap instead

Ask for the leaf fat by name on your cut sheet. Many processors toss it unless you request it. Our guide to buying a side of beef or half a hog walks through the rest of the cut sheet, and raising pigs for meat covers the hog itself. The same method works on beef fat (suet), which renders into tallow.

Start with fresh, cold fat

Lard quality is set before the pot ever goes on the stove. UMN Extension's lard-rendering folder says to chill pork fat promptly after butchering, hold it at 32 to 35°F, and render within 5 to 7 days. It adds that holding fat a long time hurts quality even in the freezer, so freeze it only if you must and render it soon after. If you have to wait a few days, don't grind it yet: spread it out in clean, shallow containers so it chills all the way through, cover it, and keep it out of the light and away from strong odors.

Equipment

  • A heavy pot. UMN recommends stainless steel or aluminum and warns against copper or rusty utensils, which speed rancidity. A heavy enameled or stainless stockpot, or a slow cooker for small batches, works well at home.
  • A thermometer that reads to at least 250°F (a candy or deep-fry thermometer).
  • A long spoon for stirring.
  • A strainer lined with cheesecloth. UMN calls for at least a double layer.
  • Clean jars or tins, washed, scalded and dried. Tempered canning jars handle hot fat.

How to render lard, step by step

  1. Trim. Cut away the rind (too much rind makes softer lard), all bits of meat (they brown and discolor the lard) and any soiled spots. UMN's trick for the rind: cut the fat into strips about 1 inch wide, then run the knife along the skin.
  2. Cut small. Cube the fat or run it through a grinder. Smaller pieces render faster and more evenly. If you grind, keep the blades sharp so the fat doesn't warm up and smear; partly frozen fat is easier to cut.
  3. Add a little water. Put a small amount of water in the bottom of the pot, as UMN advises, so the fat doesn't scorch before it starts to melt. The water cooks off during rendering.
  4. Heat low, then hold 235 to 245°F. Start on low heat and raise it gradually as the fat breaks down. UMN's target is a fat temperature between 235° and 245°F. Don't let it smoke or burn; scorched fat tastes scorched.
  5. Stir often. Frequent stirring prevents sticking and, per UMN, gives whiter lard. Expect several hours for a big pot.
  6. Know when it's done. The bubbling stops (that is the last of the water leaving), and the cracklings float. UMN describes finished cracklings as a medium chestnut color that crush easily. Over-rendering darkens the lard and lowers quality, so take it off the heat once you reach that point.
  7. Strain. Let the cracklings settle, then ladle or pour the liquid fat through the cheesecloth. UMN notes a small sprinkle of fine salt on floating cracklings helps them sink.
  8. Jar it and cool it fast. Strain into one big vessel first, stir gently to blend, then fill your jars. UMN says rapid cooling is best and warns that excessive stirring at this stage whips in air and brings on rancidity early.

Leaf lard rendered this way is snow-white and nearly flavorless once it sets. Back-fat lard is usually a little softer and more savory. Keep the two in separate jars and label them.

Hot-fat safety. Fat at 240°F burns badly and doesn't drip off skin the way water does. Use a pot no more than about two-thirds full, keep children and pets away, and never leave the stove. Keep water out of hot fat once the batch is dry, since even a few drops make it spatter. If the fat ever catches fire, turn off the heat and smother it with a lid or baking sheet. Never throw water on a grease fire.

Cracklings

The crisp bits left in the strainer are cracklings. Press out the extra fat, salt them while warm and eat them as a snack, or crumble them into cornbread, biscuits or a pan of greens. They don't keep long at room temperature; refrigerate or freeze them.

Storing lard so it doesn't go rancid

UMN's list of what makes lard keep poorly: fat that was already going off before rendering, water left in from under-rendering, overheating, too much stirring, containers exposed to heat and light, musty odors, and storage above 50°F. The fixes follow directly:

  • Render it dry. Water is the main enemy. Nebraska Extension notes that well-rendered tallow is usually less than 0.5 percent water, which is a big reason rendered fats keep better than butter.
  • Fill containers full and cover them well once the fat hardens, to limit air (UMN).
  • Keep it cool and dark. A cold pantry under 50°F, the fridge or the freezer.
  • Scrape cleanly when you use it. UMN says fat smeared up the sides of the container goes rancid first and speeds up spoilage of the rest.

How long it lasts depends on how well it was rendered and stored, and the sources don't give a single figure for home lard. For beef tallow, Montana State University Extension says weeks at room temperature, months in the fridge and up to a year in the freezer; Nebraska Extension is more generous, at months at room temperature and over a year refrigerated or frozen. For lard, the cautious approach is to keep a working jar in the fridge and freeze the rest. Throw out any lard with an off smell, off color or rancid taste.

Old-school extras from the UMN folder: it reports a USDA lab recommendation of stirring in 2 to 3 pounds of hydrogenated vegetable shortening per 50 pounds of lard just before the cracklings settle, to slow rancidity. For lard that is only slightly off, it suggests heating it with 3 or 4 thinly sliced potatoes per 10 to 15 pounds of lard until the potatoes are dark brown, then straining into clean containers. Very rancid lard can't be saved.

Using your lard

Leaf lard is the classic fat for flaky pie crust and biscuits. Back-fat lard is good for frying, roasting potatoes, refried beans and greasing pans. Swapping lard for butter or shortening in a recipe changes the water content and texture, so start with recipes written for lard. The short version

Ask your processor for the leaf fat, keep it at 32–35°F and render within a week. Trim off rind and meat, cube or grind it, add a splash of water, and heat slowly to 235–245°F, stirring often. It's done when the bubbling stops and the cracklings float and turn chestnut brown. Strain through double cheesecloth, jar it, cool it fast, and store it full, covered, cold and dark.
