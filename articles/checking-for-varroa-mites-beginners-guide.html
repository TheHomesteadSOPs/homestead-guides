Checking a Hive for Varroa Mites: A Beginner's Guide to the Alcohol Wash, Sugar Roll & Treatment Thresholds

Varroa mites are the single biggest reason backyard colonies die, and the trouble is that a hive can look perfectly healthy right up until it collapses. You can't count mites by eye on a frame of bees — most of them are hidden under the bees' bodies or sealed inside brood cells. The only way to know is to take a measured sample, count, and compare the number to a threshold. It takes about ten minutes, and it's the one test every beekeeper should run.

New to bees? Start with our beekeeping guide for the first-year basics. This article goes deep on the one skill that decides more colony deaths than any other: knowing how many mites you actually have.

Why you can't just look for mites

Virginia Cooperative Extension describes the varroa life cycle in two stages. In the travelling stage, adult female mites ride on adult bees — and they prefer young nurse bees, which is why every sampling method below takes bees from the brood nest. In the reproductive stage, a female slips into an uncapped brood cell just before it's sealed, then lays her eggs on the developing bee once the cell is capped. A single female can reproduce up to seven times in her life.

The damage isn't only the feeding. Mites spread viruses, and deformed wing virus is now the most common of them. VCE notes that over 99% of bees carrying the virus show no symptoms at all, so by the time you see bees with crumpled, useless wings walking on the landing board, the mite load is already serious. Oregon State University Extension is blunt about it: visual inspection of adult bees is not a reliable way to monitor for varroa.

If you're seeing bees with shriveled, deformed wings, don't wait for a scheduled test — sample that colony now. Visible deformed wings in European honey bees are a key sign of a heavy mite load.

Taking the sample the right way

Both sampling methods start the same way, and the setup matters more than which method you pick:

  • Pick a brood-nest frame. Take bees from a frame of open or capped worker brood covered in nurse bees — that's where mite counts run highest, so it's the honest number.
  • Find the queen first. Make sure she is not on the frame you're sampling. Penn State Extension suggests shaking the bees into a plastic tub and checking again before you scoop. An alcohol wash kills every bee in the jar, so this step is not optional.
  • Measure half a cup of bees. Bump the bees into one corner of the tub and scoop with a half-cup measuring cup. Oregon State and Penn State both count this as about 300 bees; Mississippi State's guide counts it closer to 400. Whichever you use, use the same number every time so your results are comparable.

Method 1: the alcohol wash (most accurate)

The alcohol wash kills the bees in the sample, but it dislodges more mites than any other field method, so it's the one most extension services recommend. Mississippi State University Extension's version:

  1. Pour your half cup of nurse bees into a jar with enough 70% rubbing alcohol (or washer fluid) to cover them.
  2. Seal the jar and swirl or agitate it firmly for 3–5 minutes.
  3. Pour the liquid through a mesh screen (a jar lid with 1/8-inch hardware cloth works) into a white dish, so the bees stay behind and the mites wash through.
  4. Repeat the wash until no more mites come out, and add up the mites from every rinse.
  5. Divide the mites by the number of bees. MSU's own example: 28 mites ÷ 400 bees = 7%.

Losing 300 bees sounds like a lot, but a strong summer colony has tens of thousands, and it replaces that many workers in well under a day. Losing the colony to mites costs far more.

Method 2: the powdered-sugar roll (bees survive)

If you'd rather not kill the sample, the sugar roll works on the same principle: a fine coat of powdered sugar stops the mites from gripping, and they fall off. Penn State Extension's step-by-step:

  1. Pour the half cup of bees into a clean, dry pint jar and cover it with a mesh lid (hardware cloth with eight squares per inch).
  2. Add about two tablespoons of fresh, dry powdered sugar through the mesh, enough to coat every bee.
  3. Let the jar sit in the shade for one minute, then gently roll it for two minutes so the bees work through the sugar.
  4. Turn the jar upside down over a white tray and shake it hard for 60 seconds.
  5. Count the mites in the sugar. If they're hard to spot, mist the sugar lightly with water to dissolve it.
  6. Return the sugar-coated bees to the hive — their sisters will clean them off.

The trade-off: a sugar roll is gentler but typically misses more mites than an alcohol wash, especially in humid weather when the sugar clumps. If your sugar-roll number lands close to a threshold, treat it as over.

What the number means: seasonal thresholds

Your result is mites per 100 bees (the same as a percentage). The Honey Bee Health Coalition's Tools for Varroa Management guide, written with university bee researchers, sets the action point by where the colony is in its year:

Colony phaseRoughly when (northern U.S.)OK for nowTreat promptly
Dormant (sample just before clustering)Late fall to winterUnder 1%Over 1%
Population increaseSpringUnder 2%Over 2–3%
Peak populationSummerUnder 2%Over 3%
Population decreaseLate summer to fallUnder 2%Over 2–3%

Mississippi State uses a simpler rule of thumb that lines up: 3 mites per 100 bees from May through October is a research-based treatment threshold. In practical terms, with a 300-bee sample, finding more than 6 to 9 mites in late summer means it's time to act. Penn State's sugar-roll guidance says the same thing a different way: more than 6 mites in a half-cup sample calls for action, and 2% or less is acceptable.

When to sample

  • At a minimum, twice a year. Mississippi State recommends early in the year and again no later than early-to-mid July in its region, so there's time to treat before the colony raises its winter bees.
  • Late summer is the one you can't skip. Oregon State points to late summer into September, when mite numbers are climbing while the bee population is shrinking — the worst combination for the colony.
  • After every treatment. Sample again a few weeks later to confirm the treatment worked. Mites in some areas are resistant to some products, and a follow-up count is the only way to know.

Choosing a treatment that fits the season

Mississippi State's guide calls for an integrated approach — treat only when a count says you need to, and rotate products, because constant use of the same chemical breeds resistant mites. The Honey Bee Health Coalition guide gives the conditions that decide which product will work. Always follow the label of the specific product you buy; it's the legal and safety authority.

Notice the pattern: oxalic acid is close to useless while there's lots of brood, because most mites are sealed in cells. That's why it's the classic late-fall treatment, once the queen has slowed or stopped laying — see our winterizing guide for how that fits the fall-to-winter calendar.

Never use a treatment that isn't approved for honey supers while supers are on the hive. Pull the honey first, or choose a product the label allows during a honey flow.

Helpers that aren't treatments on their own

  • Drone brood removal: mites favor drone cells, so pulling capped drone comb before it hatches (on about a 28-day cycle in spring and summer) removes mites with it. The HBHC guide rates it as less effective alone; repeated 2–3 times in spring, it helps.
  • Brood breaks: caging the queen for 1–2 weeks or splitting the colony pauses the mites' breeding. It's not a stand-alone treatment, but it makes a follow-up oxalic treatment far more effective.
  • Screened bottom boards: perhaps 5–10% effective in northern states, per the HBHC guide. Fine to have, not something to rely on.

The short version

Take half a cup of nurse bees from the brood nest (never the queen), wash them in alcohol or roll them in powdered sugar, and divide the mites by the bees. Over 2–3 mites per 100 bees in late summer and fall means treat now with a product that fits the temperature and whether supers are on. Test at least in spring and late summer, and again after every treatment. Write every count down — the trend from one test to the next tells you more than any single number.
