Canning Apples and Pie Filling Safely: A Beginner's Guide to Clear Jel, Headspace & Processing Times

A good apple harvest can turn into pie all winter — but a jar of home-canned pie filling only stays safe if it's thickened, packed and processed exactly one way. Here's what the National Center for Home Food Preservation and North Dakota State University Extension say about Clear Jel, headspace, and water-bath times for apple, blueberry, cherry and peach filling.

Why Clear Jel, and not cornstarch or flour

It's tempting to thicken pie filling the way you would a pie on the stovetop, with cornstarch or flour. Don't. North Dakota State University Extension's canning bulletin is direct about why: "Clear Jel is a starch modified to produce excellent sauce consistency even after fillings are canned and baked. Other available household starches break down, causing a runny sauce consistency when used in pie fillings."

That's a quality problem you can taste, but it points at a safety problem too: an ordinary starch that breaks down during the long boiling-water processing time can leave the jar's center too thin and too cool relative to a properly thickened filling, which is exactly the kind of uneven heating that safe canning times are built to avoid. Clear Jel is a modified food starch bred to hold its thickening power through the heat of processing (and a later pie bake), which is why it's the only thickener the National Center for Home Food Preservation's (NCHFP) tested recipes call for. It's sold at many canning-supply retailers and online — don't substitute instant Clear Jel, cornstarch, tapioca or flour for the cook-type Clear Jel these recipes specify, since only the tested recipe has been proven safe at these processing times.

Don't change the lemon juice. NDSU's bulletin is specific that the sugar and spice amounts can be adjusted to taste (make one quart, bake a test pie, then adjust next time) — but the lemon juice amount should not be altered, because it helps control the acidity that keeps the filling safe and stable in storage.

Apple pie filling, the tested recipe (NCHFP)

This is the National Center for Home Food Preservation's recipe, adapted from USDA Agriculture Information Bulletin No. 539. Use firm, crisp apples — Stayman, Golden Delicious and Rome are named as suitable varieties. If your apples aren't very tart, NCHFP says to add an extra ¼ cup of lemon juice for every 6 quarts of apple slices.

Blanched, sliced fresh apples (½-inch slices)3½ cups6 quarts
Granulated sugar¾ cup + 2 Tbsp5½ cups
Clear Jel¼ cup1¾ cups
Ground cinnamon½ tsp1 Tbsp
Cold water½ cup2½ cups
Apple juice¾ cup5 cups
Bottled lemon juice2 Tbsp¾ cup
Nutmeg (optional)⅛ tsp1 tsp
  1. Wash, peel and core apples; cut into ½-inch slices and hold in an ascorbic-acid dip so they don't brown.
  2. Working in batches of 6 cups, blanch the apple slices in 1 gallon of boiling water for 1 minute after the water returns to a boil. Drain and keep the hot slices covered.
  3. In a large kettle, combine the sugar, Clear Jel and cinnamon with the water and apple juice (add food coloring or nutmeg here if using). Cook over medium-high heat, stirring, until the mixture thickens and starts to bubble.
  4. Add the lemon juice and boil 1 minute, stirring constantly.
  5. Fold the drained apple slices into the hot syrup right away, and fill jars immediately, leaving 1 inch of headspace.
  6. Wipe rims, add lids, and process at once in a boiling-water canner for the time in the table below — don't let filled jars sit and cool before processing.
AltitudeProcessing time (pints or quarts)
0–1,000 ft25 minutes
1,001–3,000 ft30 minutes
3,001–6,000 ft35 minutes
Above 6,000 ft40 minutes

NCHFP's table doesn't split the time by jar size — the same times apply whether you're filling pints or quarts. Use a hot pack (fruit and syrup both hot when jars are filled) and a full boiling-water canner, lids resting under at least 1–2 inches of water the whole time.

Blueberry, cherry and peach fillings (NDSU)

Once you have the apple method down, the other common fruit fillings use the same Clear Jel technique with different quantities. These per-quart amounts and water-bath times are from North Dakota State University Extension's fruit pie filling bulletin; scale up for a larger batch the same way the apple recipe does.

All four use 1 inch of headspace and the same altitude step-ups as the apple recipe (add 5 minutes per altitude band above 1,000 ft). The peach version has one extra step: after adding the lemon juice, NDSU says to keep heating the mixture for 3 more minutes before folding in the fruit.

Using frozen fruit instead of fresh? NDSU says to use unsweetened fruit, rinse off any added sugar while it's still frozen, and use some of the fruit's own thawing juice in place of part of the water — but cut the Clear Jel back to ¼ cup per quart (1¾ cups for 7 quarts) regardless of fruit type, since thawed fruit releases extra liquid.

Taste-testing before you commit a whole batch

NDSU's own advice: before canning a full batch, make a single test quart, bake it into a pie, and taste it. Sugar and spice (cinnamon, nutmeg) can be adjusted up or down to your family's taste on the next batch — but as the callout above says, leave the lemon juice exactly as written. Canned pie filling is best used within a year, and stays safe as long as the lid remains vacuum sealed.

The short version

Only use Clear Jel (not cornstarch, flour, or instant Clear Jel) for canned pie filling, because ordinary starches break down during processing and leave a runny, unevenly heated jar. Follow a tested recipe's exact Clear Jel, sugar and lemon juice amounts, use a hot pack, leave 1 inch of headspace, and process in a boiling-water canner for the full time for your altitude — 25 minutes at sea level for apple, 30 for blueberry, cherry or peach, adding 5 minutes per altitude band above 1,000 feet. Don't alter the lemon juice, and test one quart before committing a whole harvest.
