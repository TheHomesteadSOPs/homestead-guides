Buying a Side of Beef or Half a Hog: A Beginner's Guide to Hanging Weight, Take-Home Meat & Freezer Space

Buying meat straight from a farm by the quarter, half or whole animal can fill a freezer for a year, but the quotes come in a language most first-time buyers haven't learned yet: live weight, hanging weight, processing fees and cut sheets. The number that surprises people most is how much smaller the pile of packages is than the animal. Here's how university Extension meat specialists say to work out what you'll actually bring home, what it really costs per pound, and how much freezer you need.

Three weights, three different numbers

Every bulk-meat conversation uses three weights, and mixing them up is the most common beginner mistake:

  • Live weight is the animal on the hoof. Animals also lose a little weight in transport ("shrink").
  • Hanging weight (hot carcass weight) is the carcass right after slaughter, with the head, hide, feet and organs removed for beef (UW–Madison), and the organs and head removed but usually the skin on for pork (UMaine). It then loses a few more percent of moisture as it chills and ages: about 2 to 5% for beef (UW–Madison, UMaine).
  • Take-home weight is what actually comes back in packages after bone, extra fat and trim are removed. This is the number that fills your freezer.

The share of live weight that becomes carcass is called the dressing percentage. For beef it's typically about 58 to 64% (UMaine; Kansas State says 60 to 64%). UMaine notes grass-finished cattle dress about 5% lower than grain-finished, and dairy crosses 3 to 5% lower. Hogs dress much higher, about 70 to 75% (UW–Madison, Kansas State), partly because the skin usually stays on; UMaine gives 64 to 75% for heritage breeds.

How much meat you actually take home

From the carcass to the freezer you lose bone, fat trim and moisture. Kansas State's guideline:

Beef65 to 70% of carcass weight55 to 60% of carcass weight
Pork75 to 80% of carcass weight65 to 70% of carcass weight

A handy rule of thumb from those numbers: expect roughly 40% of a steer's live weight and roughly half of a hog's live weight back as packaged meat. UMaine's own example, which assumes less trim, comes out higher (about 47% for a grass-fed steer), so a quote from your processor beats any rule of thumb.

A half is simply half of those numbers: roughly 240 lb of beef from a half of the UW–Madison steer, or 60 to 75 lb of pork from half a hog.

What changes your yield

  • Fat and muscling. A leaner, heavier-muscled animal yields more meat per pound of carcass (UW–Madison). UMaine notes that trimming outside fat down to a quarter inch lowers the pounds you get back.
  • Boneless vs. bone-in. Boneless cuts weigh less but you aren't losing any meat, just bone. They also pack into less freezer space (UW–Madison).
  • Ground beef. Ground beef is made from trim; asking for leaner ground (such as 90/10) means fewer total pounds (UW–Madison, Kansas State).
  • Aging. Longer aging, and especially dry aging, loses more moisture and trim (Kansas State).
  • Cured and smoked items. Bacon, ham and sausage lose weight in curing and cooking (Kansas State).
  • The extras. Keeping the liver, heart, tongue, soup bones or fat for lard adds to what you bring home (UW–Madison).

Quarter, half or whole: what you're actually getting

A "quarter" of beef isn't always the same thing. UW–Madison explains that a true forequarter holds the chuck, rib and brisket, while a hindquarter holds the loin, round and flank, so a hindquarter has no ribeyes or chuck roasts. Many farms sell "split halves" instead, giving each quarter buyer a mix from the front and back. Ask which one you're buying before you compare prices.

Working out the real price per pound

Most farms quote a price per pound of hanging weight, and the processor usually bills separately for slaughter, cutting and wrapping (SDSU), with curing and smoking often extra. That's why a low hanging-weight price isn't the whole story. To compare a farm half with store prices, add everything up and divide by take-home pounds:

Real cost per pound = (price per lb × hanging weight + all processing fees) ÷ take-home pounds.
Example with made-up numbers: if a half beef ends up costing $1,800 all in, and you take home 240 lb of packaged beef, you paid $7.50 per pound, for a mix of steaks, roasts and ground. Use your own farm's and processor's figures.

SDSU suggests asking the farmer for the animal's estimated live weight up front, and asking the processor about their fees, scheduling and how long they age the carcass.

How much freezer space you need

UMaine and SDSU both use about 1 cubic foot of freezer space for every 35 to 40 lb of packaged meat. UMaine uses a more cautious 30 lb per cubic foot for pork, and SDSU notes that odd-shaped cuts take more room.

Filling out the cut sheet

The processor will give you a cut sheet (or call you) to ask how you want the animal cut. A few tips from the Extension guides:

  • Decide on thickness and package size. SDSU suggests specifying steak and chop thickness and how many per package to suit your household.
  • Think about how you cook. More roasts and ground means fewer steaks. SDSU notes meat from older animals is better suited to ground and slow-cooked cuts.
  • Don't over-trim pork. UW–Madison warns heavy trimming can hurt bacon quality.
  • Ask for the extras you'll use: organ meats, soup bones, or the leaf fat if you want to render lard.
  • Ask about aging for beef, and whether hams and bellies can be cured and smoked (and what that costs).

Storing and thawing it safely

At 0°F, frozen meat stays safe indefinitely; it's quality, not safety, that slowly declines (SDSU). For best quality, SDSU suggests using ground meat within 3 to 4 months and steaks, chops and roasts within about 12 months; UMaine gives 6 to 12 months for beef. Thaw in the refrigerator on a low shelf (1 to 4 days for larger cuts) or in a sealed bag in cold water, never on the counter (SDSU). And because a full freezer is a big investment, it's worth knowing ahead of time what to do if the power goes out; see our power outage food safety guide.

The short version

Hanging weight is the carcass, not your meat. Expect to take home roughly 40% of a steer's live weight and about half of a hog's. A half beef is often around 240 to 280 lb of packaged meat and needs 6 to 8 cubic feet of freezer; a half hog is about 60 to 75 lb and 1.5 to 2.5 cubic feet. Add the farm's price and every processing fee, divide by take-home pounds, and you have the number to compare with the grocery store.
