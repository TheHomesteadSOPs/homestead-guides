How Much Hay Do You Need for Winter? A Beginner's Guide to Calculating Hay for Goats, Sheep & Cows

Running out of hay in February is one of the most stressful (and expensive) mistakes a new livestock keeper can make. By then local supplies are thin and prices are up. The fix is a simple calculation you can do on the back of an envelope this fall: how much each animal eats per day, how many days you'll be feeding, and how much you'll lose to storage and waste. Here's how extension specialists work it out, scaled down for a small homestead herd.

Still working out how many animals your land can carry in the first place? Try our free livestock per acre calculator. This guide is about feeding the animals you already have through the months when pasture can't.

Step 1: How much does each animal eat per day?

Livestock appetite is measured as dry matter intake, a percentage of body weight eaten per day with the water taken out. Washington State University livestock specialist Dr. Susan Kerr gives these ranges (published through Oregon State University's Small Farms program):

AnimalDaily dry matter intake (% of body weight)
Goats2 to 6%
Sheep2 to 5%
Beef cattle1 to 3%
Dairy cattle3 to 4.5%
Horses1.5 to 3%

Those ranges are wide because intake depends on the animal's stage of life. A dry, open doe or ewe sits near the low end. A heavily pregnant or milking animal sits near the top. Kerr notes that nutrients should increase gradually in the last three months of pregnancy, and that cold weather raises maintenance needs too. For a mixed group of adults on a winter hay diet, many extension calculations use 2 to 2.5% as a working figure (West Virginia University Extension uses 2 to 2.5% for cows, rising to 2.6 to 2.8% after calving). Pick the higher end if your animals will be milking, bred or facing a hard winter.

Then convert dry matter to hay as you'll actually weigh it. Hay isn't 100% dry. WVU puts grass hay at 90 to 93% dry matter; University of Georgia Extension uses about 85%. Divide your dry matter figure by that decimal (for example, ÷ 0.90) to get pounds of hay "as fed."

Step 2: How many days will you be feeding hay?

This is the number beginners most often underestimate. Kerr says hay may need to be fed from October through March, perhaps into April. The worked examples in extension calculators range from about 120 days (UGA: Thanksgiving to April) to 167 days (WVU: November 1 to mid-April) and 175 days (University of Connecticut). UConn's advice is to estimate your feeding period and consider overestimating it.

Use your own local experience if you have it: when does your pasture stop growing in fall, and when is it truly ready for grazing in spring? Kerr cautions against turning animals out too early in spring, because grazing new growth too soon damages the pasture for the rest of the year. A late spring can easily add two or three weeks to your hay bill.

Step 3: Add waste (it's bigger than you think)

Not every pound you buy ends up in an animal. Hay is lost twice: once in storage and again at the feeder.

Storage losses. Oklahoma State University extension engineer Raymond Huhnke measured dry matter losses for round bales by storage method:

Storage methodLoss, up to 9 months
Enclosed barnunder 2%
Under a roof (open shed)2 to 5%
Covered, raised off the ground2 to 4%
Covered, on the ground5 to 10%
Uncovered, raised off the ground3 to 15%
Uncovered, on the ground5 to 20%

Losses climb steeply if hay sits outside for 12 to 18 months (up to 15 to 50% uncovered on the ground). Huhnke explains that most of the spoilage happens in the outer 2 to 8 inches of a bale. On a small 4-foot bale, that outer layer holds a big share of the hay. If bales must sit outside, his recommendations are to keep them off the ground on pallets or about 6 inches of crushed rock, butt them end to end in north-south rows, leave at least 3 feet between rows, and pick a sunny, breezy, well-drained spot.

Feeding losses. UGA Extension's Steve Morgan cites classic research comparing feeding methods:

Feeding methodHay wasted
Small square bale in a rack7%
Large round bale in a ring9%
Large round bale with no ring45%

Put together, extension sources suggest adding anywhere from 10 to 50% for waste (Kerr), with about 15% a reasonable allowance for barn-stored hay fed from a ring or rack (UGA, UConn). If your hay sits outside uncovered or animals can walk on it, plan on much more.

Goats are notorious hay wasters. They pull hay out of a feeder, drop it, and then won't eat what's been on the ground. A feeder with a lid or narrow slots, kept off the ground and under cover, pays for itself quickly. Our goat & sheep shed guide covers feeder space per animal.

Step 4: Weigh your bales

Bale weights vary enormously by baler, hay type and how tightly they were packed. WVU lists 4x4 round bales at 400 to 600 lb, 4x5 rounds at 700 to 1,000 lb, and 5x6 rounds at 1,200 to 1,700 lb. UGA's Lincoln County agent stresses that bales should actually be weighed, because people often overestimate them. For small square bales, stand on a bathroom scale holding a few bales from your supplier, subtract your own weight and average them. Then you can count in bales instead of pounds.

Putting it together: a worked example

Say you keep 4 adult goats averaging 130 lb, and you plan for a 150-day hay season with hay stored in a barn and fed from a rack:

StepMathResult
Herd body weight4 × 130 lb520 lb
Daily dry matter (3%)520 × 0.0315.6 lb
Daily hay as fed (90% dry)15.6 ÷ 0.90about 17.3 lb
Season total17.3 × 150 daysabout 2,600 lb
Plus 15% waste2,600 × 1.15about 3,000 lb
Bales (if yours weigh 50 lb)3,000 ÷ 50about 60 bales

The 3% figure is a middle-of-the-range assumption for goats, not a rule. Rerun the numbers at 2.5% and 4% to see your low and high cases, and buy toward the high one if you have does kidding in late winter.

For scale, Kerr's own example for a single 1,100 lb beef cow at 2% of body weight over a 182-day winter comes to about 24 lb of hay a day and 2.2 tons for the season, before waste. A family milk cow will eat more than that.

Beyond quantity: quality, water and minerals

  • Test your hay. Kerr recommends a chemical analysis to guide feeding decisions. Your extension office can tell you how to sample bales. Hay under about 8% crude protein generally needs a protein supplement, she notes.
  • Plan for late pregnancy. Bred does and ewes need more feed in the last trimester, which usually falls in late winter on a homestead.
  • Water and minerals. Kerr lists free-choice water and trace mineral salt as basics. Animals eat less hay when water freezes, so keep it open. Use a mineral made for your species. Sheep need a sheep mineral, as our sheep guide explains.
  • Watch body condition. Kerr's final step is to monitor health, body condition and feed costs through the winter and adjust as you go. The calculation is a starting point, not a substitute for looking at your animals.
Buying the whole winter's hay at once, in fall, can save money, but Kerr notes it means knowing how much to buy, having somewhere dry to store it, and having the cash up front. If you can't store it all, line up a supplier now for mid-winter pickups.

The short version

Multiply each animal's weight by its daily intake (2 to 6% for goats, 2 to 5% for sheep, 1 to 3% for beef cattle), divide by about 0.9 to convert to hay as fed, and multiply by your feeding days (often 120 to 180). Add about 15% for waste if you store hay in a barn and feed from a rack or ring, more if it sits outside. Divide by your real, weighed bale weight. Then buy toward your high estimate, because running short in February costs more than storing a few extra bales.

Track feed, weights and costs all winter
