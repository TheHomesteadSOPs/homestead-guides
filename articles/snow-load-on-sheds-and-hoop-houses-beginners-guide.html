Snow Load on Sheds, Coops & Hoop Houses: A Beginner's Guide to Roof Weight, Warning Signs & Safe Snow Removal

A foot of fresh powder on the shed roof looks harmless. A foot of wet, settled snow with a layer of ice underneath can weigh several times as much, and farm buildings, backyard coops and plastic hoop houses are usually built lighter than a house. Here's how to estimate what's on your roof, the warning signs that it's too much, and how to clear it without getting hurt, drawn from NDSU, UVM, Kentucky, NC State, Illinois and Nebraska Extension.

How much does snow on a roof actually weigh?

The depth of snow tells you less than its type. North Dakota State University Extension agricultural engineer Ken Hellevang gives these working figures:

Snow typeWeight per cubic foot1 foot deep on the roof
Light, fluffy new snowAbout 7 lbAbout 7 lb per sq ft
Average snowAbout 15 lbAbout 15 lb per sq ft
Drifted or compacted snow20 lb or more20+ lb per sq ft
IceHellevang notes ice "adds weight rapidly." Treat any ice layer as a serious extra load.

UVM Extension's greenhouse fact sheet uses a second, handy method: think in terms of rain. A foot of light, fluffy snow may hold only about 1 inch of water, while 3 to 4 inches of heavy, wet snow can hold the same inch. Every inch of water-equivalent adds about 5.2 pounds per square foot, which UVM says is roughly 6.5 tons on a 25 x 96 foot greenhouse.

A quick example: an 8 x 12 foot shed has about 96 square feet of roof footprint. With a foot of average snow (about 15 lb per sq ft), that's roughly 1,440 pounds sitting on the rafters. Let it settle and add rain on top, and the same depth can climb toward 20 lb per sq ft or more.

What farm buildings are built to hold

Hellevang's numbers for North Dakota give a sense of scale: house roofs there are typically designed for about 30 to 40 pounds per square foot, while agricultural buildings are often designed for only about 20 to 35, and many "are often not built to a specified load" at all. A homemade coop, run cover or garden shed may be lighter still. Design loads vary a lot by region, so check your local building department's snow load figure if you're building, and assume an older or DIY outbuilding has less margin than your house.

Hellevang's rough line in the sand: more than "a couple of feet of compacted snow" may be excessive. He's also honest that deciding whether to clear a roof is "an educated guess" unless an engineer or building official has looked at it.

Where snow loads get dangerous

  • Drifts. NDSU warns that wind piles deep drifts downwind of nearby buildings and trees, and that a lower roof next to a taller one (a lean-to on a barn, a coop against a shed) collects drifted snow.
  • Uneven loads. UVM notes that snow drifted onto one side, or snow piled between two closely spaced structures, makes collapse more likely than the same weight spread evenly.
  • Rain on snow, and ice. Snow soaks up rain like a sponge, adding weight without adding depth.
  • Flat or low-pitch roofs. Snow slides off steep metal roofs on its own; it sits on flat ones.

Warning signs of an overloaded roof

NDSU's clearest warning: if rafters or trusses are "bending downward or flexing to the side, the roof is in danger." Look from inside the building with a flashlight. Also watch for a ridgeline that has started to sag, new cracking or creaking sounds, and, on a hoop house, bows or end walls that are bowing out of line (Kentucky's Center for Crop Diversification flags wall bowing as a sign to pull snow back).

If a roof is visibly sagging or creaking, get people and animals out first. Move livestock to another shelter and stay out from under it yourself. No shed, coop or tunnel is worth an injury. As NC State Extension puts it, "your safety is more important than any structure." Also check your insurance before winter: NDSU notes snow damage isn't covered by all policies, and on farm buildings it may need a separate rider.

Clearing snow from a shed, coop or barn roof safely

  1. Work from the ground with a roof rake whenever you can. NDSU recommends it over climbing up.
  2. If you must go up, set up properly. Anchor the ladder, wear footwear with good grip, and hire help if you're not comfortable on a roof. NDSU singles out metal roofs as treacherous, and warns to watch for power lines.
  3. Shovel down-slope, in the direction the shingles lie. Cold makes shingles brittle and strips granules, so don't chip at the roof surface. Leaving a thin layer is fine.
  4. Clear both sides. Don't strip one side of a gable roof and leave the other loaded; that's the uneven load you're trying to avoid.
  5. Watch where it lands. Keep animals, people and equipment clear of the drip line, and don't pile cleared snow against walls or doors where it blocks ventilation or drifts back.

Hoop houses and high tunnels need a different plan

Plastic-covered tunnels and backyard hoop houses are the structures most likely to fail in a big storm. Extension advice for them:

  • Start early. Illinois Extension says "don't wait until after a snow or ice event to begin the removal process." An inch or two probably won't cause damage, but heavier accumulations become a problem fast. Kentucky suggests checking tunnels overnight during major storms.
  • Watch for sagging plastic. Kentucky's Center for Crop Diversification says the main cause of collapse is plastic sagging between purlins, letting snow pocket. Keep the plastic tight, and on an unheated double-layer tunnel keep the inflation blower running during storms.
  • Knock snow off from inside. Push gently up on the plastic with a soft shop broom, or a painter's pole with a tennis ball on the end (NC State, Kentucky, Illinois), taking care not to puncture it. From outside, a snow broom on a long handle works from the peak down.
  • Keep it balanced. UVM recommends starting in the middle of the tunnel and working toward both ends, unloading each side as you go, because clearing one side while the other stays loaded twists the bows.
  • Heat if you have it. UVM says heated houses should turn the thermostat up to 70°F or higher before heavy snow. NC State notes that on a heated double-poly house, deflating the layers lets more heat reach the outer plastic to melt snow; UVM suggests the same for ice. Unheated tunnels should keep their inflation running instead.
  • Prop it up. For unheated tunnels, NC State suggests wedging 2x4 or 4x4 posts between the ground and the purlins or bows before a storm. UVM recommends diagonal braces near each corner.
  • Remove shade cloth before winter (snow sticks to it) and close end walls and sides; Illinois notes closed tunnels trap heat and hold less snow. Kentucky advises turning off automatic roll-up sides before a storm.
  • Last resort: cut the plastic. If collapse is imminent and you can't clear it, UVM and NC State both say to slit the plastic and let the snow fall in. Replacing a cover and a crop costs far less than replacing the frame.

Kentucky also passes along a common grower rule of thumb: don't bother clearing snow from the outside base until it reaches the hip board, though the type of snow matters more than the depth. Four feet of fluffy snow weighs far less than four feet of wet snow or ice.

Build for snow next time

  • Pick a pointed shape. NC State and Illinois both say Gothic (peaked) tunnels shed snow better than round Quonset styles. UVM notes Quonsets are weakest at the ridge and Gothics at mid-rafter.
  • Space hoops closer. Nebraska Extension (EC308) says hoops at 4, 5 or 6 feet apart, with narrower spacing shedding snow more easily; wide, flat tunnels shed poorly and are more prone to collapse. Illinois strongly recommends trusses across the bows.
  • Mind the wind and the neighbors. EC308 suggests putting the narrow end wall into the prevailing wind to limit leeward drifts, and Illinois warns tunnels set too close together get crushed by snow drifting between them.
  • For sheds and coops, a steeper roof pitch, metal roofing that sheds snow, and rafters sized for your area's design snow load are all cheaper than a collapse. If you're building a shelter for animals, put it where you can reach it with a roof rake (see choosing a building site).

The short version

Light snow weighs about 7 lb per cubic foot, average snow about 15, and drifted or compacted snow 20 or more; ice adds weight fast. Farm buildings are often built for only 20 to 35 lb per square foot, and DIY sheds and coops may hold less. Watch for rafters bending or flexing, and get animals out if a roof sags. Clear roofs from the ground with a roof rake, evenly on both sides. On hoop houses, start early, keep plastic tight, knock snow off from inside, add props, and cut the plastic if collapse is imminent.
