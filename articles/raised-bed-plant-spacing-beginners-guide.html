Raised Bed Plant Spacing: A Beginner's Guide to Plants per Square Foot, Grid Layouts & Replanting

Raised beds let you plant much closer than rows, but how close? Here's how to turn the spacing on a seed packet into the number of plants for each square foot of bed, which crops need more room, and how to keep every square producing, from extension guides at Clemson, NC State, UF/IFAS, Cornell, Virginia Tech, Nebraska and UNH.

Why spacing works differently in a bed

Seed packets are written for long single rows with a walking path beside each one. In a raised bed you never step on the soil, so you can skip the rows and space plants evenly in every direction. Virginia Cooperative Extension's intensive gardening guide explains that the in-row distance on a seed packet is really "the distance from the center of one plant to the center of the next," and in a bed you use that same distance between neighbors on all sides, staggering the rows so each plant sits in the gap of the row beside it. UF/IFAS puts the goal simply: plants should be close enough to just touch but not to compete with one another.

The payoff is a lot of food from a small space. Clemson's Home & Garden Information Center estimates that conventional rows need about 100 square feet per person for fresh eating, while a 4-by-4-foot bed divided into 1-foot squares gives you 16 square feet per person for fresh food, and a second bed (32 square feet) covers preserving. Cornell's Steve Reiners worked through carrots in a 10-by-3-foot bed: about 40 carrots in a single row at 3 inches, about 80 in a double row, and 270 when the whole width is planted in a grid, nearly 7 times as many.

Setting up the grid

  • Bed width: Virginia Tech and Cornell both describe beds 3 to 4 feet wide; Cornell suggests 8 to 12 feet long. UF/IFAS says beds wider than 4 feet are hard to reach from the side, and suggests a 3-foot aisle between beds. (Our raised bed building guide covers sizes, materials and soil.)
  • Mark 1-foot squares. Clemson's method: mark the frame at 1, 2 and 3 feet along each side of a 4-by-4 bed and stretch string between the marks to make 16 squares. Thin wood lath or twine both work.
  • Fill it right. UF/IFAS suggests a 4-by-4 box with 6- to 8-inch sides, filled to the top with a good potting mix plus compost or other organic material, leveled without packing it down. Clemson lists a commercial planting mix as one option.
  • One crop per square is the usual rule (UF/IFAS), which makes it easy to replant one square at a time.

Turning seed-packet spacing into plants per square

NC State Extension's 4-H guide lays out the conversion: read the spacing on the packet, then use the matching count for a 12-by-12-inch square.

If your packet gives two numbers, a distance between plants and a wider one between rows, use the smaller in-row figure. Virginia Tech's table is a handy cross-check for crops not listed above: for example leaf lettuce 4–6 inches, head lettuce 10–12, peppers 12–15, broccoli and cabbage 15–18, and tomatoes 18–24 inches.

When the guides disagree, start with the roomier figure in your first year and tighten up once you see how big your varieties actually get in your soil. Virginia Tech also notes that some crops give little return for the space in a small garden: sweet corn, melons, winter squash and pumpkins, unless you grow miniature varieties or train them up a trellis.

Grow up, not out

  • Put the trellis on the north side of the bed so it doesn't shade the shorter crops (Clemson).
  • Support heights from UNH Extension: pole beans on a 6–7 foot frame; peas and cucumbers on 5–6 foot supports; tomato cages about 5–6 feet tall and 2½ feet across, or a 5–6 foot stake with the plant pruned to one or two stems. Melons and winter squash need strong supports and slings for the fruit.
  • Water more often. Virginia Tech notes that trellised plants dry out quickly, and that the shady strip beside a trellis suits crops that tolerate some shade.

Replanting a square after harvest

A grid makes it easy to keep every square working all season. When a square is harvested, UF/IFAS suggests working in a trowel of compost, leveling it and planting again. Extension examples of what can follow what:

  • Spring to summer: early cabbage or broccoli followed by snap beans or zucchini; spring lettuce, radishes or spinach followed by carrots or beets (UNH). Nebraska Extension suggests spring peas, lettuce, cauliflower or broccoli, then summer squash, beans, Swiss chard or carrots.
  • A steady supply: sow radishes, green onions or lettuce every 7 to 10 days (Nebraska), or relay plantings of bush beans or cucumbers about every two weeks (Virginia Tech).
  • Tuck quick crops in between slow ones. UNH points out that a pepper needs about 12 inches at full size, so radishes, leaf lettuce or spinach can grow in the gap while it fills in. Nebraska notes radishes sown in a carrot row are pulled in about 25 to 30 days, thinning the carrots as they go. Virginia Tech's rule for mixing two crops: average their spacings (radishes at 2 inches with beans at 4 inches means plants 3 inches apart).

The trade-offs of close spacing

Tight spacing asks more of you. Cornell notes that crowded plants shade out weeds, but hand-weeding inside a grid is tedious, leaves that stay wet can encourage disease, and close-planted crops likely need more water and nutrients. UNH adds that seedlings must be thinned, poor air circulation can invite disease and pests, and early damage is harder to spot. Leave the recommended space rather than squeezing in extra plants.

Raised beds also drain faster than ground soil. UF/IFAS suggests checking a rain gauge, aiming for about 1 to 2 inches of water a week from rain and irrigation, watering in the morning, and using a soaker hose or drip tape. Once plants are 3 to 4 inches tall, a 2- to 3-inch layer of mulch kept off the stems helps hold moisture. Fertilize according to a soil test (Clemson); our soil test guide walks through it.

The short version

Divide each bed into 1-foot squares with string, put one crop in each square, and turn the seed-packet spacing into a count: 3 inches means 16 per square, 4 inches means 9, 6 inches means 4, 12 inches means 1, and 18 to 24 inches means one plant per two squares. Grow peas, pole beans and cucumbers up a trellis on the north side, replant each square with compost as soon as it's harvested, and when guides disagree on a crop, start with the roomier number.
