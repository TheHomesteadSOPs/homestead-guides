Protecting Chickens From Frostbite: A Beginner's Guide to Combs, Coop Moisture, Roosts & Safe Treatment

Chickens are tougher in the cold than most new keepers expect. Ohio State University Extension says most breeds can tolerate cold, even below freezing, and that the trouble starts when wind and moisture come along with it. University of Kentucky poultry specialist Jacquie Jacob notes that frostbite can become a problem once temperatures reach the single digits and below, and that it's most likely at night in a cold, poorly ventilated coop with damp bedding. Here's how to keep combs and toes safe, what frostbite looks like, and what to do (and not do) if it happens.

Which birds are most at risk

Frostbite happens when the fluid in tissue freezes and the cells die. On a chicken, that means the parts that stick out and have no feathers: the comb, the wattles and sometimes the toes (University of Kentucky).

  • Roosters are the most often affected, because of their big combs and wattles (Kentucky).
  • Large single-combed hens such as Leghorns and Minorcas are also at risk (Kentucky; University of Maryland Extension).
  • Small combs hold up better. Kentucky notes that pea and strawberry combs are much less affected.
  • Bantams may need extra protection even when full-size birds don't (Maryland).

If you're still choosing birds for a cold climate, Ohio State lists cold-tolerant breeds including Ameraucana, Australorp, Brahma, Buff Orpington, Cochin, Delaware, Dominique, New Hampshire, Plymouth Rock, Rhode Island Red, Speckled Sussex and Wyandotte. Even in these breeds, roosters' combs and wattles can still freeze.

Moisture, not cold, is the real problem

This is the part that surprises people. Ohio State says humid air creates more frostbite risk than dry air, and Maryland notes that damp air feels much colder than dry air. A flock gives off a lot of moisture from breathing and droppings. Seal the coop up tight to keep it warm and that moisture stays inside, along with ammonia that can cause breathing problems (Ohio State).

  • Vent high, not low. Maryland recommends keeping a vent or opening near the roof or ceiling, while making sure no hole blows wind directly on the birds.
  • Don't close it all off. Ohio State says to fix drafts around doors and windows but never block ventilation completely. If you wrap a run or coop in plastic for winter, it shouldn't go all the way up to the roof.
  • Read the windows. Kentucky's simple test: if you see condensation on windows or uninsulated walls in the morning, the coop needs more ventilation.
  • Keep the litter dry. Maryland suggests 3 to 4 inches of pine shavings or straw, which also gives off a little heat as it breaks down. Ohio State warns that deep bedding can raise humidity, so check it for damp spots every day. Our deep litter guide covers how to manage it.
  • Stop spills. Spilled water adds moisture (Kentucky), and Ohio State says to check waterers for leaks.

Roosts that keep toes warm

Chickens protect their feet by settling down over them on the roost, so the shape of the roost matters.

  • Use wood. Maryland advises against metal and plastic pipe, which conduct cold.
  • Go flat and wide. Maryland prefers a flat surface like a 2x4, so birds can cover their feet with their feathers. Ohio State gives a roost size of 1½ to 3 inches across.
  • Give each bird room. Ohio State recommends at least 6 to 8 inches of roost space per chicken.
  • Not over the water. Don't put perches above feeders or waterers (Maryland).

Water and feed when it's freezing

  • Keep water liquid. Frozen water is no water at all. Maryland suggests a heater under the waterer and checking water several times a day; Ohio State says to change it at least twice a day, more often when it's very cold, and to check for ice in the evening.
  • Use heaters made for the job. Kentucky recommends choosing drinkers carefully and considering drinker warmers. Maryland says to use a heated base that won't melt or start a fire, and Ohio State warns that heated bowls can malfunction and cause a fire, so check them daily.
  • Feed for warmth. Keep feed available at all times (Maryland). Both Maryland and Ohio State suggest giving scratch grains late in the day to help birds stay warm overnight. Maryland limits suet to 10% of total feed. Ohio State recommends a layer pellet or crumble with 14 to 17% crude protein.
  • Collect eggs often. Ohio State says to gather eggs once or twice a day so they don't freeze, and to throw out eggs that froze and may be cracked.

Should you heat the coop?

Usually not. Maryland says adult birds protected from wind and wet weather should not need any extra heat, and Ohio State says acclimated, cold-tolerant birds in a dry, ventilated coop don't usually need it. Kentucky notes that during extreme cold (below zero for a few days), a heat lamp can help, but that flocks with deep litter and a water warmer do well below zero with no outside heat at all.

Heat lamps and heaters start coop fires. Kentucky and Maryland both warn about the fire risk of heat lamps, and Maryland adds propane and kerosene heaters to the list. If you use a lamp, secure it so it can't fall into the bedding, keep it away from anything that burns, and never let water splash onto a hot bulb (Maryland). Kentucky also notes that a heat lamp can disturb the birds' lighting schedule and cut egg production.

What about petroleum jelly?

Coating combs and wattles is a common tip. Maryland says petroleum jelly can be coated on the combs and wattles of large-combed birds. Kentucky agrees it helps with mild freezing, but stresses that good coop management is the real key. In other words, it's a small extra for a rooster with a big comb on a bitter night, not a replacement for a dry, well-ventilated coop.

What frostbite looks like

Ohio State lists gray, black or brittle areas as the signs to look for. Kentucky describes damaged areas that turn off-color at first, then black at the tips of the comb or toes or the ends of the wattles. Over the following weeks, the dead tissue dries and falls off, and it does not grow back (Kentucky). Check combs and feet when you close up the coop on the coldest nights and again in the morning.

Once the bird is cared for, find the cause: a wet spot in the litter, a leaking waterer, a vent that's closed or a draft at roost level. Fixing that protects the rest of the flock. Our winter laying guide covers lighting and the rest of winter flock care.

The short version

Most chickens handle cold well; frostbite comes from cold plus moisture and wind, especially below the single digits. Big-combed roosters and Leghorn-type hens are most at risk. Vent the coop high, keep litter and water spills dry, use flat wooden roosts, keep water from freezing, and skip the heat lamp unless the cold is extreme. If frostbite happens, warm the bird slowly, don't rub it or cut off black tissue, and watch for infection.
