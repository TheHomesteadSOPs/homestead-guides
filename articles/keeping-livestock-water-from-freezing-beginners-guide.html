Keeping Livestock Water From Freezing: A Beginner's Guide to Winter Water for Goats, Sheep & Cattle

A frozen tank is more than an inconvenience. Animals that can't drink eat less, lose condition and get stressed at exactly the time of year they need the most energy to stay warm. The good news is that keeping water open is mostly about planning before the first hard freeze: knowing how much each animal drinks, picking the right heater or tank, and checking it every single day. Here's what extension livestock specialists recommend, scaled down for a small homestead.

Working out your winter feed too? Water and hay go together: animals that don't drink enough also eat less hay. Our winter hay calculator guide covers the feed side.

Why winter water matters as much as summer water

It's easy to assume animals need less water in the cold. They do drink somewhat less, but as Purdue Extension's Jeff Burbrink puts it, water is essential to livestock "whether the temperature is 100 F or zero." Three things make winter harder:

  • Less water in the feed. Green pasture carries a lot of water. On a dry hay diet, nearly all of an animal's water has to come from the tank or bucket (Nebraska Extension).
  • Less water means less feed. North Dakota State University notes that cattle that stop drinking or drink much less also cut their feed intake. Oklahoma State's Dana Zook adds that this makes it hard for animals to hold body condition just as cold raises their energy needs.
  • Cold, icy water gets ignored. Animals simply drink less from a tank full of slush. NC State Extension's advice is that water should be "the first consideration each day" in winter livestock care.

How much water do they need in winter?

Daily water needs swing with body size, diet and especially milk production. These are the figures extension sources give:

AnimalTypical daily waterSource
Goat (about 110 lb)1 to 3 gal, toward the low end in winterLangston University, via goats.extension.org
Lactating goatadd about 1 quart for every pint of milkLangston University
Lactating sheep or goatsabout 3 galPurdue Extension

Use these numbers to size your setup. A 100-gallon tank shared by four goats and a milk cow will go down fast, and a 5-gallon heated bucket that's perfect for two dry goats is too small for a milking doe with twins. The Langston example works out to about 2.5 gallons a day for a doe giving 5 pints at peak.

Bucks and wethers: Langston University's guidance is that their water should be warm in winter specifically to encourage drinking and reduce the risk of urinary calculi (bladder stones), a serious problem in male goats. Don't let the buck pen be the one with the frozen bucket.

Can animals just eat snow?

Sometimes, but it's not a plan. Utah State University's Jim Keyes reviewed the research: cows relying on snow alone ate 30 to 40 pounds of it a day, and it takes about 4 inches of snow to make half an inch of water. The catches are real:

  • The snow has to be clean, fresh and loose. Ice-crusted, wind-blown or trampled snow is not adequate (USU). NDSU notes that wind-packed or refrozen snow is too hard on their mouths.
  • Eating snow is a learned behavior. Some cows take 4 or 5 days (USU) and others up to a week (Nebraska) to learn it.
  • Thin animals should never depend on snow. Both USU and Nebraska say cattle at a body condition score of 3 or less should not be forced to rely on snow alone; they should be at least a 4 and healthy.
  • Melting snow costs energy. NC State points out that animals burn calories melting snow and warming it to body temperature.

The research that showed snow can work was done on mature beef cows on open range. For a few goats or sheep in a small paddock, where the snow is quickly trampled and dirty, treat snow as an emergency backup only. NDSU's bottom line: even when a lot of snow is available, cattle still need water.

Your options for keeping water open

Purdue Extension lists the three common choices: a heated water bucket, a heater in the water trough, and a heated automatic waterer. Without a heater, the ice has to be broken by hand. Here's how they compare for a homestead:

OptionBest forWatch out for
Heated bucketA few goats, sheep, a buck pen, poultry-sized setupsSmall capacity; cords within reach of chewing animals
Tank heater (floating, sinking or drain-plug type)An existing stock tank shared by a groupElement must stay submerged; check for shorts and stray voltage
Heated automatic watererLarger herds, a permanent barn or paddock siteNeeds a frost-free water line; NC State says check daily, since a failed heater or lid that won't close freezes up fast
No heater: break ice by handMild climates, short cold snapsMust be done at least daily, more in hard cold; thick ice may be impossible for animals to break themselves (SDSU)

Whatever you choose, Purdue suggests that slightly warm water, roughly 40 to 60°F, encourages animals to drink. Fill tanks high enough that the smallest animals (kids, lambs, calves) can reach the water (Oklahoma State).

Electrical safety: the part beginners overlook

Electricity and water troughs need respect, both for you and for your animals.

  • Stray voltage stops animals drinking. NC State warns that a few volts, too little for you to feel, can cause cattle not to drink. Purdue's Burbrink describes horses that quit drinking from a heated trough even though the owner's hand felt nothing; replacing the heater fixed it. If animals suddenly avoid a heated tank, suspect the heater first and offer a bucket while you sort it out.
  • Check for shorts and bare wire. SDSU recommends periodically checking for electrical shorts running through the water; NC State says to watch for shorts or bare wire.
  • Use grounded, rated equipment. For heat tape on pipes, Purdue cites the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission's estimate of about 2,000 heat-tape-related fires a year and recommends only heat tapes with grounded three-prong plugs, replacing any more than three years old. Use heaters made for livestock tanks, route and protect cords so animals can't chew or tangle in them, and have an electrician look at any outdoor circuit you're unsure about.
  • Plan for outages. SDSU recommends a backup power source for heated tanks. If you run a generator, NDSU warns never to run it in an enclosed or partly enclosed building because of carbon monoxide.

Siting and daily habits

  • Check water every day, more often in a cold snap. SDSU's take-home message is to check water sources regularly and break ice if necessary.
  • Keep footing dry. Mud and ice around a waterer spread foot rot and parasites in goats (Langston) and cause foot abrasions on frozen, rough ground (NC State). A pad of gravel around the tank helps in both cases.
  • Break pond ice carefully. If livestock drink from a pond, SDSU advises not breaking ice too close to the bank (it stirs up mud) or too far out (risk of falling through or injury). Never walk out on pond ice to do it.
  • Keep it clean. Clean water gets drunk; dirty water doesn't (Langston). Never haul water in tanks, pumps or hoses that have held herbicides, insecticides or strong cleaners (SDSU).
  • Watch who's drinking. Animals drink less in blizzards and ice storms (SDSU), so watch for anyone holding back once the weather clears, and offer a separate bucket to timid or low-ranking animals.
Chickens too: the same rules apply to the coop. A heated base or heated poultry waterer is the usual fix, with the same cord and fire precautions. Our winter laying guide covers the rest of winter flock care.

The short version

Plan on roughly 1 gallon per 100 lb of body weight for dry cattle (2 for milking), 1 to 3 gallons for an adult goat, and about 3 for a milking sheep or goat. Don't count on snow, especially in a small, trampled paddock. Pick a heated bucket, tank heater or heated waterer sized to your group, protect cords, watch for animals avoiding the tank (stray voltage), keep a backup plan for power outages, and check the water every day before you do anything else.
