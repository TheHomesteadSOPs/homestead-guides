Blanching and Freezing Vegetables: A Beginner's Guide to Blanching Times, Cooling & Packing for the Freezer

Freezing is the easiest way to put up a big garden harvest: no pressure canner, no processing times, no jars. But tossing raw beans or broccoli straight into a freezer bag gives you tough, dull, off-tasting vegetables by midwinter. The step that makes the difference is blanching, a short dip in boiling water followed by a fast chill. It takes a few minutes per batch, and the times are specific for each vegetable.

Still deciding whether to can, freeze or dry a crop? Our canning vs. freezing vs. drying guide compares all three. This article goes step by step through freezing vegetables the way extension food-safety programs recommend.

Why blanching matters

Vegetables keep "living" after you pick them. Natural enzymes go on breaking down flavor, color and texture, and freezing only slows them down; it doesn't stop them. The National Center for Home Food Preservation (NCHFP) calls blanching "a must for almost all vegetables to be frozen." A short blanch stops those enzymes, and it also:

  • cleans off some surface dirt and microorganisms,
  • brightens the color and helps slow vitamin loss,
  • wilts greens and softens vegetables like broccoli and asparagus so they pack more tightly.

Clemson Extension adds an important point: blanching is "critical to quality, but not safety." Frozen vegetables that weren't blanched aren't dangerous. They just taste worse.

Timing matters both ways. NCHFP warns that under-blanching speeds up enzyme activity and is "worse than no blanching." Over-blanching cooks the vegetable and costs you flavor, color, vitamins and minerals. Use a timer, not a guess.

How to water-blanch, step by step

  • Prep: wash, sort, trim and cut the vegetables into even pieces so they blanch evenly.
  • Use plenty of water: 1 gallon of water per pound of prepared vegetables, or 2 gallons per pound for leafy greens (University of Maine Extension).
  • Bring it to a hard boil, lower the vegetables in a wire basket or blancher, and cover the pot.
  • Start the timer when the water boils again. NCHFP: the water should return to a boil within 1 minute. If it takes longer, you put in too many vegetables for that amount of water. Do smaller batches.
  • Keep the heat on high for the full time in the chart below.

Steam blanching

To steam-blanch, put an inch or two of water in a pot with a tight lid and a basket that holds the food at least 3 inches above the bottom. Bring it to a boil, add the vegetables in a single layer, cover and start timing. NCHFP says steam blanching takes about 1½ times longer than water blanching. Broccoli is a good candidate: 3 minutes in water or 5 in steam.

What about the microwave? NCHFP says microwave blanching "may not be effective," because some enzymes may survive, and it doesn't save time or energy. Stick with the stovetop.

Cool it fast: the ice bath

As soon as the time is up, plunge the vegetables into cold water at 60°F or below. Change the water often, run cold tap water over them, or use ice: about 1 pound of ice for each pound of vegetables. The rule of thumb from NCHFP and University of Maine Extension: cool for the same length of time you blanched. Corn on the cob is the exception and needs twice its blanching time to cool all the way to the cob. Then drain thoroughly. Extra water turns into ice crystals and hurts quality.

Blanching times for common garden vegetables

Times are in minutes of water blanching, from University of Maine Extension Bulletin #4384 and NCHFP. Start timing when the water returns to a boil.

AsparagusSmall stalks 2, medium 3, large 4
Beans, snap, green or wax3
Beans, lima, butter or pintoSmall 2, medium 3, large 4
Broccoli (1½-inch florets)3 (5 if steamed)
Brussels sproutsSmall 3, medium 4, large 5
CarrotsSmall whole 5; diced, sliced or strips 2
Cauliflower3
Corn on the cobSmall ears 7, medium 9, large 11
Corn, whole kernel4 (blanch on the cob, then cut)
Greens2 (collards 3)
Peas, green1½ to 2½
Peas, edible podSmall 2, large 3

A few things don't follow the chart. Tomatoes aren't blanched for freezing; University of Maine says to dip them in boiling water for 30 seconds only to loosen the skins. Herbs need no blanching at all. And peas and sweet corn lose sugar within hours of picking, so pick them the morning you freeze them. Our harvest timing guide has the ripeness signs for each crop.

Packing and freezing

University of Maine Extension gives two ways to pack drained vegetables:

  • Dry pack: fill freezer bags or containers, press out as much air as you can, and seal.
  • Tray pack: spread the pieces in a single layer on a sheet pan, freeze until firm, then bag them. They stay loose, so you can pour out a handful at a time. This is great for peas, corn and berries.

Use freezer-grade bags or rigid containers that resist moisture and seal tightly. Leave room for food to expand as it freezes:

VegetableHeadspace
Asparagus, broccoli, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts, corn on the cobNone needed
Beans, carrots, cut corn, greens, peas, peppers, summer squash½ inch
Tomatoes1 inch

Label every package with what it is and the date. Freeze at 0°F or below, and don't overload the freezer with warm packages all at once; that slows freezing and makes bigger ice crystals. At 0°F, University of Maine says frozen vegetables keep their best quality for 8 to 12 months, which is about one garden season to the next.

Keep a simple freezer list on the door: what went in, how many packages, and the date. Cross items off as you use them. It's the easiest way to avoid finding a three-year-old bag of zucchini at the bottom of the chest freezer.

The short version

Blanch almost every vegetable before freezing. Use 1 gallon of boiling water per pound, start the timer when it boils again, follow the chart exactly, then chill in ice water for the same amount of time. Drain well, pack with the right headspace, label, and freeze at 0°F. Use it all within 8 to 12 months.
