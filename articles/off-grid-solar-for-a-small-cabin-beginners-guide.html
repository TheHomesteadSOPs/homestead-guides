Off-Grid Solar for a Small Cabin: A Beginner's Guide to Sizing Panels, Batteries & Loads

A few solar panels on a cabin roof can run lights, charge phones and laptops, and pump water for years with almost no noise or fuel. But a system that's sized by guesswork either goes dark on the third cloudy day in December or costs twice what it needed to. The good news: sizing a small cabin system is mostly arithmetic you can do on paper before you buy anything. Here's the method, using guidance from the U.S. Department of Energy, the National Renewable Energy Laboratory and university extension.

First, is off-grid the right call?

The U.S. Department of Energy notes that in remote locations, a stand-alone system can cost less than extending a power line, which it estimates at $15,000 to $50,000 per mile. For a hunting cabin, a bunkie at the back of the property or a cabin with no road access, that math often favors solar.

Before you size anything, cut the load. University of Alaska Fairbanks Cooperative Extension puts it plainly in its solar design manual: energy efficiency is "the most important part of a well-planned system," and efficiency improvements almost always pay back fastest. Every watt-hour you don't use is a watt-hour you don't have to buy panels and batteries for. LED bulbs, a laptop instead of a desktop, and DC appliances that run straight off the battery all shrink the system.

The parts of a small system

DOE lists the pieces of a stand-alone system: the solar panels (the array), plus "balance of system" equipment to store and deliver power safely:

  • Charge controller — sits between panels and batteries and keeps the batteries from being overcharged. Many also include a low-voltage disconnect, which UAF notes prevents over-discharging that can permanently damage a battery bank.
  • Battery bank — stores daytime energy for night and cloudy days.
  • Inverter — turns the battery's DC power into the 120-volt AC that household plugs use. Skip it entirely if everything you run is 12-volt.
  • Safety equipment and meters — fuses or breakers, disconnects, and a battery monitor so you can see what's left.

Step 1: Add up your daily watt-hours

DOE's battery guide says to multiply each appliance's wattage by the hours you use it each day. Add them up and you have your daily load in watt-hours (Wh). Wattage is on the device label or power brick; a plug-in usage meter gives a real number for anything that cycles on and off. Here's an example for a simple weekend cabin:

LoadWatts × hours per dayWh per day
4 LED lights4 × 9 W × 5 h180
Phone and laptop charging65 W × 2 h130
Radio or Wi-Fi hotspot10 W × 6 h60
12 V water pump60 W × 0.5 h30
Total400 Wh

Watch for the big ones. A refrigerator can easily dwarf everything above. Check its yellow EnergyGuide label: divide the kWh per year by 365 and multiply by 1,000 to get Wh per day. A fridge rated at 300 kWh a year uses about 820 Wh a day, double this whole cabin's load.

Don't plan to heat with solar electricity. A 1,500-watt space heater running for one hour uses 1,500 Wh, almost four days of the example budget above. Electric heat, electric water heaters and electric cooking don't belong on a small off-grid system. Heat with wood or a properly vented heater instead; our guides to heating a small cabin safely and firewood and wood heat cover the carbon monoxide rules.

Step 2: Size the battery bank

The battery has to carry you through the night and through cloudy stretches when the panels make little. Engineers call that buffer days of autonomy: the number of days a full battery can run the load with no help from the panels (NREL). UAF says off-grid battery banks are typically sized for one to three cloudy days. Penn State's solar engineering course gives an example of 3 days of autonomy for about 95% availability in a sunny location, with far more needed for critical loads in cloudier places. For a weekend cabin with a backup plan, 2 days is a common starting point.

You can't use the whole battery. DOE explains that lead-acid batteries can't be fully discharged, so the bank must be bigger than the storage you actually need. Penn State gives allowable depth of discharge as anywhere from 20 to 80% depending on battery type. Use the figure on your battery's spec sheet. For planning, this guide assumes about 50% for lead-acid and 80% for lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4).

StepMathResult
Usable storage needed400 Wh × 2 days800 Wh
Lead-acid bank (50% usable)800 ÷ 0.51,600 Wh rated (about 133 Ah at 12 V)
Lithium bank (80% usable)800 ÷ 0.81,000 Wh rated (about 83 Ah at 12 V)

To convert watt-hours to amp-hours, divide by the battery voltage. DOE's own example: 1 kWh of daily use on a 24-volt bank needs 42 amp-hours of useful storage. Penn State notes small systems typically run at 12 volts and larger ones at 24 or 48.

Cold matters. DOE and NREL both note that lead-acid capacity drops as batteries get colder, so add margin if they'll live in an unheated cabin. DOE also warns that discharged batteries can freeze and burst, so keep them charged through the winter. Many lithium batteries can't be charged below freezing unless they have a built-in heater, so check the spec sheet before you buy one for a cold cabin. UAF notes gel batteries can be a good choice for unheated spaces because they resist freezing.

Pick a real deep-cycle battery. DOE recommends deep-discharge batteries made for stationary solar use (golf-cart batteries are a cheaper alternative) and warns that car and marine batteries "will quickly fail" in this job. Flooded lead-acid is usually cheapest but needs distilled water topped up; sealed AGM and gel batteries skip that chore. DOE adds that batteries are typically the largest lifetime cost of an off-grid system, lasting anywhere from 1 to 15 years depending on type and care.

Step 3: Size the panels for your darkest month

Panel output depends on peak sun hours: NREL defines it as the day's total sunlight divided by 1,000 watts per square meter, so 3 peak sun hours means the same energy as 3 hours of full, bright sun. The number changes enormously by season. Penn State and NREL both size for the critical design month, the month when your load is largest compared with the sun available, which for a cabin used year-round is almost always December or January. UAF recommends NREL's free PVWatts calculator to estimate monthly output for your exact location.

A simple planning formula: daily Wh ÷ (winter peak sun hours × 0.7). The 0.7 is our conservative allowance for wiring, charging, battery and inverter losses, plus panels rarely hitting their label rating. For the example cabin with 3 winter sun hours:

StepMathResult
Panel watts needed400 ÷ (3 × 0.7)about 190 W
If December only gives 2 sun hours400 ÷ (2 × 0.7)about 290 W

Round up. You also need spare capacity to recharge the battery after a cloudy spell, not just keep pace with daily use, so a 300 to 400 watt array is a sensible target for this cabin. In summer it will make far more than you need.

Step 4: Mount the panels where the sun actually reaches

  • Face due south and tilt steeply. UAF says a south-facing tilt at or slightly below your latitude is best for year-round output, while a steeper tilt favors winter. For a cabin used mostly in winter, err steep.
  • Plan for snow. UAF doesn't recommend flat arrays because they won't shed snow well. On the plus side, fresh snow on the ground reflects 70 to 80% of sunlight, and UAF cites research showing snow cover can boost solar collection by 15 to 30%.
  • Watch the trees. A cabin tucked into the woods may get little direct sun on its roof in winter, when the sun is low. A ground mount in the nearest clearing often beats a shaded roof. Our guide to siting a cabin near trees covers reading the site first.
  • Mind cold-weather voltage. Panels make higher voltage when cold. UAF's example applies the National Electrical Code's 1.25 correction factor to a panel's open-circuit voltage to make sure it won't exceed the charge controller or inverter limit on a frigid morning.
  • Keep the electronics inside. UAF notes most off-grid inverters aren't weatherproof and should be mounted indoors, close to the battery bank.

Step 5: Have a backup plan

Sizing panels and batteries for the worst week of the year gets expensive fast. UAF's advice is to size the system moderately and add a small backup generator for the occasional sunless stretch; in a well-designed system it says the generator may only need to run 50 to 200 hours a year. Gasoline, diesel and propane all work. Run a generator outdoors only, well away from doors and windows.

Battery safety and doing it to code

Batteries give off explosive gas while charging. DOE and NREL both stress ventilation. DOE recommends a dry, well-ventilated, temperature-moderated spot, such as a shed, or a vented, locked battery box. Fuse the battery cables, follow the manufacturer's voltage settings for your charge controller, and keep sparks and flames away. UAF's manual is not an installation guide, and it recommends consulting a licensed electrician or qualified professional before buying equipment or starting electrical work. Check with your local building department about permits, too.

The short version

List every load and multiply watts by hours to get daily watt-hours, and keep heat and big appliances off the solar system. Multiply by 1 to 3 days of autonomy, then divide by your battery's usable fraction (about 0.5 for lead-acid, 0.8 for lithium) to size the bank. Divide daily watt-hours by your December peak sun hours times 0.7 to size the panels, then round up. Face them south at a steep tilt in an unshaded spot, add a small generator for long cloudy spells, and have the wiring checked by a qualified electrician.
