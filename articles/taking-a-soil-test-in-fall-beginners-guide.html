Taking a Soil Test in Fall: A Beginner's Guide to Sampling Your Garden & Reading the Results

Most garden problems that get blamed on bad seed, bad weather or bad luck are really soil problems: a pH that's locking up nutrients, too much phosphorus from years of all-purpose fertilizer, or too little organic matter. A soil test costs little and tells you exactly what to add (and what to stop adding). Fall, after harvest, is the best time to do it. Here's how to take a sample the lab can actually use, and how to make sense of the report.

Closing the garden down for the year? Take your sample before you spread compost, manure or lime as part of putting the garden to bed. Otherwise the test will measure what you just added, not your soil.

Why fall is the best time

University of Georgia Extension's Soil Testing for Home Lawns, Gardens and Wildlife Food Plots (Circular 896) says you can test any time of year, but fall is generally the most desirable for two reasons:

  • The soil is dry and easy to get to. Beds are harvested and you're not walking between plants.
  • Lime works slowly. UGA advises mixing lime into the soil 2 to 3 months before planting if possible. A fall test gives you the results in time to lime over winter and plant on schedule in spring.

Whatever the season, sample before you add lime, fertilizer or manure, per the University of Vermont's soil lab.

How often to test

  • Vegetable gardens: every 1 to 2 years (UGA).
  • Lawns and ornamentals: every 2 to 3 years once fertility is at medium or high levels (UGA).
  • NC State Extension adds a soil-type rule: sandy soils at least every 2 to 3 years, clay soils every 3 to 4 years.

Test sooner if you're starting a new garden, have just moved, or plants are struggling for no obvious reason.

What you'll need

  • A clean stainless steel or chrome-plated trowel, spade or soil probe. NC State says to avoid brass, bronze and galvanized tools, which can contaminate the sample.
  • A clean plastic bucket. UGA specifies plastic, to avoid metal contamination, and says never to use a container that has held fertilizer or lime.
  • Your lab's sample bag or box and information form. Most state land-grant universities run a soil lab; your county extension office will have bags, forms and prices.
Skip the drugstore kit. NC State Extension says over-the-counter soil test kits are not considered reliable. A lab test gives you actual nutrient levels plus a recommendation for how much lime and fertilizer to apply, which is the part you really need.

How to take the sample, step by step

  1. Divide your land into zones. Each sample should represent one kind of area. UGA suggests splitting by plant type, how well plants grow, soil type and drainage. That means separate samples for the vegetable garden, lawn, flower beds, orchard and shrub borders (NC State), and for front and back lawns if they differ (Auburn University/Alabama Extension).
  2. Skip the odd spots. Don't sample where fertilizer or lime has spilled, or other unusual areas (UVM), such as an old burn pile or where a compost heap sat.
  3. Dig to the right depth. For vegetable gardens, sample to the depth you till or plant. UGA says 6 inches; NC State says 6 to 8 inches; UVM says 6 to 10 inches for annually tilled ground. Lawns are shallower (about 4 inches).
  4. Take a thin slice, top to bottom. If you don't have a probe, dig a hole to that depth, then shave a thin slice of soil from one side of the hole in a single stroke from the bottom to the top (NC State, UVM). Every slice should be about the same size so each spot counts equally.
  5. Repeat across the zone in a zig-zag. Take at least 8 to 10 cores from random spots (UGA, UVM). Alabama Extension recommends 15 to 20. More cores give a more representative sample.
  6. Mix well and clean it up. Combine the cores in the bucket, break up clods and remove roots, stalks, leaves and rocks (NC State).
  7. Dry it if your lab asks. UGA says to air dry the sample overnight on a flat surface lined with clean white paper.
  8. Fill the bag and the form. Send about a pint of the mix (UGA, Alabama), or fill to your lab's line. On the form, list what you plan to grow. Alabama Extension notes you should say if the garden is organic, so the recommendations fit.

Results come back fast. UGA reports about 4 to 6 days from the time the lab receives a sample; NC State's agent quotes about two weeks by email.

Reading the report

Iowa State University Extension's guide How to Interpret Soil Test Results walks through each number. The key ones for a vegetable gardener:

ResultWhat it tells you
pHMost garden crops do best between 6.0 and 7.5. Lime raises pH; elemental sulfur lowers it. Iowa State notes that most micronutrient deficiency symptoms are really caused by the wrong pH, not a lack of the nutrient.
Buffer pHNot the same as pH. It measures how strongly the soil resists change, and is used only to work out how much lime you need.
Phosphorus (P)Bray-P1 test (soils below pH 7.4): low 0 to 5 ppm, medium 6 to 10, high 11 to 25, very high above 25. Many established gardens and lawns already have enough, so a low- or zero-phosphorus fertilizer is often recommended.
Potassium (K)Above 125 ppm is enough and needs no fertilizer. Below 50 ppm suggests a deficiency to correct.
Organic matter3 to 6% is ideal and many gardeners aim for about 5%. Below 3% means it's time to add compost, leaves, manure or crop residue.

Why there's no nitrogen number. Iowa State explains that standard tests don't measure nitrogen, because nitrate moves so easily with water that a single reading isn't reliable. Instead, nitrogen recommendations are based on your crop and your organic matter: each 1% of organic matter supplies roughly 0.7 pounds of nitrogen per 1,000 square feet over a season.

Standard tests also don't check for heavy metals. If you garden near an old painted building or a roadside, ask your lab about a separate test.

Follow the lab's numbers, not the bag's. The report's lime and fertilizer recommendations are calculated for your soil and crop. Iowa State's own phosphorus ranges, for example, change depending on which test method the lab used, so read your results against your lab's scale rather than a chart from another state.

The short version

Test your vegetable garden every 1 to 2 years, in fall after harvest and before adding lime, fertilizer or manure. Use a clean stainless trowel and a plastic bucket. Take at least 8 to 10 thin, even slices, about 6 inches deep, in a zig-zag across one zone, mix them, and send about a pint to your state lab with the form filled in. Aim for pH 6.0 to 7.5 and 3 to 6% organic matter, follow the lab's recommendations, and lime this winter so it has 2 to 3 months to work before spring planting.
