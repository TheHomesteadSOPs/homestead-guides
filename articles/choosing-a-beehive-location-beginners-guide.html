Choosing a Beehive Location: A Beginner's Guide to Sun, Wind, Water, Flight Paths & Neighbors

Where you put a hive affects how well the colony winters, how much it forages, and whether your neighbors ever notice you keep bees. Winter is the time to pick the spot, before packages and nucs arrive in spring. Here's what to look for, drawn from Mississippi State, Texas A&M, Utah State and the University of Georgia's bee program.

Start with the rules, not the yard

Before you pick a spot, check whether you can keep bees there at all. Mississippi State University Extension notes that zoning codes can set a minimum lot size and a maximum number of colonies, some cities ban beekeeping outright, and homeowners' associations may forbid it. Texas A&M AgriLife Extension says to check with your city, municipality and HOA first. Many states also run an apiary registration or inspection program; in Mississippi it's optional for hobbyists, but rules differ by state, so ask your state department of agriculture.

Which way should the entrance face?

All four extension guides agree: face the entrance south to east, never north.

  • Utah State University Extension: south or southeast, so the hive gets 6 to 8 hours of direct sun starting early in the morning.
  • Texas A&M: east or southeast if possible, and not directly north.
  • Mississippi State: "somewhere between south and east." East gets the bees out foraging earlier in the day; south gives longer foraging time in the cooler months.

Sun or shade?

This is where the advice depends on your climate.

SourceRecommendation
University of Georgia Bee ProgramA site that is sheltered from wind and in full sun
Utah State University (dry, cold-winter West)6 to 8 hours of direct sun from early morning
Texas A&M (hot Texas summers)Morning sun and afternoon shade; full sun means bees need more water, while shade invites small hive beetles
Mississippi State (humid South)Full sun is very hard on bees in humid summers, but hot, dry, sunny conditions also hold back small hive beetles and varroa, so it's a trade-off
A simple rule of thumb: in the North and in cool or dry climates, give the hive as much sun as you can, especially in the morning. In hot, humid areas, aim for morning sun with some afternoon shade, but avoid deep shade under trees. Mississippi State specifically says wooded spots are not recommended, because understory placement invites small hive beetles and ants.

Shelter from wind, and stay out of low spots

  • Windbreak on the north side. Mississippi State calls a tree line, shrubs or a building on the north side "ideal": it cuts winter wind and reduces chilling losses. Georgia also says to pick a site sheltered from wind.
  • Avoid frost pockets. Georgia says avoid low spots where cold, damp air collects in winter. Mississippi State's example of a bad site is a hive at the bottom of a hill, in a flood plain, with no windbreak and too much shade.
  • Stay off flood-prone ground, drainage ditches and creek banks (Mississippi State).

Get the hive off the ground

  • Stand height: Utah State recommends a stand or cinder blocks that raise the hive 12 to 18 inches off the ground, partly to deter skunks. A raised hive is also easier on your back when you lift boxes.
  • A slight forward tilt: Texas A&M says hives don't have to be perfectly level, and a slight slant toward the entrance lets moisture drain out.
  • Weigh it down: Utah State suggests a large rock on top or a strap around the hive against raccoons and wind.

If you're building your own boxes and stand, our guide to building a Langstroth beehive covers the dimensions.

Provide water before the bees find your neighbor's

Bees need a steady water source, especially in hot weather. Texas A&M says a colony may drink up to a gallon a day in hot weather, and that you should supply water if there isn't a natural source within 100 yards.

  • Set it up before the bees arrive. Mississippi State warns that bees often choose a neighbor's chlorinated swimming pool, and once they've fixed on a water source it's "almost impossible" to change their minds. Georgia mentions pools and water spigots too.
  • Make it safe to land on. Put pebbles or straw in a bucket or dish so bees can drink without drowning (Texas A&M). Bees often prefer slightly "dirty" water, like a muddy puddle or birdbath, over clean tap water.
  • Other options: a dripping faucet, a small fountain or an ornamental pond (Mississippi State), or a Boardman entrance feeder filled with water in dry spells (Georgia).

Flight paths and neighbors

Bees leave the hive in a fairly straight line from the entrance, so plan where that line goes.

  • Keep 10 to 15 feet in front of the entrance clear of foot traffic. Beyond that, flight paths should be above head height (Utah State).
  • Use a barrier to lift them up. Mississippi State recommends a barrier at least 6 feet tall a few feet in front of the entrance near public areas, so bees fly up and over people. Texas A&M suggests a solid fence or dense hedge between the hives and people.
  • Don't aim the entrance at people. Georgia says flight paths shouldn't cross sidewalks, playgrounds or other public areas, and Mississippi State adds busy roads. Face the entrance away from doors, patios and paths.
  • Out of sight is out of mind. Georgia says pick a discreet site, and Utah State says choose a low-traffic area where the bees are less likely to be disturbed. Add a small wire fence if children play nearby.

Livestock, pesticides and access

  • Livestock: Mississippi State says never keep bees where penned animals can't get away from them, and suggests an electric fence about 20 feet out from the hives to keep cattle and horses from knocking them over.
  • Pesticides: if you're placing hives on someone else's farmland, talk to the grower about spraying first. Mississippi State also notes that night-time mosquito sprays can drift onto bees clustered on the front of the hive.
  • Access: you'll be carrying heavy honey supers away from the hive, so make sure you can get a cart or vehicle close. Mississippi State says an out-yard on a low, wet field may need a four-wheel-drive truck or ATV.

Forage and spacing between hives

  • Forage range: bees prefer to forage within about 2 miles of the hive but can travel up to 5 (Mississippi State); Texas A&M says 2 miles or more, though longer trips cost energy. You don't need flowers in your own yard, but you do want plenty within a couple of miles.
  • Between hives: leave a few feet between hives in a row, and alternate entrance directions to reduce bees drifting into the wrong hive (Mississippi State). Our beginner's guide to beekeeping explains why it pays to start with more than one hive, so leave room for at least two.

The short version

Check local and HOA rules first. Then pick a discreet, low-traffic spot on high, well-drained ground with a windbreak to the north, face the entrance south to east, and give it morning sun (with some afternoon shade in hot, humid climates, but never deep woods). Set the hive 12 to 18 inches up on a stand with a slight forward tilt, put a pebble-filled water dish within 100 yards before the bees arrive, keep 10 to 15 feet in front of the entrance clear, and use a 6-foot fence or hedge to send the flight path over people's heads. Leave room for a second hive and for getting a cart in at harvest time. Once the site is ready, see package bees vs. nucs to order your bees.
