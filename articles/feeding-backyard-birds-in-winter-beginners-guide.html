Feeding Backyard Birds in Winter: A Beginner's Guide to the Best Seed, Feeders, Placement & Clean Feeders

A feeder outside the kitchen window is one of the cheapest winter pleasures on a homestead, and it's easy to do well or badly. Here's which seed draws which birds, the feeder types and where to hang them, water in freezing weather, and the cleaning routine that keeps birds healthy, from extension guides at Oregon State, Nebraska, UNH, West Virginia, Wisconsin, Clemson and Washington State.

What birds need in winter

Nebraska Extension sums it up as three things: food, water and cover. A feeder supplies the first, but birds also need a place to drink and somewhere close by to duck out of the wind and away from hawks and cats. Washington State University Extension's Skagit County Master Gardeners suggest putting every feeder "out of the wind" with a bush, tree or low plantings nearby, and Oregon State's wildlife specialist Dana Sanchez puts it bluntly: if there's no shrub nearby, birds won't use the feeder.

Feeding matters most in hard weather. Nebraska Extension notes that birds lean on feeders hardest during winter extremes and in late winter and early spring, when snow or ice covers natural food. UW–Madison Extension's advice is to stay consistent and keep feeding through late winter and early spring once you've started.

The best seed: start with black-oil sunflower

Every extension guide we read puts black-oil sunflower first. WSU says it has twice the calories per pound of striped sunflower and a smaller shell that leaves less mess; WVU Extension adds that its thin shell is easy for most birds to crack, while striped sunflower's thicker shell suits bigger bills. Shelled sunflower hearts cost more and spoil faster (WVU).

What to skip

  • Cheap filler mixes. Oregon State calls mixes with red millet, golden millet, flax, rape seed and oats "a waste": birds kick the fillers out, where they sprout as weeds or draw rats. UW–Madison names milo and hulled oats as fillers to avoid, and its wildlife specialist Jamie Nack suggests skipping mixes entirely and buying single seeds. WVU adds that raked-out filler feeds fungus and bacteria and brings in mice, squirrels and deer.
  • Bread. WSU's Skagit County guide says plainly not to feed birds bread in winter.
  • More than a day or two of seed at a time. UNH Extension suggests filling only about a day's worth, and WSU a day or two, so seed doesn't sit and rot.
Where guides differ on millet. Nebraska Extension recommends white proso millet alongside sunflower for ground-feeding birds, while Oregon State (in the Pacific Northwest) says to avoid seed mixes that contain millet because birds toss it. The difference is partly the birds in each region and partly the mix: a scoop of white proso on a ground tray is not the same as a bag padded with red millet and oats. Try white proso on its own and watch whether your birds eat it.

Feeder types

  • Hopper (house-style) feeder: UW–Madison's pick for beginners. Its roof keeps seed dry. WSU notes hoppers and platforms suit grosbeaks, jays and starlings, and squirrels and chipmunks love them too.
  • Tube feeder with short perches: for chickadees and finches; Nebraska and WSU note tubes keep out bigger birds like jays and starlings. WSU warns that the bottom of a tube builds up mold and bacteria, so clean that part well.
  • Platform (tray) feeder: draws the widest mix of birds. Choose one with a screened, draining floor (WSU) and raise it slightly off the ground (Oregon State).
  • Nyjer feeder: a fine-wire or small-port tube for goldfinches and siskins.
  • Suet cage: for woodpeckers, nuthatches and chickadees.

UW–Madison suggests putting out more than one style to spread birds out and cut down on squabbling, and choosing feeders with a roof or drainage holes so seed never sits wet. Oregon State recommends a catch pan under seed feeders to limit spilled seed and rats.

Where to hang feeders

  • Windows: UW–Madison says to place feeders within 3 feet of a window or more than 30 feet away. WVU Extension explains the close option: a bird leaving a feeder that near can't build up enough speed to hit the glass hard. Window strikes kill millions of birds a year (WVU).
  • Height and cover: UW–Madison suggests hanging feeders 5 to 6 feet off the ground and about 10 feet from cover, because cats hide in shrubs. Nebraska Extension suggests shrubs or trees 10 to 15 feet from the feeder: close enough for a quick escape from a hawk, far enough that a cat can't pounce from them.
  • Out of the wind, and somewhere you can see it from inside (Nebraska, WSU).
  • Squirrels: hang feeders on a pole away from anything squirrels can jump from, add a baffle under a pole feeder, or use a cage-style feeder that closes when a squirrel lands (Oregon State, Nebraska). Nebraska notes that a Cornell study found hot pepper on seed doesn't bother birds, but squirrels often keep trying anyway.
  • Keep chemicals away. Oregon State asks you not to spray pesticides or spread pelletized weed-and-feed or moss killer where birds feed.

Water in freezing weather

Birds need open water all winter. Oregon State suggests a shallow, flat-bottomed dish with ½ to 1 inch of water, changed daily or every other day. Nebraska Extension suggests a large clay saucer or similar easy-to-clean container, with an immersion heater to keep it from freezing; WSU says baths, fountains or small ponds work if kept clean, ice-free and near cover.

Keeping feeders clean

Crowds of birds at one spot spread disease, and dirty feeders make it worse. Clemson Extension notes that a 10% bleach solution is the most effective way to get rid of salmonella on feeders, and Nebraska Extension warns that wet seed grows a fungus that can cause a potentially fatal infection in birds.

  1. Empty and scrub. Dump old seed and scrub off debris inside and out; a long-handled bottle brush helps (Oregon State).
  2. Soak in dilute bleach. Most guides use 1 part bleach to 9 parts water (Clemson, Nebraska, WSU, UNH); Oregon State uses 1 part bleach to 10 parts water. Clemson soaks for 10 minutes, UNH for 15. UNH also lists 1 part white vinegar to 1 part water, or hot soapy water, as gentler options.
  3. Rinse and dry completely before refilling, so seed doesn't go moldy. Wear gloves and wash your hands afterward (UNH).
  4. Clean underneath too. Nebraska suggests raking up and discarding hulls and spilled seed every two weeks, or moving the feeder once seed builds up several inches deep. Clemson suggests sweeping under feeders at least monthly, more often if rodents or insects show up.
How often? Nebraska, UNH, Clemson and WVU say about every two weeks (Clemson says at least that often, and more often with heavy use, after rain, or if you find a dead bird). Oregon State says weekly. Every two weeks is a reasonable baseline; go weekly when a feeder is busy or seed gets wet.

Sick birds and bird flu

UNH Extension lists the signs of a sick bird: crusty, swollen or closed eyes, fluffed-up feathers, slow or clumsy movement, or bright green droppings. If you see them, take the feeder down (with gloves), clean it, and leave it down for several days so birds disperse. On avian flu, Oregon State Extension (reviewed 2025) reports that veterinarians consider the risk to native songbirds very low and that there was no official recommendation to take down wild bird feeders, but regular cleaning still matters for salmonella and mold. Don't handle dead birds (WSU), and check your state wildlife agency's current advice during an outbreak. If you keep backyard poultry, Oregon State's guidance for flocks focuses on keeping wild ducks and geese away from your birds.

When to start, and suet in warm spells

Nebraska Extension suggests having feeders up by mid to late September, but says feeders started in December or January still attract birds: numbers start low and climb with each cold snap. Suet is best used in fall and winter, Oregon State says; in warm weather check it often and remove it when it smells, and UNH notes suet goes rancid as temperatures rise. In bear country, UNH suggests bringing feeders in by April.

A feeder works best as a supplement to natural food. WSU suggests planting winter-fruiting shrubs such as viburnum, holly, cotoneaster, pyracantha, Oregon grape, crab apple, snowberry, beautyberry and wax myrtle, and leaving tufted grasses uncut through winter. Our guide to putting the garden to bed has more on leaving seed heads and cover in place.

The short version

Offer black-oil sunflower in a hopper or tube feeder, add nyjer for finches and suet for woodpeckers, and skip bags padded with filler. Hang feeders 5 to 6 feet up, within 3 feet of a window or more than 30 feet away, with shrubs 10 to 15 feet off for cover. Keep a shallow dish of unfrozen water out, fill only a day or two of seed at a time, and soak feeders in 1 part bleach to 9 parts water about every two weeks, more often when they're busy or wet.
