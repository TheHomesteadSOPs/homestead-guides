Growing Herbs Indoors in Winter: A Beginner's Guide to Light, Pots, Watering & Harvesting on a Windowsill

Fresh basil and chives in January are one of the easiest small wins a homestead kitchen can have, as long as you give the pots enough light. Winter sun is short and weak, so light is the thing that makes or breaks an indoor herb garden. Here's which herbs to pick, how much light they need, and how to pot, water, feed and harvest them, drawn from Illinois, Iowa State, UNH, Cornell and Purdue Extension.

Which herbs grow best indoors?

Iowa State University Extension sorts indoor herbs into two groups. The easiest are parsley, mint, chives, basil, oregano, cilantro and thyme. A second group, rosemary, sage, lemon balm, marjoram, chervil and bay laurel, can be grown inside too, but they need more light or tend to have bigger pest problems such as spider mites.

  • Lower-light herbs: UNH Extension says chives, thyme, oregano, sage and marjoram "will thrive" with 4 to 6 hours of direct sun.
  • Rosemary prefers full sun and, per UNH, cool temperatures in the 40s and 50s °F. A cool, bright window suits it better than a warm kitchen.
  • Basil is the opposite: heat-loving and the trickiest of the common herbs indoors (UNH). Cornell's Genesee County Master Gardeners note basil won't tolerate temperatures below 50°F.

How much light do indoor herbs need?

Sources give slightly different minimums, but they all agree that most herbs need a lot more light than a typical houseplant.

  • Pick the brightest window. Iowa State says south-facing is best and a bright east or west window is acceptable; UNH recommends south or west.
  • Turn the pots every few days so the plants don't lean toward the glass (Illinois, Cornell).
  • A simple light helps a lot. Iowa State suggests anything from a compact fluorescent in a task lamp to a plug-in 4-foot LED shop light, ideally full-spectrum and high-output, run on an automatic timer.
Signs of too little light: long, pale, floppy stems with small, widely spaced leaves. Move the pot closer to the window or add a light before you change anything else.

Temperature, humidity and air

  • Temperature: Illinois Extension gives 60 to 70°F. Cornell suggests 65 to 70°F days and 55 to 60°F nights, and says most herbs survive the mid-to-low 40s, but basil does not tolerate below 50°F.
  • Watch the cold glass and the hot vent. Keep leaves from touching a cold window (Cornell) and keep pots away from heat vents and other direct heat sources (Illinois).
  • Raise humidity: winter heating dries indoor air. Set pots on a shallow tray of pebbles and water, group them together, or run a humidifier (Illinois). Cornell advises not misting rosemary, which can encourage mildew.
  • Move the air: a small fan helps; Cornell suggests an oscillating fan for a couple of hours a day.

Pots and potting mix

  • Size and drainage: Illinois Extension recommends at least a 6-inch diameter container with a drainage hole. Iowa State warns that a pot much too large stays wet, and suggests one kind of herb per pot.
  • Clay over plastic: Iowa State and Cornell both prefer clay or terracotta, which dries out more evenly.
  • Use potting mix, not garden soil (Illinois, Purdue). Cornell's own blend is equal parts sand, commercial potting soil, peat moss and perlite, with a soil pH between 6 and 7.

Watering

Overwatering kills more indoor herbs than underwatering, especially on cloudy weeks when the plants use little water. UNH says to water "only when the top inch or two of soil is dry." When you do water, water until some runs out the bottom (Purdue), then empty the saucer so the pot doesn't sit in water (Cornell). Cornell also says not to use softened water.

Rosemary is the herb people most often lose. Cornell says it hates overwatering and cold, damp soil, while Illinois Extension says it should never dry out completely. Both are true: keep rosemary lightly moist, never soggy and never bone-dry.

Feeding

Illinois Extension and Cornell both suggest a liquid, water-soluble fertilizer every two weeks at a low rate, and both warn that heavy feeding makes more leaves but less aroma and flavor. Iowa State is more cautious: half or quarter strength, early spring through summer, and no fertilizer in winter, when growth is slow. A reasonable middle path for winter: feed lightly and only when the plants are actively putting out new growth.

Where to get the plants

  • Buy small transplants for the quickest harvest; UNH notes you'll have more to eat sooner than from seed.
  • Start from seed: Purdue lists dill, parsley, thyme, anise, basil and cilantro as easy from seed. Sprout seeds in a warm, bright spot out of direct sun, then move them into strong light once they're up.
  • Take cuttings from the garden: Purdue suggests taking cuttings of basil, mint, sage and oregano before the outdoor plants die back in fall.

Pests to watch for

Cornell names whiteflies, mealybugs, spider mites and aphids as the main indoor herb pests. Check the undersides of leaves when you water. For an outbreak, Cornell suggests a spray of one to two tablespoons of mild dishwashing soap in a gallon of warm water, applied once a week as long as pests are visible, and rinsing the herbs before you use them.

Harvesting so the plants keep producing

  • Cut often to keep plants bushy and stop them flowering. Cut a few inches down the stem rather than picking single leaves; cut parsley and cilantro stalks at the base (Illinois).
  • Annuals such as basil and parsley can be cut back hard, just above a pair of leaves, leaving about 4 to 6 inches of stem. Perennials such as chives, mint, oregano and thyme: take the top one-third of the growth (Iowa State).
  • Pick young, tender stems that haven't bloomed (Cornell). After several harvests, Iowa State suggests replacing tired annuals with a fresh sowing.
  • Swapping fresh for dried in recipes: use about three times as much fresh, or 1 tablespoon fresh for 1 teaspoon dried (Illinois). See our fresh vs. dried herb guide.

Come spring, Iowa State notes rosemary, bay and marjoram can go back outside once the danger of frost has passed. For planting a full outdoor herb bed, see starting an herb garden, and for the fastest indoor greens of all, growing microgreens.

The short version

Start with parsley, mint, chives, basil, oregano, cilantro or thyme. Give them your brightest south or west window, at least 6 hours of direct sun, or a grow light 6 to 12 inches above the leaves for 12 to 16 hours a day. Keep them at about 60 to 70°F, off cold glass and away from heat vents, in a 6-inch or larger pot with drainage and real potting mix. Water when the top inch or two is dry, feed lightly, watch for spider mites and aphids, and harvest often by cutting stems, not single leaves.
