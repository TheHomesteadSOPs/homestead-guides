Winter Sowing in Milk Jugs: A Beginner's Guide to What to Sow, When to Start & How to Plant Out

Winter sowing lets you start perennials, natives and hardy vegetables outside in cut milk jugs, with no grow lights and almost no watering. Cold and snow do the work, and the seedlings come out already hardened off. Here's what to sow, when to start, and how to cut, fill, place and plant out the jugs, drawn from Illinois, Michigan State, Missouri, NC State, Rutgers and WSU Extension.

What winter sowing is, and why it works

Winter sowing means planting seeds in clear, homemade mini-greenhouses (usually cut milk jugs) and leaving them outside through the rest of winter. Rain and snow water them, the freeze-thaw cycles do the work a fridge would otherwise do for seeds that need cold, and the seedlings come up when the weather tells them to. Illinois and WSU Extension both credit the method to Trudi Davidoff, who pioneered it in upstate New York.

  • It breaks dormancy naturally. Michigan State University Extension explains that many seeds, especially natives, need a cold, moist period (cold stratification) before they will sprout. Some need up to 90 days below 40°F, and a few need two winters.
  • The seedlings harden off on their own. Because they grow up outdoors, they are used to sun, wind and cold by the time you plant them out (Illinois, NC State Extension).
  • No lights, no trays, little watering. WSU Extension's Skagit County Master Gardeners list those as the main advantages over starting seeds indoors, and reported nearly 90% germination across their own jugs.

When to start

Start dates vary by region and by what you are sowing, so take these as a range rather than a rule:

SourceWhen to sow
WSU Extension (Skagit Co., Pacific Northwest)From about the December 21 solstice through February for hardy seeds; March to April for tender annuals and vegetables; tomatoes, peppers, squash and melons wait until April
Michigan State University ExtensionNovember to March in Michigan; sowing too early in fall risks rot or early sprouting during warm spells
Rutgers (Ocean County, NJ)January through March, while nights are still chilly; perennials needing cold first, then hardy annuals
University of Illinois ExtensionWait until January or February; native seeds that need cold go in January
University of Missouri ExtensionLate winter; warm-season vegetables in April

A simple way to plan: sow the seeds that need cold first, then the cold-tolerant crops, and the frost-tender ones last.

What to winter sow

GroupExamples from extension guidesWhen
Natives and perennials that need coldConeflower, black-eyed Susan, Shasta daisy, milkweed, lupine, columbine, native grasses, hollyhock, lavender (NC State, WSU, MSU)First, in early to mid winter
Cold-tolerant vegetables and herbsKale, spinach, Swiss chard, lettuce, broccoli, Brussels sprouts; thyme, oregano, sage, parsley (NC State, WSU, Illinois)Mid to late winter
Hardy and tender annual flowersSweet pea early; petunias, cosmos, zinnias, marigolds, alyssum later (WSU, Illinois)Late winter to early spring
Warm-season cropsTomatoes, peppers, eggplant, squash, pumpkins, melons, basil (WSU, Missouri)Not before March or April, if at all
Guides differ on warm-season crops. NC State's Beaufort County guide says to avoid tomatoes and peppers entirely, while WSU and Missouri say they can go in jugs in April. Michigan State goes further and says the method suits natives, trees and shrubs best, since most vegetables and annuals don't need cold stratification. If you want early tomatoes, our guide to starting seeds indoors is the more reliable route. Illinois and WSU also disagree on cosmos (early vs. March to April), so if in doubt, sow later.

What you need

  • Clean, translucent gallon milk jugs (or deep clear clamshells; MSU says containers need at least 3 to 4 inches of depth, and flat takeout boxes are too shallow)
  • Scissors or a utility knife, plus a drill, awl, nail or hot glue gun for the holes
  • Potting mix, not garden soil (Rutgers, MSU)
  • Seeds, a marker that survives sun and rain, and duct tape (MSU warns that paper tape and clear office tape won't hold)

How to winter sow a milk jug, step by step

  1. Rinse the jug and throw the cap away. The open neck lets rain in and heat out (Missouri, Rutgers, NC State).
  2. Make drainage holes. Rutgers says to make "plenty." WSU found one center hole wasn't enough in a wet climate and recommends several small holes across the bottom. MSU lists missing drainage as one of the most common mistakes.
  3. Cut the jug around the middle, leaving a hinge. WSU cuts about 4 inches up from the bottom and leaves the hinge under the handle; Illinois leaves about half an inch uncut.
  4. Add 3 to 4 inches of moist potting mix. Pre-moisten it to the feel of a damp sponge and let it drain (Illinois, Missouri, NC State). Don't soak it: MSU warns that overwatering before sealing leads to fungus and rot.
  5. Sow at the depth on the seed packet. Tiny seeds can sit on the surface; press them in for good seed-to-soil contact (Illinois). Don't overcrowd (MSU).
  6. Label it twice. Put a label with the plant and date inside, and write on the outside too. MSU notes outside writing fades; WSU labels three places and recommends a UV-resistant marker over an ordinary permanent marker.
  7. Close and tape the seam all the way around with duct tape or clear poly tape.
  8. Set it outside where it gets sun, rain and snow (see below), and leave it there.

Where to put the jugs

  • Sun and weather, but shelter from wind (Illinois, Missouri, NC State). Snow on the jugs is fine.
  • Not against a hot wall. MSU suggests the east side of a building or under a shrub, and says to avoid rocks and south- or west-facing walls. Rutgers starts jugs facing south and moves them to an east or west spot later so the seedlings don't bake. WSU found a southeast spot best.
  • Group them together to even out temperature swings (MSU), and make sure they never sit in standing water (WSU).

Winter and spring care

  • Check about once a week. Look for condensation on the inside walls or moist soil (WSU, Rutgers). No condensation means it's too dry, so water gently (WSU uses a bottle with holes poked in the cap). Rutgers says to add holes if it stays dry, or tape some shut if it stays soggy. Also check that the tape is holding (MSU).
  • Be patient. Seeds stay dormant until the temperatures tell them to sprout (MSU), and some, such as columbine and milkweed, can take months (WSU).
  • Vent once they're up. A closed jug heats quickly on a sunny day, even in cold weather, and can wilt seedlings (Missouri). Open the top on sunny days and close it at dusk (Illinois, Missouri); once nights are milder, take the lid off during the day, then for good.
  • Thin crowded clumps and watch for birds once the tops are open (MSU).

Transplanting the seedlings

  • When: Missouri Extension says when seedlings are about 2 to 3 inches tall with true leaves; MSU says after a couple of sets of true leaves. Frost-tender plants wait until after your last frost (NC State), and Illinois says to wait until soil temperatures suit the plant. Our frost dates guide helps you find yours.
  • How: cut flaps down the side of the jug and slide the block of soil out (Illinois). WSU describes two ways: tease out 2 or 3 seedlings at a time ("taffy method"), or cut the block into squares like a pan of brownies and plant each square ("brownie method"). Plants can go straight into the garden or into a 4-inch pot first.
  • Hardening off: mostly done already, but if you kept jugs closed late into spring, open them gradually for a few days first (NC State). See hardening off seedlings.

Common problems and fixes

ProblemLikely cause and fix
Soil dry, no condensationToo many open holes or a sunny, windy spot. Water gently and tape a few top holes shut (Rutgers).
Soggy soil, mould or rotToo few drainage holes or soil too wet at sowing. Add more bottom holes (MSU, WSU).
Green algae on the soilConstant wet. WSU added drainage holes and switched to a mix without moisture-control crystals.
Wilted or scorched seedlingsJug overheated on a sunny day. Vent in the daytime and move away from hot walls (Missouri, MSU).
Nothing sproutedOld seed or seeds that need a second winter (MSU), or it's simply too early. Leave the jug outside and keep checking it.
Tender seedlings killed by a cold snapIllinois warns that a warm spell can sprout seeds that aren't hardy enough for the cold that follows. Sow frost-tender plants later and close the jugs at dusk.

The short version

Cut a clean milk jug around the middle, leaving a hinge, punch several drainage holes, add 3 to 4 inches of moist potting mix, sow at packet depth, label inside and out, tape it shut and leave the cap off. Set it outside in sun and weather but out of the wind and away from hot walls. Start with natives and perennials that need cold in early to mid winter, then hardy vegetables and herbs, and save tender crops for March or April. Check weekly for condensation, vent on sunny spring days, and plant out in clumps once the seedlings have true leaves. For saving your own seed for next winter's jugs, see seed saving for beginners.
