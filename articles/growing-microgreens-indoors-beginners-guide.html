Growing Microgreens Indoors: A Beginner's Guide to Seeding, Light, Watering & Harvest in 7 to 21 Days

The garden is asleep, but a sunny windowsill isn't. Microgreens are vegetable and herb seedlings grown thickly and cut young, and Mississippi State University Extension says most are ready 7 to 21 days after they germinate. All you need is a shallow tray, potting mix, good seed and a few hours of light a day. Here's how extension horticulturists recommend growing them, from picking crops to storing the harvest.

Microgreens vs. sprouts vs. baby greens

  • Sprouts are germinated in water or a high-humidity system and eaten whole, roots and all (Mississippi State). UF/IFAS Extension notes sprouts carry the highest risk of microbial contamination of the three.
  • Microgreens grow in potting mix or on a grow mat, and you eat only the stems and leaves. Mississippi State describes them as 1 to 3 inches tall at harvest, cut at the seed-leaf (cotyledon) stage or when the first true leaves appear.
  • Baby greens are grown a bit longer, to about 3 to 4 inches (UF/IFAS). Neither "microgreens" nor "baby greens" has a legal definition; they're marketing terms for the stage of growth.

Pick your crops by speed

Mississippi State points out that a tray is cut once, all at the same time, so grow crops with similar timing together. Its groupings, counted from sowing:

SpeedCrops
Fast: 7–10 daysPea shoots, radish
Medium: 10–15 daysMustard, kale, pac choi, red cabbage, kohlrabi, arugula
Slow: 16–25 daysBeets, red amaranth, carrots, scallions, chard, cilantro, basil

For a first try, radish and pea shoots are hard to beat: fast, forgiving and big enough to handle easily. University of Illinois Extension says most microgreens are ready within 10 to 14 days.

What you need

  • A shallow container. Mississippi State uses standard 10 by 20 inch bedding flats, but any shallow container works, including clean take-out boxes, clamshells and pie plates (UF/IFAS Orange County). UF/IFAS suggests containers 2 to 3½ inches deep, with drainage holes a plus; if a tray has holes, set it in a second tray without holes so you can bottom-water.
  • Growing medium. A peat-based potting mix is Mississippi State's recommended method for home growers. UF/IFAS Extension lists sterile, loose, soilless germinating mixes (peat, coconut fiber, vermiculite, perlite) that hold moisture, drain freely and contain no fertilizer. Fill to about 1 to 2 inches (Illinois).
  • Good seed. Use fresh, untreated seed with good germination, with no chemical treatments or coatings (Illinois; UF/IFAS). Mississippi State recommends buying in bulk from established seed suppliers, because garden-center packets are too small to be economical at microgreen sowing rates.
  • Light. A sunny window or a simple shop light; more on this below.

How much seed per tray

Microgreens are sown far more thickly than garden seed. Mississippi State's suggested sowing amounts per standard flat (its radish and red cabbage rates are given for a 10 by 20 inch tray), with typical yields:

CropSeed per trayTypical yield
Pea shootsabout 6 oz seed6–8 oz
Radish2–3 tablespoons6–8 oz
Red cabbage, kale, mustard, pac choi2–3 teaspoons4–6 oz
Arugula, broccoli2–3 teaspoons3–5 oz
Beets, Swiss chard3–5 tablespoons3–6 oz
Cilantro1 tablespoon4–5 oz
Basil½–1 teaspoon2–4 oz

If you're using a smaller container, UF/IFAS Extension's rule of thumb works anywhere: about 10 to 12 seeds per square inch for small seed and 6 to 8 per square inch for larger seed. Scatter seed evenly over moist mix and press it in lightly; covering with a thin layer of mix is optional and matters more for large seed (UF/IFAS; Illinois).

Soak the big seeds first. UF/IFAS Extension's soaking times: peas 8 to 12 hours, sunflower 8 hours, beets 24 hours, cilantro 2 hours. Mississippi State notes peas germinate in uneven waves over 5 or 6 days unless soaked overnight. Small seeds like kale, radish and mustard go in dry.

Germination: keep it dark, warm and damp

  • Cover the tray with a clear dome, plastic wrap or a damp paper towel to hold in moisture (Mississippi State; Illinois). UF/IFAS Extension keeps trays covered for about 4 to 5 days, then removes the cover about a day after the seed leaves emerge.
  • Temperature: 65 to 75°F is ideal for germinating most microgreens (UF/IFAS). In a cold winter house, the top of the refrigerator or a seedling heat mat helps.

Light and water after they sprout

  • Light: UF/IFAS Orange County and Illinois Extension both say seedlings need at least 4 hours of light a day. Turn trays on a windowsill daily so the greens grow straight (UF/IFAS). If they stretch tall, thin and pale ("leggy"), they need more light; Illinois suggests adding a grow light. A simple fluorescent shop light hung over the trays works (Mississippi State; UF/IFAS).
  • Water from below. Mississippi State says bottom watering is effective, especially on a kitchen windowsill: pour water into the outer tray and let the mix soak it up. Keep the mix moist but never soggy; UF/IFAS warns that overwatering causes damping-off, the fungal problem that makes seedlings collapse at the soil line. Light misting once or twice a day also works once seedlings are up (Illinois).
  • Mind the exceptions. Peas prefer the drier side, and Mississippi State says amaranth must be grown very dry.

Harvesting and storing

Harvest when the seed leaves are fully open, or once the first true leaves form, at roughly 2 to 3 inches tall (UF/IFAS; Illinois). Mississippi State's method: microgreens are fragile, so use sharp scissors, grab a bunch and cut just above the soil. Morning, when greens are cool and crisp, is best (UF/IFAS).

  • Wash before eating, as with any fresh vegetable (Mississippi State).
  • Refrigerate in a bag or container. Illinois Extension says they keep up to about a week; UF/IFAS gives 5 to 6 days. They're highly perishable, so cool them quickly after cutting (UF/IFAS Orange County), or simply cut only what you'll eat that day.
  • Don't count on regrowth. Mississippi State calls microgreen harvest a one-cut process. Pea shoots can be recut once, and scallions several times at about 2-week intervals, but for most crops the spent mix goes to the compost pile and you sow a new tray. Sowing a small tray every week gives a steady supply.

Are they really more nutritious?

Mississippi State Extension cites a 2012 study (Xiao and colleagues, Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry) that measured vitamin and carotenoid levels in microgreens. Compared with mature leaves, red cabbage microgreens had about 6 times more vitamin C and 40 times more vitamin E, and cilantro microgreens had about 3 times more beta-carotene. Nutrient levels vary a lot by crop, but even a small handful adds real color, flavor and freshness to winter meals.

The short version

Fill a shallow tray with an inch or two of moist potting mix, sow untreated seed thickly (soak peas and sunflowers first), cover for a few days at 65 to 75°F, then give at least 4 hours of light a day and water from below. Cut with scissors at 2 to 3 inches tall, usually 7 to 21 days after sowing, wash, and eat within a week.
