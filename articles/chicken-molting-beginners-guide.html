Chicken Molting: A Beginner's Guide to Why Hens Lose Feathers, How Long It Lasts, Feeding Protein & When Eggs Come Back

Every fall, backyard keepers find drifts of feathers in the coop, a few bald-necked hens and an empty nest box, and wonder what went wrong. Usually nothing did: the flock is molting. Here's what poultry specialists at Kansas State, Mississippi State, Utah State, Oregon State, Kentucky, Alabama and Texas A&M's veterinary college say about why hens molt, how long it takes, what to feed them, and how to tell a molt from a problem.

What molting is, and when it happens

Molting is the yearly swap of old, worn feathers for new ones. Texas A&M's College of Veterinary Medicine describes it as the process "through which hens cease to lay eggs and, instead, divert their energy toward replacing their feathers." Growing a full coat of feathers takes a lot of protein and energy, so most hens stop laying until it's done (Mississippi State, Kansas State).

  • Trigger: shorter days. Kansas State says birds usually molt as day length decreases, and Texas A&M says hens respond to day length "more so than changes in temperature." That's why most molts start in late summer or fall and can run into winter.
  • First molt: Texas A&M puts a hen's first full molt at around 18 months of age, then once a year after that. (Chicks go through quick feather changes as they grow, but that's not the adult molt.)
  • Off-season molts: Oregon State notes a molt can happen at any time of year, especially after stress.

What it looks like: the order feathers fall

A molt follows a pattern. Kansas State, Mississippi State and Utah State all describe the same order: head and neck first, then the breast and body, then the wings, and the tail last. You'll usually notice a scruffy neck and a trail of small feathers in the coop before anything dramatic.

In the wings, the 10 large, stiff primary flight feathers on each side drop in sequence. Utah State says they go from the innermost (#1) to the outermost (#10), and Mississippi State adds that primaries are lost before secondaries. New feathers grow in as pin feathers: tight, quill-like shafts still wrapped in a sheath, which open as the feather grows.

Handle molting hens gently. Texas A&M warns that the new pin feathers "are very fragile and can be easily damaged," and advises owners to limit handling and keep stress low during the molt. Pin feathers have a blood supply while they grow, so a molting hen may be touchy about being picked up.

How long a molt lasts

Molt length varies a lot by breed, age and how hard the hen has been laying. The sources give these ranges:

SourceHow long it takes
Texas A&M (veterinary college)Most molts about 8 weeks from feather loss to replacement; range 1–3 months
Kansas StateSome breeds over 1–2 months; older or fancy breeds 3–5 months
University of KentuckyCommercial laying strains 4–6 weeks; heritage types longer, sometimes 3–4 months
Mississippi StateLate molters 2–3 months; early molters as long as 4–6 months

Oregon State adds that hens lay best until about 2 to 3 years old, and that molts become more frequent and longer as hens age. A hen who sailed through her first molt may take noticeably longer at four.

Early molters vs. late molters

Mississippi State's extension poultry page explains an old culling trick: how and when a hen molts says something about how well she lays.

  • Late molters lay for 12 to 14 months before molting, are "generally the better laying hens," and look ragged and tattered while laying. When they finally molt, they drop primary feathers in groups of two or more, regrow them together, and finish faster, so they get back to full production sooner.
  • Early molters may start molting after only a few months of laying, look smoother and better groomed, are "generally poorer layers," and drop primaries one at a time, which can stretch the molt to 4–6 months.

Utah State notes that strong layers keep laying at the expense of their feathers, so a very scruffy hen in late fall is often your best producer, not a sick one. Some high-producing hens even lay and molt at the same time, but only if they can hold their body weight, and their molt takes longer.

Eggs during and after the molt

Expect egg production to drop off or stop while hens rebuild feathers (Mississippi State, Kansas State). The good news, from Kansas State: after a molt, hens often lay more eggs over a given period, with better shell and interior quality than before. If the whole flock molts at once, a quiet nest box for a month or two is normal. Our winter laying guide covers what else slows eggs in the cold months.

Feeding hens through a molt

Feathers are built from protein, so feed matters more during a molt than at any other time of year. Here's what the sources say, including where they differ:

  • More protein: Texas A&M says molting hens should get a diet high in protein and that "a 20% protein diet is usually adequate." For comparison, Alabama Extension lists standard layer rations at about 15–19% protein for Leghorn-type layers and 14–16% for dual-purpose layers, so a 20% feed is a step up.
  • Calcium: Texas A&M notes that calcium is the focus for laying hens, not molting ones. Alabama Extension takes a different route for flocks with no supplemental lighting: switch from layer feed to a developer ration during the molt and other non-laying periods, and offer a calcium supplement and grit on the side. Either way, offering oyster shell in a separate feeder lets each hen take what she needs (Oregon State, Alabama Extension).
  • Go easy on scratch and treats. Alabama Extension says most scratch grains average about 10% protein, so they dilute the feed: 5 parts of a 20% feed mixed with 1 part scratch works out to about 18.3% protein. Oregon State's rule for treats is to give only what hens clean up in 15 minutes, or none at all.
  • Don't switch to chick feed. Oregon State says not to feed starter or grower diets to layers. Kansas State adds that flocks on a balanced commercial ration rarely have feathering problems caused by feed.

Is it molting, or something else?

Not all feather loss is a molt. These clues from Kansas State, Oregon State and Kentucky help tell them apart:

What you seeLikely cause
Feathers lost in a pattern (head, neck, body), new pin feathers coming in, in fall, with egg numbers tapering offNormal molt (Kansas State)
Bare spots or broken, frayed feathers, sometimes just quills leftFeather pecking; Oregon State says bare spots "are almost always the result of picking." Kansas State links pecking to boredom, overcrowding and very bright light.
Bare backs and the backs of heads on hens onlyMating damage from a heavy rooster or too many roosters (Kansas State); the feathers regrow
Damaged feathers with mites around the vent or under the leg scalesMites; treat the whole flock and the coop (Kansas State)
The whole flock losing feathers fast, out of seasonA serious stress such as running out of water or feed, or a lighting problem (Oregon State); Kentucky adds dehydration and extreme heat

For pecking, Kansas State suggests reducing crowding, giving hens something else to peck at, and using a lower-wattage bulb or a dimmer if coop lights are bright. Our coop size calculator is a quick check on whether crowding could be the problem.

Lights, and why not to force a molt

Because day length drives the molt, supplemental light can delay it. Kansas State says that holding total light at about 16 hours (daylight plus a bulb on a timer) keeps most flocks from molting, while letting the hours drop will usually start one. Oregon State keeps laying flocks at 14 to 16 hours, with a light just bright enough to read a newspaper by, on a timer (it warns even a one-day lapse can hurt production). But Oregon State also recommends letting hens molt in their second winter, by turning the extra light off for about six weeks, so they molt more completely and come back in better feather.

Commercial egg farms sometimes induce a molt on purpose by cutting light and feed. Kansas State says this "can be difficult" and that flock owners should talk to an extension specialist before trying it. For a backyard flock there's no need: let the natural fall molt run its course, keep feed and water in front of the hens, and keep stress low.

The short version

Molting is normal: shorter days in fall trigger hens to drop old feathers in order (head, neck, body, wings, tail) and grow new ones, usually over about 8 weeks but anywhere from 1 to several months. Egg laying pauses, then often comes back stronger. Feed a higher-protein ration (about 20%), cut back scratch and treats, keep oyster shell available, and handle hens as little as possible while pin feathers grow. Bare patches, broken feathers or sudden flock-wide loss point to pecking, mites or stress instead, so check for those.
