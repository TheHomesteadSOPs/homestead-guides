Making Beeswax Candles at Home: A Beginner's Guide to Dipping, Pouring & Wick Sizing

Once a batch of cappings or old comb has been rendered into clean wax, the obvious next step is candles. The Mid-Atlantic Apiculture Research & Extension Consortium (MAAREC) and longtime Bee Culture columnist Dr. James E. Tew both describe the same two home methods — hand-dipping and pouring into a mold — along with the wick, temperature and safety details that make the difference between a candle that burns cleanly and one that smokes, drips or cracks.

Melting the wax

Beeswax is flammable. MAAREC warns that if beeswax boils over or flows onto a burner, there is danger of a violent fire — keep the heat low and never leave melting wax unattended. Bee Culture repeats the same caution: beeswax "is highly flammable and can result in a quick, hot fire." Melt it in a water bath, not a pot sitting directly on a burner, and keep a lid nearby to smother any flare-up. Never pour water on burning wax.

Our rendering guide covers draining, rinsing and filtering cappings before this step. Wax for candles should be clean and free of dirt — MAAREC notes this is a key requirement for show-quality candles, and darker, propolis-stained wax is better saved for other projects than for display candles.

Picking a wick

Neither source gives an exact wick-size chart for beginners, so plan to buy wick sized to the candle you're making from a candle-supply or craft store rather than guessing. Bee Culture's Tew explains why size matters: too large a wick burns the candle's sides out from excess heat, while too small a wick leaves a hole burning down the center until the flame goes out. "Specify the diameter candle to be made when you purchase wicking," he advises — all modern wicking is braided, and a well-sized braided wick curls to one side as it burns and needs little trimming. MAAREC agrees that wick suitable for the candle's diameter should be in place before you start, and most molds come with their own wick-installation directions.

Hand-dipped candles

MAAREC's step-by-step method:

  1. Draw the wick taut in a frame sized to fit your wax container. Use a large container and make several candles at once.
  2. On the very first dip, leave the wick in the wax for about a minute so it soaks in fully.
  3. Dip on the frame and withdraw in one smooth motion. Keep the wax liquid but not excessively hot.
  4. Hang the dipped candles for 3 to 5 minutes between dips so each layer solidifies before the next.
  5. After 5 to 7 dips, the candles are roughly the diameter of a pencil and can be dipped individually from there. Keep going until they're about ¾ to ⅞ inch wide.
  6. Before the final dip, clip the excess wax from the bottom of the wick — this can be up to about an inch.
  7. Leave the single drip that forms at the bottom as the mark of a hand-dipped candle.

Bee Culture's simpler description of the same idea: attach a weight to the end of the wick, dip to the depth you want, pull out and wait a few seconds for the wax to solidify, then "dip and wait, dip and wait" until the candle reaches the size you're after — as Tew puts it, this method won't guarantee a perfect candle, but it will reliably produce a functional one. MAAREC adds a practical tip for keeping your best wax looking its best: use a second pot to replenish wax as you go, saving darker wax for the first few dips (which end up in the candle's core) and your cleanest, lightest wax for the last three to five dips, which form the outer, visible layer. A finished taper made this way weighs around 5 ounces.

Poured candles

Pouring into a mold is more hands-off and gives a smoother finish, at the cost of a little character compared with dipping.

  • Prep the mold. Thread the wick through first. MAAREC says to grease metal molds with silicone spray or vegetable oil (silicone molds need no spray), and Bee Culture notes craft stores also sell a dedicated candle-release compound.
  • Seal the wick holes. Seal the top wick hole with wax or clay, or set the mold on a sponge dipped in cold water, and work on a surface you don't mind getting a little wax on.
  • Pour in an even, steady manner — MAAREC's own phrasing — rather than all at once.
  • Top it off as it shrinks. Beeswax contracts noticeably as it cools. About 2 to 5 minutes after the first pour, add a little more wax to fill the dip that forms at the top.
  • Trim the rim while it's still soft. Remove excess wax from the mold's rim reservoir soon after it hardens, while it's still warm, rather than waiting until it's fully set.
  • Let it finish hardening, usually overnight, before unmolding. On molds that don't come apart, cut the wicking first; a few minutes in the freezer, or running warm (not hot) water over the outside, can help release a stubborn candle. MAAREC mentions lye as a last resort for stuck wax, but calls it "extremely caustic and dangerous" — not a beginner move.

Traditional taper molds run a single wick through two cavities, producing a pair of candles joined at the wick — the same is true of some poured candles made in a shared tin, which Bee Culture notes must be cut apart afterward to make two separate candles.

Rolled candles, the no-heat option

If you'd rather skip melting altogether, MAAREC describes simply rolling a sheet of beeswax foundation (the same sheets used in frames, sized about 8½ by 16¾ inches) around a wick by hand. Rolled candles need no molten wax, but they also burn noticeably faster than a dipped or poured candle of the same size.

Shrinkage, cracking and cleanup

  • Bigger mold, more shrinkage. MAAREC notes larger candles shrink more as they cool, which is part of why topping off a poured candle matters.
  • Cool slowly to avoid cracks. Let candles cool in a warm room rather than somewhere cold or drafty; a heavily greased mold, or soapy water on the mold's sides, also helps release without sticking. For very even, slow cooling, MAAREC suggests dry heat such as a turned-off oven, or a water bath kept around 160°F.
  • The "bloom" is harmless. Bee Culture notes that natural beeswax candles can develop a crystalline white film called bloom over time. It isn't a flaw or a sign of a problem — a brisk wipe with a cloth polishes it right off.

The short version

Melt clean, light-colored wax slowly in a water bath (roughly 143–147°F, depending on the source) in a non-reactive pot, never directly on a burner, and never leave it unattended. Buy wick sized to your candle's diameter from a candle-supply store. For dipped candles, soak the wick a full minute on the first dip, then dip and hang to solidify for 3 to 5 minutes between dips until you reach the width you want, finishing with your cleanest wax. For poured candles, seal the wick hole, pour steadily, top off the shrinkage after a few minutes, and trim the rim while it's still soft. Either way, cool slowly in a warm room to avoid cracking.
