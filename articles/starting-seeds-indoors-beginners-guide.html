Starting Seeds Indoors: A Beginner's Guide to Timing, Grow Lights, Watering & Preventing Damping-Off

Growing your own transplants is cheaper than buying flats, gets you varieties the garden center never stocks, and gives slow crops like onions and peppers the head start they need. It also has a few ways to go wrong: starting too early, too little light, and soggy mix that invites damping-off. Here's what five university extension guides say about each step, from counting back from your frost date to the day the seedlings move outside.

Step 1: Count back from your last frost date

Every indoor start date is counted backward from when the transplant can go outside, and that hinges on your area's average last spring frost (our frost dates guide explains how to find yours). Rutgers puts the most common mistake in capital letters: "DON'T START PLANTS TOO EARLY." An overgrown, root-bound seedling transplants worse than a younger one.

A worked example. Say your average last frost is May 15. Peppers at 8–10 weeks get sown between about March 6 and March 20; tomatoes at 6–8 weeks between about March 20 and April 3. Those counts land on your planting-out date, so warm-season crops that shouldn't go out until after the frost risk has passed can be started a little later. The sources don't agree exactly (Oklahoma State and Utah State both list tomatoes at 5–7 weeks), so treat the range as a window, not a deadline.

Some crops are better sown straight into the garden. Rutgers says carrots, beets and peas "should always be seeded directly in the garden," and that beans and sweet corn transplant poorly. If you do start cucumbers, squash or melons indoors, keep it short: Oklahoma State recommends seeding vine crops directly into the pot they'll grow in (one seed, two at most), and the University of Maryland suggests two or three seeds per cell, keeping the strongest, with no potting up.

Step 2: Containers, mix and a clean start

  • Drainage is non-negotiable. Almost any clean container works if it has drainage holes. UMD says at least 2 inches deep; Utah State says it should hold at least 3 tablespoons of mix.
  • Sanitize reused trays. Rutgers: a 1:10 solution of household bleach and water, soak 10 minutes, dry thoroughly. Utah State: wash in hot soapy water, then soak 10 minutes in 1 part bleach to 9 parts water. UMD gives a 1:10 soak of 1–2 minutes after a disease problem, then a rinse.
  • Use a soilless seed-starting mix, never garden soil. UMD notes garden soil weighs roughly 75 pounds per cubic foot against about 10 for soilless mix, far too dense for tiny roots. Utah State says to use fresh mix every time.

Step 3: Sowing and germination

  1. Plant at the right depth. UMD's rule of thumb is about twice the seed's diameter; Utah State says three to four times its diameter; Colorado State simply says follow the packet. The table above gives Rutgers' depths for common crops. Tiny seeds go barely under the surface.
  2. Moisten, then cover. Mist the mix, then cover the tray with clear plastic until seedlings emerge (Utah State, Colorado State). Rutgers suggests bottom-watering: set the container in a shallow pan until the surface looks moist, then let it drain.
  3. Keep it warm. UMD says the mix should be 65–75°F; Colorado State says most seeds germinate best at 70–75°F, with tomatoes, peppers and eggplant closer to 80°F. A heat mat helps the warm-season crops.
  4. Uncover and give light the moment they sprout. Remove the plastic as seedlings emerge, and Utah State says they need light "as soon as they have emerged."

Step 4: Light, and how close is close enough

Most of these extension guides assume fluorescent or LED shop lights rather than a windowsill. The one thing they all agree on is that the lights hang just a few inches above the leaves and move up as the plants grow. On the exact numbers, they differ:

A cheap outlet timer set to 14 hours sits inside every range above. Utah State specifically says to turn the lights off at night; seedlings do not need them on around the clock.

Step 5: Temperature, water and food after they're up

  • Grow them a bit cooler than you germinated them. UMD: 65–70°F by day and 55–60°F at night. Rutgers: keep the room at 60–70°F. Utah State: nights 5–10 degrees cooler than days. Oklahoma State's table splits it by crop — for example tomatoes 70–80°F day / 60–65°F night, cabbage-family crops and lettuce 60–70 / 50–60, onions 60–70 / 45–55.
  • Water when the top is nearly dry. That's UMD's rule; Utah State says "damp but never water logged." Oklahoma State suggests watering in the morning or early afternoon so plants don't sit wet overnight.
  • Feed lightly, and later than you think. UMD says a commercial potting mix supplies enough nutrients for at least 6 weeks; after that, use a water-soluble fertilizer at the label rate for indoor plants. Colorado State suggests quarter- to half-strength once true leaves form. (Rutgers' weekly 20-20-20 schedule, from 3 weeks after seeding, is paired with its own homemade peat-vermiculite mix, which has less fertilizer built in.)

Step 6: Potting up

Seedlings grown in an open flat need their own pots once the first true leaves appear (the second set, which look like the plant's real leaves). Rutgers, UMD and Oklahoma State all use that cue; Oklahoma State says it usually comes two to three weeks after seeding. Hold seedlings by a leaf (Rutgers) or the seed leaves (Oklahoma State), never by the stem, and Oklahoma State says to set each one slightly deeper than it grew. UMD says to keep the tops 1–2 inches from the lights after potting up.

Damping-off: the seedling killer

Seedlings that suddenly flop over at the soil line have usually been hit by damping-off. UMD names the culprits as the water molds Pythium and Phytophthora and the fungus Rhizoctonia, which thrive when the mix is kept too wet. Colorado State adds high temperatures and poor light. Prevention is mostly housekeeping:

  • Start with a fresh soilless mix (Rutgers says it reduces the risk) and sanitized containers; Oklahoma State calls it "kitchen clean."
  • Don't keep the mix saturated, and avoid cycles of soaking then drying out (UMD).
  • Remove the plastic cover as soon as seedlings emerge, and give strong light.

Step 7: Hardening off

Before seedlings go outside for good, they need a gradual introduction to sun, wind and cooler nights. Rutgers and Oklahoma State say about a week; Utah State and Colorado State say about two weeks. Utah State starts when seedlings are 4–6 inches tall or have 4–6 leaves, beginning with an hour outdoors in the afternoon and building up to a full day and night. Rutgers adds that tomatoes should be hardened by cutting back water rather than exposing them to cold. Our hardening-off guide walks through a full schedule.

The short version

Count back from your last frost date: about 10–12 weeks for onions and leeks, 8–10 for peppers and eggplant, 5–8 for tomatoes, 4–7 for the cabbage family, and only 2–5 for cucumbers, squash and melons (or sow those outdoors). Use clean containers with drainage and a fresh soilless mix, sow at about two to three times the seed's width, keep the mix around 65–80°F until sprouts appear, then uncover and hang lights 2–6 inches above the seedlings for 12–16 hours a day. Grow them cooler, water when the surface is nearly dry, feed lightly, pot up at the first true leaves, and harden off for one to two weeks before planting out.
