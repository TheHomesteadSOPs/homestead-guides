How Long Leftovers Last in the Fridge: A Beginner's Guide to Refrigerator Storage Times, Cooling & Reheating

After a big holiday meal or a day of cooking ahead, the fridge fills up fast with turkey, gravy, stuffing and soup. Here's how many days each kind of food stays safe, why you can't trust the sniff test, how to cool a big pot of food quickly, where things belong on each shelf, and how to reheat leftovers properly, from extension guides at Minnesota, Clemson, Illinois, NDSU, Rutgers, Nebraska and Delaware.

The short rule: 3 to 4 days

Most cooked leftovers (meat, poultry, casseroles, soups and stews) keep 3 to 4 days in the refrigerator (University of Minnesota Extension, University of Illinois Extension, University of Nebraska–Lincoln). Clemson says it's best to use leftovers within 4 days, NDSU says up to 4 days, and Rutgers puts it as never more than 4 days. If you won't eat it by then, freeze it.

Don't trust your nose. The bacteria that make people sick can grow without changing how food looks, smells or tastes (Illinois Extension). Rutgers adds that bacteria can grow in the warm center of a deep container without any warning signs, and that you should never taste a leftover to check it. Go by the calendar, and when in doubt, throw it out (Minnesota, Rutgers).

Check your refrigerator temperature

Keep food at 40°F (4°C) or below (NDSU, Minnesota, Delaware). Minnesota Extension suggests setting the fridge at 36 to 38°F and notes that about one in four home refrigerators runs too warm. Clemson's leftovers fact sheet uses 41°F or colder. The dial alone won't tell you, so use an appliance thermometer (Clemson, NDSU).

Illinois Extension's check: put a thermometer in a glass of water in the middle of the fridge, wait 5 to 8 hours, and adjust the control if it doesn't read 38 to 40°F. Check again after another 5 to 8 hours.

Bacteria multiply fastest in what's called the Danger Zone, 40 to 140°F in NDSU's and Nebraska's guides (Clemson gives 41 to 135°F). The whole point of fast cooling and steady cold is to keep food out of that range.

The 2-hour rule

  • Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours of cooking (every source we used). Rutgers points out that the 2 hours includes the time food sits on the table while people eat.
  • Within 1 hour on a hot day, such as a picnic or summer porch dinner (NDSU, Rutgers).
  • Food left out longer gets thrown away (Nebraska), however good it still looks.

Cooling a big batch fast

A stockpot of chili or a whole turkey carcass holds heat for hours in the middle, even inside a cold fridge. The fix is to make it small and shallow:

  • Use shallow containers. Minnesota says under 2 inches deep; Clemson says no more than 2 inches for soups and chili; Rutgers and Nebraska allow up to 3 inches. Shallower cools faster, so 2 inches is the safer target.
  • Cut big things down. Slice turkey and ham off the bone into smaller portions (Minnesota), and divide large cuts of meat or whole poultry into smaller pieces (NDSU). Take stuffing out of the bird and store it separately (Rutgers).
  • Use an ice bath for soups and stews. Set the pot in a sink of ice water and stir now and then (NDSU, Nebraska).
  • Hot food can go straight in the fridge once it's divided up (NDSU). What Clemson warns against is putting a big, deep pot of hot food in the refrigerator whole.
  • Give it air. Nebraska suggests cooling leftovers on the top shelf, loosely covered, then covering them tightly once cold. Space containers apart (Rutgers) and don't overcrowd the fridge, because cold air has to circulate (Minnesota, Illinois).
  • Label each container with what it is and the date it was cooked (Rutgers, Nebraska), so day 4 isn't a guess.

Notice that gravy, broth and anything swimming in it get the shortest time of any cooked food: 1 to 2 days, half the usual leftover window. Freeze extra gravy the day after the meal instead of keeping it in the fridge all week.

Where things go in the fridge

Illinois Extension lays the refrigerator out from top to bottom so that anything that drips lands on food that will be cooked anyway:

  • Top shelf: ready-to-eat food, dairy and juice.
  • Next shelf down: cooked food you'll reheat, including leftovers.
  • Lower shelves: raw fish and steaks, then raw ground meat, then raw poultry at the very bottom. Raw meat, poultry and seafood go in a sealed package on a plate (Clemson).
  • The door is often warmer, so keep it for condiments (Illinois).
  • Never keep raw vegetables on the same shelf as raw meat (Illinois), and use separate cutting boards for raw meat and ready-to-eat food (Clemson).

Reheating leftovers safely

  • Reheat to 165°F all the way through, checked with a food thermometer (Minnesota, NDSU, Nebraska, Rutgers). Rutgers notes that just warm is not enough.
  • Bring sauces, soups and gravy to a boil (NDSU, Rutgers); Nebraska says a rolling boil.
  • In the microwave, cover and vent the dish, stir or turn it halfway, and let it stand 2 minutes before checking the temperature (Minnesota). Microwaves heat unevenly, so stir or use the turntable (Rutgers).
  • Use microwave-safe dishes. Margarine and cottage cheese tubs aren't heat-stable (Minnesota), and Clemson says not to reheat in foam or restaurant carry-out containers.
  • In the oven, reheat meat and poultry at no lower than 325°F (Nebraska).
  • Not in a slow cooker. Nebraska doesn't recommend slow cookers for reheating, because food spends too long in the Danger Zone on the way up.

Freezing what you can't eat in time

Freezing stops the clock. Frozen leftovers stay safe indefinitely at 0°F, and Nebraska gives 2 to 6 months for the best quality. Clemson's times: casseroles about 2 months, cooked meat, soups and gravy 2 to 3 months, cooked poultry dishes 4 months, and poultry pieces in broth or gravy 6 months. Clemson says egg, chicken and tuna salads don't freeze, and Nebraska adds hard-cooked egg whites, lettuce, cabbage and mayonnaise to the list. You can reheat frozen leftovers without thawing them first (Nebraska). Our freezer storage times guide covers wrapping, freezer burn and thawing in detail.

The short version

Get leftovers into a fridge at 40°F or colder within 2 hours (1 hour on a hot day), in shallow containers about 2 inches deep, with big roasts and birds cut down first. Eat cooked leftovers within 3 to 4 days, gravy and broth within 1 to 2, and raw ground meat or poultry within 1 to 2. Keep raw meat on the bottom shelf, label everything with a date, reheat to 165°F and boil sauces and gravy. Anything you won't finish in time goes in the freezer, and anything you're unsure of goes in the trash, not in your mouth.
