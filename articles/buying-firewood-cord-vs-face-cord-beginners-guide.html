Buying Firewood: A Beginner's Guide to Cords, Face Cords, Seasoned Wood & the Best Species to Burn

Firewood is sold in units most people have never measured: cords, face cords, ricks and "pickup loads." Only one of those has a fixed size. Add in the fact that a cord of dense oak can hold nearly twice the heat of a cord of cottonwood, and two loads at the same price can be very different deals. Here's how university Extension foresters say to compare what you're buying, check that it's actually dry, and avoid bringing tree pests home with it.

A full cord is the only unit with a fixed size

Every Extension source agrees on the definition: a standard cord is a stack 4 feet high, 4 feet deep and 8 feet long, or 128 cubic feet. Oklahoma State University Extension points out that the 128 cubic feet includes bark and the air between pieces. How much actual wood is in it depends on how straight the pieces are and how tightly they're stacked: University of Maryland Extension puts it at roughly 65 to 90 cubic feet (about 80 is typical in Maryland), and South Dakota State University Extension says 70 to 80.

That's why a cord should always be bought stacked and measured. A loose pile thrown off a truck takes up more room than the same wood stacked, so it's very hard to judge by eye.

Face cords, ricks and truckloads

A face cord (also called a rick or a stove cord in some areas) is one row of wood 4 feet high and 8 feet long, but only one piece deep. Its size depends entirely on how long the pieces are cut. Utah State University Extension describes it as 4 by 8 feet by the piece length, or 32 to 48 cubic feet. Oklahoma State calls it about a third of a cord (16-inch pieces); Maryland says a third to a half, with pieces 16 to 24 inches long.

The math is simple, and it's worth doing before you compare prices:

Piece length in a 4 × 8 ft rowVolumeShare of a full cord
12 inches32 cu ft1/4 cord
16 inchesabout 43 cu ft1/3 cord
24 inches64 cu ft1/2 cord

So a "$150 face cord" of 16-inch wood is really about $450 a cord. Divide any price by the share of a cord to get a fair comparison.

Pickup loads are the hardest to judge. Utah State says a full-size pickup holds about half a cord (64 cubic feet) when stacked neatly; Maryland gives a range of a fifth to a half of a cord; South Dakota State says a 6-foot bed holds about a quarter cord; and Oklahoma State notes that a full cord won't fit in a half-ton pickup bed. If a seller quotes by the truckload, ask them to stack it so you can measure it.

Which species give the most heat per cord

Heat comes from wood, and denser wood packs more of it into the same stack. Oklahoma State notes that denser species have higher heat values, and Utah State points out that a cord of Osage-orange holds almost twice the heat of a cord of cottonwood. That's why a cord of oak is worth more than a cord of aspen, even though both take up the same space.

Softwoods still have a job

Lighter woods aren't worthless; they just give less heat per stack. New Mexico State University's guide rates cedar, juniper and piñon as medium heat and "excellent kindling," and Douglas-fir and southern yellow pine as high heat but smoky. Spruce, poplar and similar light woods are rated low heat with sparks. Resin is the reason conifers pop and throw sparks (Utah State), so South Dakota State says a fireplace screen is essential when burning pine. A small stack of softwood is handy for starting fires and for mild spring and fall days; dense hardwood is what carries you through a cold night.

Is it actually seasoned?

"Seasoned" means dry, not old. Freshly cut wood can be up to 50% water (Maryland; Utah State says 50% or more by weight), and that water has to boil off before the wood gives you any heat. New Mexico State says seasoned wood has about 20% more heat value than fresh.

  • The target: below 20% moisture (Maryland; Oklahoma State says ideally 10 to 20%). South Dakota State uses below 28% as its limit.
  • The time: 6 to 9 months after cutting (Oklahoma State) or after splitting, stacking and covering (Maryland). South Dakota State says at least nine months after splitting. Wood dries much faster once it's split.
  • Why it matters: burning wet wood makes more creosote in the chimney (Utah State; Maryland ties creosote to wood above 20% moisture), and creosote is what fuels chimney fires.
  • What to ask: when was it cut, when was it split, and has it been stacked off the ground and covered since? If you own a moisture meter, split a piece and test the fresh inside face, not the weathered outside.

If the wood is green, you can still buy it. Just buy it early, ideally the spring before you need it, and stack it to dry for the coming winter.

Buy local: don't move firewood

Firewood is one of the main ways tree-killing pests spread. Oklahoma State lists emerald ash borer, gypsy moth (now called spongy moth) and oak wilt as things firewood can carry, and says local sourcing is best. South Dakota State says out-of-state firewood is the most likely way emerald ash borer will arrive. University of Maryland Extension's rule is never to move firewood more than 50 miles, and notes that even seasoned or debarked wood may still carry pests such as emerald ash borer, Asian longhorned beetle and Sirex woodwasp. Many states and counties have firewood quarantines; check yours before you haul wood home from a cabin or campsite.

Burn only wood

Oklahoma State advises against burning anything other than wood in a wood-burning appliance, and Maryland says artificial fire logs shouldn't go in wood stoves. Follow your stove's manual for what it's rated to burn.

The short version

A full cord is 4 × 4 × 8 feet (128 cubic feet), stacked. A face cord is one 4 × 8 foot row, and its size depends on piece length (16-inch pieces make about a third of a cord). Convert every price to price per full cord before comparing. Dense hardwoods like oak, locust, hickory and ash give the most heat per cord; light softwoods make good kindling. Buy wood below 20% moisture, or buy green wood early and season it 6 to 9 months, split and covered. And buy it close to home.
