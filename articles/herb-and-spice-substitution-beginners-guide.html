Substituting Dried Herbs for Fresh (and Back Again): A Beginner's Guide to Ratios, Timing & Storage

"Just use less if it's dried" is true, but vague enough to ruin a dish either direction. There's an actual ratio behind it, a reason the swap only reliably works one way, and a handful of exceptions (garlic, onion, ginger) that don't follow the herb rule at all. Here's the real math, sourced from university extension home-food programs.

Growing your own supply so you're not guessing at grocery-store dried herbs? Our starting an herb garden guide covers what to plant and how to harvest it.

The ratio that actually matters

Pennsylvania State University (Penn State) Extension gives the conversion in full: 1 tablespoon fresh herbs = 1 teaspoon dried herbs = ¼ teaspoon powdered herb. The reason is concentration, not magic — drying removes water and leaves the flavor compounds behind, so dried herbs run 3 to 4 times stronger than fresh by volume. Oklahoma State University (OSU) Extension's herbs and spices fact sheet states the same relationship as a general guideline: roughly three times as much fresh herb as dried.

It reliably works in only one direction

OSU Extension is specific on this point: substituting fresh herbs for dried in a recipe written for dried generally works fine, but going the other way — subbing dried into a recipe written for fresh — is less reliable. Fresh herbs carry water and a brighter, more volatile flavor that dried versions simply don't fully replicate, so a dried substitute can land close on pure "herb flavor" while still changing the dish's texture and brightness. When a recipe leans on a specific fresh herb for its finishing character (think fresh basil on a caprese salad, not basil cooked into a sauce), that's the case where no amount of ratio math fully closes the gap.

When to add each one during cooking

Timing matters as much as quantity. Per OSU Extension:

  • Delicate fresh herbs — basil, chives, cilantro, dill leaves, parsley, marjoram, mint — go in during the last one to two minutes of cooking, or get sprinkled on right before serving. Cook them longer and the flavor cooks off.
  • Sturdier fresh herbs — dill seed, oregano, rosemary, tarragon, thyme — can go in up to 20 minutes before the dish finishes without losing much.
  • Breads, batters, and similar baked goods need their herbs mixed in at the start, fresh or dried.
  • Cold dishes with fresh herbs (dressings, dips, salads) benefit from at least a couple of hours in the fridge first, so the flavors have time to blend into the rest of the dish.

Dried herbs and ground spices, by contrast, are generally added earlier in the cooking process than their fresh counterparts — they need the heat and moisture of cooking to rehydrate and release their flavor, which is part of why they hold up fine in a long-simmered soup or stew where a delicate fresh herb would just disappear.

Garlic, onion & ginger: the ratios that don't follow the herb rule

These three get substituted constantly in home kitchens, and none of them use the 3:1 (or 4:1) herb ratio — their dried/powdered forms are far more concentrated. Iowa State University Extension's Answerline program gives the actual numbers:

Fresh formDried/powdered equivalent
1 clove fresh garlic⅛ tsp garlic powder
1 Tbsp fresh ginger⅛ tsp ground ginger
1 tsp onion powder1 Tbsp dried onion flakes (not fresh onion)

And for the salted versions specifically: 1 teaspoon garlic salt is roughly ¼ teaspoon garlic powder plus ¾ teaspoon salt; 1 teaspoon onion salt is roughly ½ teaspoon onion powder plus ½ teaspoon salt — useful if a recipe calls for the salted version and your pantry only has the plain one, or vice versa.

Short on a spice blend? A few tested stand-ins

Also from Iowa State Extension's Answerline, for the blends that run out at the worst moment:

  • Allspice (1 tsp): ½ tsp cinnamon + ½ tsp ground cloves + a pinch of nutmeg.
  • Apple pie spice (1 Tbsp): 2 tsp cinnamon + 1 tsp nutmeg + a pinch of ground allspice.
  • Paprika (1 Tbsp): 1 Tbsp ancho chile powder.
  • Za'atar (1 Tbsp): 2 tsp dried thyme + ¾ tsp sumac (optional) + ½ tsp toasted sesame seeds + ⅛ tsp salt, ground coarsely together.

Drying your own, so the ratio stays consistent

Penn State Extension recommends harvesting herbs just before they flower, since flowering turns the leaves bitter, and picking mid-morning once the dew has evaporated. Rinse in cool water, shake off the excess, and only dry leaves and stems that are clean and unbruised. For the actual drying, Penn State lists four home methods — air-drying, oven drying, microwave drying, and a dehydrator — and specifically advises against sun-drying in a humid climate, where slow moisture loss can dull both color and flavor before the herb finishes drying.

Storing dried herbs and spices so the ratio keeps working

A ratio is only as good as the dried herb's actual potency, and that fades with bad storage. OSU Extension's storage rules: keep dried herbs and spices in tightly covered, airtight containers, in a dark cupboard or drawer, away from heat, light, and moisture — not above or near the stove, dishwasher, microwave, refrigerator, sink, or a heating vent. Measure with a dry spoon, and never shake seasoning directly from the container into a steaming pot, since the rising steam can get inside and clump or spoil what's left. Paprika, chili powder, and red pepper specifically benefit from refrigeration to hold their color, especially through summer heat — just let a cold container warm up gradually before opening it in a humid kitchen, so condensation doesn't form inside.

Shelf life, per OSU Extension: dried herbs and ground spices stay good for about a year; whole spices (peppercorns, whole cloves, cinnamon sticks) hold their potency for about two years. Past that window, a dried herb hasn't gone unsafe, it's just gone weak — which is exactly when the 3:1 ratio above stops being accurate and you'll need more of it, or a fresh jar, to get the flavor a recipe expects.

The short version

1 Tbsp fresh herb = 1 tsp dried = ¼ tsp powdered — and that swap works far better going fresh-to-dried than the reverse. Add delicate fresh herbs at the very end, dried ones earlier. Garlic, onion, and ginger use their own, much more concentrated ratios. And store whatever you dry airtight, dark, and away from heat, because a year-old jar of dried oregano isn't dangerous, just quietly weaker than the ratio assumes.
