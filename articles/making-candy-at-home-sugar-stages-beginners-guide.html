Candy Making at Home: A Beginner's Guide to Sugar Stages, the Cold-Water Test & Altitude Adjustments

Candy is really just sugar syrup cooked to a specific temperature, and getting that temperature right is most of what separates soft fudge from chewy caramel from brittle toffee. Colorado State University Extension and the Exploratorium's Science of Cooking project both map out the same stages cooks have used for generations, with ranges that shift once you're above sea level. Here's how to read those stages, back up a thermometer with the cold-water test, and adjust for your own elevation.

The sugar stages, from thread to hard-crack

As a sugar syrup boils, water cooks off and the sugar concentration climbs, which raises the syrup's boiling point. The higher the temperature the syrup reaches, the harder and more brittle it will set once it cools — that's the whole logic behind every candy recipe's target temperature.

The sources don't agree down to the degree. Colorado State University Extension's chart and the Exploratorium's chart overlap but don't line up exactly — CSU puts soft ball at 234–240°F and hard crack at 300–308°F, while the Exploratorium gives 235–240°F and 300–310°F for the same two stages. The ranges above blend both. Either end of a range is a reasonable stopping point; follow the exact number your own recipe gives first.

The cold-water test

A thermometer is the more precise tool, but the cold-water test is the backup cooks used long before thermometers existed, and it still works as a double-check. Drop a small spoonful of the hot syrup into a cup of cold (not icy) water, let it sit for a few seconds, then gather it with your fingers and see how it behaves — a thread, a soft ball, a firm ball, a hard ball, pliable threads, or brittle threads that snap, matching the stages above. The Exploratorium notes that the highest temperature the syrup reaches is what determines how it sets, so one quick dip tells you where you are in the process.

Let it cool before you touch it. Hot sugar syrup can cause a serious burn. The Exploratorium's own caution for the hard-crack stage applies at every stage: let the syrup sit in the cold water for a few moments before picking it up.

Keeping your thermometer honest

  • Pick the right tool. CSU Extension notes that candy thermometers typically measure from 100–400°F, should clip to the side of the pan so the bulb doesn't touch the bottom, and should be easy to read at a glance.
  • Calibrate it in plain water first. Both CSU Extension and the Exploratorium recommend testing a thermometer in a pot of boiling water before you rely on it for candy: at sea level it should read 212°F. If it's off, adjust your target temperatures by the same amount, or replace the thermometer.
  • Know what's inside it. CSU notes that newer candy thermometers use a non-toxic, blue-colored alcohol, and some digital models have alarms. Older mercury-based thermometers need extra care: if one breaks, the mercury has to be treated and disposed of as hazardous waste, not wiped up and thrown in the trash.

Adjusting for altitude

Water boils at 212°F at sea level, and that boiling point drops by about 1°F for every 500 feet of elevation — roughly 202°F at 5,000 feet. Because syrup boils cooler the higher you go, a sea-level recipe cooked to its listed temperature at altitude will actually be more concentrated (and harder) than the recipe intended, which CSU says can leave candy too hard or grainy if you don't adjust for it.

The rule: for every 1,000 feet above sea level, subtract 2°F from every temperature in the recipe (equivalently, 1°F for every 500 feet). CSU Extension's own worked example: a recipe that finishes at 236°F at sea level should finish at 216°F at 10,000 feet — 20°F lower.

Avoiding grainy or crystallized candy

Grainy candy usually means a stray sugar crystal started a chain reaction in the pot. CSU Extension's steps for keeping that from happening:

  1. If the recipe calls for butter, grease the sides of the pan with it first.
  2. Bring the liquid (water, milk, etc.) to a boil, then remove it from the heat.
  3. Add the sugar, then return the pan to the heat.
  4. Cover the pan briefly so rising steam washes any sugar crystals back down the sides.
  5. Uncover, and don't stir again once it's cooking (stirring can trigger crystallization).
  6. Let the finished candy cool undisturbed, without moving or jostling the pan.
  7. For taffy, caramel and brittle, pour by holding the pan close to the cooling pan or sheet — within about an inch — and don't scrape out the last bit of syrup, which is more likely to have started crystallizing.

The short version

Candy stages run from soft ball (fudge, around 234–240°F) up through hard crack (brittle, around 300–310°F); sources' exact ranges vary slightly, so trust your own recipe's number first. Calibrate your thermometer in plain boiling water (212°F at sea level) before you trust it with sugar, and use the cold-water test as a backup. Above sea level, subtract about 2°F per 1,000 feet of elevation from every temperature in the recipe. Avoid stray crystals by washing down the pan sides with steam, not stirring once it's boiling, and letting the finished candy cool undisturbed.
