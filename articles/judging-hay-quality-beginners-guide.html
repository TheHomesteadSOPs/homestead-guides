Judging Hay Quality: A Beginner's Guide to Leafiness, Cutting Stage, Color, Smell, Mold & Reading a Hay Test

Two stacks of hay can weigh the same and cost the same and still feed your goats, sheep or cow very differently. Here's how to judge a bale by looking, smelling and feeling it, why the cutting date matters more than the color, how to spot mold and hay that's heating, and how to sample hay for a lab test and read the numbers that come back, from Oklahoma State, UNH, Minnesota, Kansas State and Missouri extension guides.

Why quality matters as much as quantity

Our guide to how much hay you need for winter works out the tonnage. This one is about what's inside those bales. Missouri Extension's example shows how big the gap can be: alfalfa-grass hay cut at late bud to early bloom tested about 65% TDN and 18% crude protein, while the same field cut at half-bloom or later dropped to 48 to 50% TDN and 12% protein. Missouri adds that a 10-day delay in cutting can cost about 20% of the TDN and 40% of the protein, and that fescue and orchardgrass can fall to about 6% crude protein once they've bloomed. Animals eating poor hay need more of it, or a supplement, to hold their condition.

Oklahoma State's hay-judging guide says looking at hay and testing it in a lab "complement each other." A lab number tells you protein and energy; your eyes and nose catch mold, dust, weeds and junk that a lab report won't.

1. Cutting stage: the biggest single factor

  • Grass hay: the best is cut before the plants flower. Oklahoma State rates grass with 1% or less of its seed heads showing as good quality, with quality dropping above that. Look for long stems topped with seed heads as the sign of late-cut, mature grass.
  • Alfalfa: bud stage is highest quality, and hay with about 1 in 10 stems blooming (early bloom) is still very good. Quality falls fast as more blossoms open and is low at late bloom (Oklahoma State).
  • Clover: best cut before bloom (Oklahoma State).
  • Later cuttings: second and third cuttings of grass usually have little stem or seed head and generally make very high quality hay (UNH). Orchardgrass matures early in the season, timothy and bromegrass later, so the same first-cutting date catches them at different stages.

Mature plants carry a lower share of nutrients and a higher share of fiber (UNH), and animals eat less of stemmy, fibrous hay.

2. Leafiness

As a rule of thumb, two-thirds of the protein in hay is in the leaves (Oklahoma State). Open a bale and look for plenty of leaves still attached to the stems. Leaves shatter off when hay is handled below about 15% moisture (Oklahoma State), and legumes like alfalfa lose leaves if they're left in the field until the stems dry (UNH). A pile of fine leaf bits at the bottom of the bale is nutrition your animals will waste.

3. Color: useful, but don't judge on color alone

  • Bright green usually means the hay cured well (UNH, Oklahoma State).
  • Bleached outer layers are mostly sun-fading. Oklahoma State says bleached hay may be as good as green hay, and calls judging hay on color alone a mistake.
  • Rain on the cut hay before baling can wash nutrients out (Oklahoma State) even when it still looks fine.
  • Dark green, brown or black hay was usually baled too wet and heated (Oklahoma State). Brown, bleached-through or musty hay signals low quality (UNH).

4. Smell and feel

  • Good hay smells sweet and fresh, like fresh-cut grass (UNH, Oklahoma State).
  • Musty, moldy, mildewed or rotten smells mean low quality (Oklahoma State).
  • A tobacco-like smell with brown color points to hay baled at roughly 20% moisture or more that heated in the bale (Oklahoma State).
  • Soft beats brittle. Oklahoma State advises choosing soft hay; coarse, brittle hay can cut how much animals eat.

5. Mold, dust and foreign material

Shake a flake apart. White or gray dust puffing out, clumps that stick together, or a musty smell all suggest mold. Animals won't eat moldy hay readily (Oklahoma State), and Minnesota Extension warns that moldy hay can cause colic, heaves and respiratory disease in horses, the most sensitive of the common hay eaters. Mold spores are a risk to people too: Minnesota names farmer's lung, so wear a dust mask when handling dusty hay.

Also look for sticks, trash, tree leaves, weeds and baling wire (UNH). Some weeds are toxic (Oklahoma State, UNH), so if you see plants you don't recognize, ask your extension office before feeding the hay. Single-species hay tends to be more uniform than a mixed, weedy field (Oklahoma State).

How hay judges weigh it. Oklahoma State's hay-judging scorecard gives different weights depending on which animal will eat the hay. Leafiness is worth 25 points for beef cattle but only 15 for horses, while odor (20) and softness (20) count more for horses than for beef cattle (15 each). Mold takes up to 20 points off for every kind of animal. In other words, cattle tolerate a little dust better than horses do, but nobody should get moldy hay.

Safe moisture at baling, and hay that's heating

Most mold and heat problems trace back to hay baled too wet. Ask the seller how dry it was when baled and how long it's been stacked.

Moisture at balingWhat to expect (Minnesota Extension)
Under 10%Brittle and dry; leaves shatter
10–15%Recommended; least fire risk
16–20%Could mold without a preservative; slight fire risk
21–25%Likely to mold without a preservative; moderate fire risk
Over 25%Severe heat damage; high fire risk

Kansas State and Missouri give upper limits by bale type: small square bales under 22% and large round or large square bales under 18%, because big, dense bales hold heat. UNH suggests taking hay off the field at 15 to 18%. Kansas State adds that hay baled at 28% moisture can lose up to a third of its feeding value.

All hay heats a little after baling unless it's under about 15% moisture (Kansas State, Missouri). If you've just stacked fresh hay in a barn, Kansas State recommends checking its temperature twice a day for six weeks:

Temperature inside the stackWhat it means
Under 130°FLittle risk; keep checking (Kansas State, Minnesota)
130–140°FMay go up or down; recheck in a few hours (Kansas State)
About 150°FLikely to keep climbing; move hay to let it cool and check every 2 hours (Kansas State)
175°F or moreFire imminent or present; call the fire department (Kansas State, Minnesota)
Warning signs of a hay fire: a caramel or burning smell, vapor or smoke, a strong musty smell, or hay that's hot to the touch (Kansas State). Don't move hay that shows signs of fire, because opening it lets air in; Minnesota warns that bales above 190°F may burst into flame when moved. Kansas State's probe method uses a long pipe pushed into the stack from boards laid on top, worked by two people with a lifeline.

Getting hay tested

Looks can't tell you crude protein or fiber (Minnesota). A forage test can, and it's the best way to know whether your hay meets your animals' needs. UNH's sampling method:

  • Sample each lot separately. A lot is hay of the same type, from the same field, cut on the same day (Minnesota).
  • Use a core probe, not a handful pulled off the outside; UNH says grab samples aren't acceptable.
  • Take about 20 cores, each from a different bale, and combine them into one sample. For a small lot of large round bales, take 2 or 3 cores per bale.
  • Drill 12 to 24 inches in, straight on. Probe small square bales from the end; probe round bales from the side, angled toward the center.
  • Seal the sample in a plastic bag, keep it cool and dry, and mail it early in the week so it doesn't sit over a weekend.

Your county extension office can usually lend a probe or tell you where to send samples.

Reading the hay test

  • CP (crude protein) is estimated from the nitrogen in the hay (Minnesota, UNH). Higher is better.
  • NDF (neutral detergent fiber) is the total fiber. Higher NDF means animals eat less (Minnesota, UNH).
  • ADF (acid detergent fiber) is the least digestible fiber. Higher ADF means lower digestibility (Minnesota).
  • TDN (total digestible nutrients) is an energy estimate (UNH, Minnesota).
  • RFV and RFQ combine intake and energy into one number for comparing lots, with full-bloom alfalfa set at 100. RFV uses only ADF and NDF and is less reliable for grass hay; RFQ usually works better for grass (Minnesota).

Don't be put off by a hay that lands in the lower grades. These grades were built around dairy-quality alfalfa. UNH notes that an RFV of 85 to 90 is still high-quality hay for animals at maintenance or light production, which describes a lot of backyard goats, sheep and dry cows. Hay under about 8% crude protein, though, generally needs a protein supplement (Susan Kerr of WSU Extension, cited in our hay quantity guide). Match the hay to the animal: a heavily pregnant or milking doe needs better hay than a wether at maintenance.

A quick checklist for buying hay

  • Ask what's in it, which cutting it is, when it was cut, and whether it got rained on.
  • Ask if it's been tested, and for the report if so.
  • Open a bale from the middle of the stack, not the top: look for leaves, few seed heads, and green color inside.
  • Smell it: sweet and fresh, never musty, sour or caramel-like.
  • Shake a flake: no dust cloud, no white or gray mold, no clumps, no sticks, trash or wire.
  • Feel it: soft and pliable, not coarse and brittle.
  • For horses, be strictest about mold and dust.

The short version

Cutting stage matters most: grass cut before it heads out and alfalfa cut at bud to early bloom feed far better than late, stemmy hay. Look for lots of attached leaves, a sweet smell and a soft feel; treat color as a clue, not a verdict. Reject musty, moldy or dusty hay, especially for horses. Hay should be baled at 10 to 15% moisture and no wetter than about 22% for small squares or 18% for big bales, and freshly stacked hay over 150°F needs action. For real numbers, core-sample about 20 bales per lot and send them for a forage test, then compare CP, NDF, ADF and RFV against what your animals need.
